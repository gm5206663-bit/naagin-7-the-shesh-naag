# Chapter 19 — The First Day (Part 2)

*Episode reference: Naagin, season 7, Episode 9.*

*Draft; not yet accepted.*

## Woh Cheez Humari

The passage behind the hall at nine at night, where the caterers' trolleys turn, with two bulbs on the ceiling and one of them flickering at the far end. A steel tray stands on the floor by the wall with the evening's used glasses on it and the cloth over it has been pulled half back. Sheshnaag's hood is not shown, but the passage feels like a temple that has forgotten its god.

Parmeet stands with his hands behind his back. Dimple stands beside him. Pinky stands against the wall with her arms folded.

"Bas ek baar ye ladki humare ghar mein rehne aa jaye." Parmeet says it quietly. "To bhai sahab, naagmani humari."

"Uske paas kya hai?" Dimple's chin comes up.

"Uske paas woh zameen hai jiske neeche woh cheez hai." Parmeet looks at the flickering bulb at the end of the passage. "Kitne ki hai?"

"Chalis lakh, tau ji. Ek bigha nadi ki taraf." Pinky's voice comes off the wall without any waiting in it. "Aur us aadmi ka hisaab alag hai, jo magarmach ko khilata hai. Chhe lakh mahina. Do hafte chahiye toh teen."

"Toh chalo khareed lete hain." Dimple says it as a woman ending a conversation.

"Zameen bikti nahin hai wahan." Parmeet does not turn round. "Aur chalis din mein nikalte hain kagaz. Humare paas chalis din nahin hain, aur us mahine daam bhi badh jate hain, kyunki poora desh wahi aata hai."

"Chalis lakh is ghar ke paas nahin hai, tau ji." Pinky says it the way she reads a bill. "Jo hai woh mere gale mein hai, aur woh bhi mere naam pe nahin. Aur kal subah printer wale ko chaar lakh pandrah hazaar dena hai."

"Phir bhi ek hisaab toh banta hai." Dimple lifts her chin. "Teen crore aadmi aate hain us mahine. Ek din ka bhi hisaab na lagao?"

"Ek din ka hisaab lagta hai." Parmeet's voice goes flat, the way his does when the number is bigger than the room. "Us din wahan ka rate doona hota hai, aur phir bhi koi bechta nahin." Parmeet looks at the door and then at the floor. "Woh file is ghar mein nahin hai, Dimple. Do ghante baith kar zameen ka kaagaz nahin banta."

Parmeet turns his head to the far end of the passage. "Vihaan. Attari wali file. LAAO."

Vihaan steps forward from the pantry shadow. His jaw is tight, his hand steady on the doorframe. He does not smile. "File mere paas hai. Attari wali. Mera printout."

Parmeet looks at him — not welcoming, using. "Tum jaante ho uske paas kya hai? Zameen? Woh cheez neeche zameen mein?"

"Kyunki maine wahi file padhi hai," Vihaan says. "Aur main jaanta hoon ki uske paas woh zameen hai. Woh file meri hai, aur main usey uske saath jaane doonga."

Parmeet's voice goes flat, the way his does when the number is bigger than the room. "Toh lo file le ao. Woh naagmani humein milani hai. Tum karoge."

"Tum kyon mujhse?" Vihaan asks. His breath is steady.

Parmeet does not soften. "Toh karo. Woh naagmani humein chahiye. Tum jao."

Vihaan goes. On his way out he says, "Kyunki main chahata hoon ki woh ghar mein rahe. Ek raat. Do raat. Jitne din lagte hain. Main usey dekhta rahunga."

"Guest room tayyar hai." Pinky says it to the floor, and she does not look at the man in the doorway as she says it. "Lehnga bhi."

"Lehnga kal." Parmeet starts walking. "Pehle woh ghar mein rahe. Ek raat bhi kaafi hai."

Vihaan comes out of the pantry shadow fully now, with the phone in his hand and a man talking into it about a van in Ghaziabad. He stops inside the door because the tray is in the way. He is smiling, small and sharp, at nothing.

"Bulb kaun badalta hai yahan?" he says. "Ye flicker karegi toh raat ko catering wala bill mein daal kar layega. Aur tray utha lo — neeche bachche daud rahe hain."

"Hum baat kar rahe the." Dimple says it to Parmeet's back, not to him.

"Haan." He takes the earbud out of his ear and puts it back in again half a second later, which is the whole of his answer. His hand is steady on the phone. "Ghaziabad wala van kal subah char baje aayega. Driver poochta hai ki ground ka entry kaun dega. Main usse yahi bataunga: mujhse. Aur nadi wale ka bill main bharunga. Aap apna kaam dekhiye."

He says it the way he would say anything about a van, and then he is back on the phone, and the three of them let the second go by without repeating the sentence he walked into. Nobody in that passage asks him to leave.

Parmeet's hand stays on the back of a chair. Dimple looks at the man in the doorway the way a woman looks at a room she has already arranged in her head and cannot re-arrange now. Pinky has moved off the wall and is standing in front of it, which nobody notices.

Two bulbs are on over the trolleys' turning and one of them is dying at the far end. Under the cloth on the steel tray there are the evening's glasses, and on the mela's list the corridor they came through is counted like everything else. The count runs on one sheet of paper, good for fifteen days, and it buys the right to put a generator on that ground and to be standing on it at two in the morning with a digger and six of his friends. The paper is in the carton that left this house in November. It is on the channel's fourth floor now, in a drawer he keeps shut, and his name is printed on it four times.

The flickering bulb at the end of the passage goes out. Nobody in that passage turns round to look at it, and the other bulb goes on holding its one small circle of light on the floor.

A stack of plates stands on the floor by the caterers' trolley with a cloth between them.

The passage's second bulb has a wire to it that goes up over the door and is taped there with tape that has gone hard.

## Wafa

The guest room at ten at night, with the lamp on the dressing table and the rest of the room dark, and the window open on the garden and the tent's canvas moving outside it. The trunk stands at the foot of the bed with its straps cut and one lid up. A tin of something sweet stands open on the bedside table with the paper still on it.

Pinky comes in without knocking and puts the cup down on the dressing table hard enough to slop it.

"Chai."

"Rakh do." Ahana is at the window with her back to the room.

Pinky does not go. She stands by the bed with her hands on the frame of it and her eyes going over the trunk and the shoes and the watch on the bed.

"Wo aadmi mera tha." Pinky's voice is low. "Aaj poori duniya ke saamne—"

Ahana turns round. In the lamp's light her face is the face in the photograph on Parmeet's phone and nothing else at all.

"Wafa karne waale ke hisse hi dagabazi likhi jaati hai." Ahana says it without heat. "Mujhe bhi mila tha ek aisa veer hai. Par iska matlab ye to nahin ki main dobara pyaar nahin kar sakti."

Pinky's hand comes up and stops and goes back down.

"Tumhe kuch pata nahin hai." Pinky picks the cup up off the dressing table and takes it with her. "Is ghar mein tum kisi ko nahin jaanti."

The door shuts. The canvas outside the window is pulled out and let go by the wind, and the cup's ring on the dressing table is the only thing in the room that moves.

On the other side of the door, in the corridor, Vihaan stands with his hand on the wood. He has not knocked. He has not spoken. He does not know what passes in there, and he will not know it from the corridor.

"Ahana." He says it quietly, to the wood.

Nothing comes back.

The corridor lamp dips once and comes back, and Vihaan's hand stays on the doorframe.

## Bhai Ho Na

The terrace at eleven at night, with the party's noise coming up through the floor and the marigold along the parapet going dark, and one bulb on over the stairhead. A crate of bottles stands by the parapet with the ice gone to water in it.

Pinky comes up the last four steps with her arms folded and stands in front of her brother.

"Dad ne sirf paison ke liye us ladki ko ghar mein entry de di." Her chin is up. "Aur tum kuch nahin kar rahe."

"Pinky, dheere."

"Aur ye baat Vihaan ko pata nahin chalni chahiye." She says the name flatly. "Woh kal se ghar mein hai aur woh sab poochhta hai."

"Main kya karoon?"

"Tu mera bhai hai na." Pinky's hand comes flat on his chest. "Mere bhai ho na, abhi shaadi nahin hone doge."

"Pinky—"

"Main warning de rahi hoon." She takes her hand back. "I am warning you."

Aryaman looks at the parapet and not at her, and the crate beside him has one bottle in it and the bottle is on its side.

"Ja." He says it quietly. "Andar ja. Aur kisi ko kuch mat bata."

Pinky goes down the stairs. Aryaman stays where he is for a while with his phone in his hand and his thumb over the screen, and then he puts the phone in his pocket and looks at the city instead.

Vihaan is not on the terrace. He is in the hall below, standing at the back between the door and the wall, counting the chairs again.

"Shyam." He says it to the empty hall.

No one answers.

The hall is empty now, the guests gone, the marigold brown at the edges, and his hands are at his sides, and he is not thinking about Pinky or Aryaman or the sagai. He is thinking about the woman in the red dress who walked into his father's house and says she does not think so, and about the way the lamp dipped when she crossed the threshold, and about the phone in Parmeet's pocket that rang three times and no one answers.

## Chhupana Mushkil Ho Raha Hai

The guest room at the end of the upper corridor at midnight, with the lamp off and the light of the garden's tent coming in through the window and lying on the floor in strips. The trunk stands at the foot of the bed with one strap done and one still loose, and the bed has not been turned down, and the room has the smell of a room that has had its door shut for a year.

A glass of water stands on the dressing table where the cup stood, and the ring of the cup is still on the wood beside it.

In the lid pocket of the trunk there are two file folders folded the wrong way round, which is how a man folds them at an allotment counter when he has been standing in a queue. They have her father's name printed on them and a number of a plot, and the number is small, and one of them has a red stamp on the corner that says the paper is three months from being renewed. She has carried both of them in the train's overhead rack for eleven hours and has not opened either since the hall.

She has both hands on the frame of the mirror and she is looking at the face she came in with.

"To kaisa lag raha hai?" She asks it in her sister's cadence, the chin up, a pause left in it before the last word, the laugh taken out. "Finally aa hi gayi is ghar mein. Chhupana mushkil ho raha hai na tere liye?"

"Haan," she answers in her own voice, and does not change it for the other one. "Aa hi gayi."

The strips of light move on the floor when the canvas outside is let go and comes back.

"Tujhe yahan chahiye kya?" She gives that one the same way. Then she answers it, and she is quicker than the question. "Paisa. Is ghar ka paisa. Aur jo beta mile."

"In logon ne mujhse mera sab kuch cheen liya." Her hands come off the frame. "Mamma, Papa, Rohan, aur aap."

At *aap* she turns round, and there is nobody in the room but her, and the room is the room they gave her, with somebody else's sheets on the bed and somebody else's flowers going over in the vase.

"Vihaan se pyaar Purvi ne kiya tha." The first of them she gives in her sister's voice, with the laugh taken out of it. The second she says in her own voice, to the dark of the room, in the voice she uses downstairs. "Ahana Vihaan se sirf nafrat karti hai."

Then she says it again, and the second time is louder than a guest room at midnight carries to anybody, and it is thrown at a door that is shut. "Sirf nafrat."

Nobody in the room answers her.

She sits down on the edge of the bed to take her shoes off, and the door is what she sees, because she has left it unlocked and it shuts without a sound.

"Kaun hai?" she says to the door.

No answer.

"Main jaanti hoon aap yahan hain." She stands up. "Main sab jaanti hoon."

A room of this house at a wedding has a key in its lock and the house keeps the keys. There is no key here, and there is a new strike plate on the frame, and the brass on it has not gone soft yet. Somebody in this house has paid a man to change a lock in a guest corridor at eight o'clock, on a night when every man in it is standing in front of a tent, and that man has been paid more than the plate costs.

The window is open an inch, which she has not done, and the latch has the dust of a year on it with a clean line through where two fingers moved it. Outside, on the wall, the corridor's own bulb has been changed and is a size brighter than it needs to be, and it will be burning at four in the morning for a woman who cannot sleep, and nobody in this house gets up at four for anybody.

She heard the number said in the hall, with the whole room there to hear it said, and the number in her head is seven. The corridor she walked has eight painted on the inside of her door, high up, where only a man who has just moved something looks.

The register comes up to her at ten minutes past ten on a boy with a jug, because a guest of this house signs for her room, and he stands there with the pen in his hand and does not offer it. She reads the line they have made for her — Ahana Dhawan, and in the column for the town, where a house like this writes a caste and a district, somebody has put a small question mark instead. The name above it has been written twice, the first one crossed through in a line drawn once and hard, and she puts her finger on it without meaning to.

"Yeh naam kis ka tha?" The boy does not look at the page. "Pata nahin, madam. Unhone kaha tha ki main poochhun nahin."

"Mera naam Ahana Dhawan hai." She says it to the boy, and to the page. "Yeh register mein likho."

"Ji madam." The boy takes the pen back. "Yeh wahan pehle se likha tha."

Somebody has taken a name off this page to put her on it, and the boy has been told not to say whose.

She signs it. Then she stands with her hand on the window's glass and looks down at the drive and the last of the cars and the wet place on the lawn where the sprinkler has been going since before dark, and she does not move for as long as the glass holds her.

She has said two sentences aloud in a room with nobody in it, and there is a pen in the corridor going down the stairs.

The only person in this house who would pay a man at eight at night to leave a door unlocked so that a stranger would never have to ask anybody for anything is the one man she came here without having worked out how to sit across from at a breakfast table.

Outside, boots go along the passage and stop at the stair. They do not come on and they do not go back, and she stands with her head turned toward the door and waits for whichever of the two it is going to be. The tent's band takes its last song down and stops with them, and the waiting is the end of it, and she is still sitting there with her hands flat on the mattress and one shoe off.

On the other side of the door the corridor lamp dips once and comes back, and there is nothing in the corridor. Then the lamp in the room is turned up, and the face in the glass is the face the gate let in, and the clock on the bedside table goes on ticking, and the strips of light go out and come back when the canvas is let go, once.

## Mudde Ki Baat

The small room at the back of the Suri house at two in the night, with the door shut and one bulb on over the table and the rest of the room in the dark, and the table cleared except for a register and a glass. The window has a grille on it and somebody's dupatta hangs over the chair back.

Parmeet sits at the head of the table with his hands folded. Dimple sits at his right. Aryaman stands.

"Mudde ki baat ye hai ki Ahana ko koi problem nahin honi chahiye." Parmeet's finger comes down on the register. "Humein woh naagmani chahiye, aur us tak pahunchne ka raasta woh ladki hai."

"Aur agar woh na maane?" Dimple says it without lifting her eyes from the register.

"Woh maanegi." Parmeet sits back. "Uska zameen ka kaam hai. Uske paas paper hain, humare paas paisa hai. Aur agar phir bhi—"

He does not finish it. The glass on the table has a fly in it and nobody has emptied it.

"Aryaman." Parmeet looks up at his son. "Tu uske saath rehna. Ghar mein, mehmaan ki tarah. Aur jo woh poochhe, sab bata dena."

"Main usse jhooth nahin bolunga."

"Jhooth kaun bol raha hai?" Parmeet's hands come open. "Hum sirf uska swagat kar rahe hain."

There is a knock at the panel, and Pinky comes in sideways with her dupatta over one shoulder and her phone in her hand, and she does not wait for the room to ask.

"Woh upar kya kar rahi hai, akele andhere mein?" Pinky says. "Main darwaze se guzri toh bol rahi thi kuch. Kuch nahin, bas do baar ek hi baat."

"Kaunsi baat?" Dimple's eyes come up off the register for the first time.

"Paise wali baat." Pinky says it like a woman reading a report. "Ki use is ghar ka paisa chahiye, aur is ghar ka beta — jo mile. Ki is ghar se nafhat karti hai, yeh do baar boli. Main kya jaanu, bhabhi ka kaam hai."

She goes, and the door is left open a hand's width, and Dimple looks at her father-in-law with that left at the table between them.

"Toh do baar boli hai." Parmeet puts his finger down on the register and leaves it there. "Ek baar dil se hoti hai, ek baar zubaan se. Jo do baar bolti hai, woh samajh gayi hai ki kaun sun raha hai." He turns the glass on the table round with one finger and lets it go. "Aur jo aurat apni keemat do baar batati hai, usse lena aasaan hota hai. Beta kaun, yeh hum tay karenge."

Aryaman goes out of that room without a word, and the door shuts behind him, and the fly goes round the glass on the table and settles again.

Vihaan is not in this room. He is in the hall outside, standing at the door of the small room, his hand on the panel. He has not gone in. He has not said a word.

"Papa." He says it to the wood.

Nothing comes back.

He does not know what passes in there, and he will not know it from the hall. He stands at the door, and the door does not open for him.

## Mamuli Sa Pitt Saanp

The store room at the side of the house at three in the night, with one bulb on over the bench and the yard beyond the door in the dark, and a generator going somewhere behind the wall at its one note. A crate stands open on the bench with the straw pulled back out of it. A basket sits on the floor with its lid beside it.

Parmeet comes in with his sleeves rolled and a pair of tongs in his hand, and he looks into the crate and then he takes the lid off the basket and puts his hand in.

What he lifts out is small. It is grey and it is thin as a finger and it curls once round his wrist and then hangs.

"Kuch din pehle tere hi kul ki ek naagin ko pakda tha maine." Parmeet holds it up to the bulb. "Uski reed ki haddi par ek hi waar kiya tha maine."

The snake's head comes up level with his own and stays there, and Parmeet looks at it with something in his face that the men at the door do not look at for long.

"Tu to ek mamuli sa pitt saanp hai."

He puts it into the basket and the lid goes on and a cloth goes over the lid and a rope round the cloth. Then he takes a sheet of paper off the bench and writes on it with the pen that is chained to the bench, and he folds the paper once and puts it under the rope.

"Ye Maa Sapela ke paas jaayega." He hands the basket to the man at the door. "Uske chele ke baare mein. Aur woh jaanti hai ki mujhe kya chahiye."

"Kya chahiye aapko, tau ji?" the man at the door says.

"Woh jo cheez hai neeche zameen mein." Parmeet folds the paper once. "Wo mili toh sab kuch hamara ho jayega."

The generator behind the wall goes on at its one note. The straw on the bench is still pulled back out of the crate, and the crate is open, and it stays open.

The straw pulled out of the crate lies on the bench in a heap and the tongs are on the bench beside it.

A tin stands at the end of the bench with its lid off and the powder in it half gone.

## Meri Been Hi Nahin

The small room at the back at four in the night, with the bulb on over the table and the case open on the floor, and a been lying across the case with its reed end towards the door. The room smells of the box and of the smoke that never leaves it.

Maa Sapela sits on the floor with her legs folded under her and the basket in front of her, and the cloth and the rope are on the floor beside it. The paper is in her hand and she has read it twice.

"Kya hai ismein?" she says to the basket. "Kya hai jo mere paas aaya hai?"

The basket's lid comes off. The snake in it comes up and hangs in the air and turns its head from one side of the room to the other and does not settle.

Maa Sapela picks the been up. She puts the mouthpiece to her lips and blows three notes, and the three notes go round the room and come back off the wall and stop in the middle of the air.

The snake does not move.

"Ye saanp kaabu mein kyun nahin aa raha?" She lowers the instrument and turns it over in her hands. "Ye to meri been hi nahin hai."

Her man is at the door with his hands folded and he says nothing, because there is nothing in that trade to say. The bulb over the table hums. The case on the floor has a rope mark in its lid where the rope has been for years.

Maa Sapela puts the been back into the case and shuts it and puts her hand flat on the lid.

"Woh jo aaj is ghar mein aayi hai." She looks up at her man. "Uske paas woh cheez hai jo mere paas nahin hai."

"Kya cheez?" her man says.

"Woh cheez jo neeche zameen mein hai." Maa Sapela puts her hand on the case. "Woh cheez jo hum sabko badal degi."

## Pehla Din

The lawn at the side of the Suri house at ten past five in the morning, with the sky going grey over the wall and the tent's canvas down at the west side and the grass wet to the ankle. The chairs are stacked at the edge of the lawn in twos and fours. A bucket stands by the wall with a cloth over the top of it, and the cloth is wet through.

The woman walks the length of that lawn with her hands at her sides and stops where the lawn meets the drive, and the marigold on the gatepost beside her has gone brown at the ends.

The grass under her feet holds the water and lets it go with every step, and the marks of her feet fill behind her as she goes.

At the edge of the lawn the chairs stand in stacks of six, and one stack has gone over in the night and nobody has set it up.

"Mere badre ka yeh pehla din." Her voice is low and it does not shake. "Aur unki khushi ka aakhri."

Behind her the house stands with its lights going out one by one as the cleaning ends, and the tent's poles stand in the grass, and the drive has the last of the night's cars in it.

"Jung ka aagaaz ho chuka hai." She turns her face up to the grey. "Mere Bharat desh ke dushman, mere parivaar ke dushman, aur is kahani ke dushmanon ka ant."

The sprinkler at the far end of the lawn turns and hisses and lays its water down in its long arc, and the woman stands in it and does not move out of the way of it.

The water goes over the path and onto the drive and into the gutter, and the gutter runs all the way to the gate.

A man at the gate has his hand on the boom and he has stopped with it half up and he does not bring it down.

The marigold on the gatepost beside her has a garland come loose and one end of it in the wet grass.

A glass stands on the lawn's edge where somebody set it down in the night, and the rain has filled it.

A tray stands on the step with the last of the wedding's sweet boxes on it and the string still round them.

Then the sky comes up properly over the wall and the first of the house's doors opens, and she is a guest standing on a wet lawn at five in the morning with her hands at her sides.

Vihaan stands in the doorway with his coat on over the shirt he has slept in. His hand is on the doorframe, and his knuckles are white.

"Nashta side hall mein hai," he says. "Aap yahan khadi rahenge toh bheega hi rahega. Andar aaiye, aur woh glass utha ke rakh dijiye, hawa se gir jaayega."

"Nahin." She does not turn. "Yahan theek hai."

"Thode der mein baarish shuru ho jayegi." He looks at the sky. "Andar aaiye."

"Baarish ho toh ho." She lifts her face to the grey. "Main yahan khadi rahengi."

"Yahan se hilegi nahin?" He says it quietly.

"Hilegi nahin."

"Thik hai." He moves back from the doorframe. "Main wahan hoga. Andar."

"Jaldi aana." She does not turn.

"Aana hoga." He holds the doorframe. "Aapke liye."

She does not move at once, and he does not ask her why. He stands in the door long enough for his arm to have tired, and then he moves back and holds it, and the two of them go in past each other with four feet of wet between them, and the door stays open behind until the wind takes it.


### THE CARD

*The season's last chapter has no tomorrow to preview, so the frame at its foot is not a precap. It is the card, run the way it was run on the first night, and it asks the question the season has spent nineteen chapters not answering.*

Black, and the been alone.

The logotype comes up over thundercloud, and the something red is still falling, and it is still too slow to be rain.

The voice asks it once, and the choir does not answer: *"Kaun hai woh."*

One low string is struck after it, and left hanging, and the hang is where the book stops.

A drum, once. Then the next season, and no music under it until it asks.
