# Chapter 12 — The Hymn

*Episode reference: Naagin, season 7, Episode 4, "Naagin Legacy Unleashed" (last part).*

*Draft; not yet accepted.*

### TITLES

The logotype comes up over thundercloud, with something red falling through the frame too slowly to be rain.

A man's voice asks two words in the pitch of a been: *"Kaun hai woh."* A choir of women answers underneath him and leaves the answer unfinished.

The been stops. One low string is struck and left hanging over the black.

A drum, once. Then the story, and no music under it until the story asks.


## The Leftovers

The lawn at twenty minutes to eleven, with the light of the house standing in its windows, and the party over in the way a party is over: the guests are still out there, and the work has already started behind them.

The service gate at the side of the Suri house stands open on the lane, and there is a wet patch of light on the road where the street lamp leans, and the rest of the lane has gone dark and smells of the drain and of somebody's jasmine.

Two steel trays come out of the gate. Vihaan carries them, one across each forearm, and they are heavier than they need to be because the cook has put the good bones in with the rice. His sleeves are turned back twice and his collar is open at the throat and his watch has been pushed up his arm because his wrists are wet. He has been at this since the last guest came up the drive, and before that at the gift book, and before that in the photograph, and there is nothing on his face about any of it. He smiles small and private at the empty trays, hand steady on the steel, the smile of a man doing a thing no one will thank him for.


The man at the end of the lane takes the trays. He is old and thin, in a checked lungi, and there is a birthmark the size of a thumbprint over one eyebrow, and he comes out of the dark to take the food and does not thank anybody and has been doing this for eleven years.

"Sukhriya," Vihaan says, and the old man looks at him for a second, because nobody in that house says it.

The party's music comes round the corner of the house: an old song about a wedding, the singer's voice going thin under the generator. The air has turned cool, and it has turned in the last hour. The street lamp buzzes.

And across the lane, at the edge of a step, something is sitting.

Vihaan's hands stop on the empty trays.

The animal is low and long and sits the way nothing in this lane sits. Its coat is wrong for the season, thick at the neck and matted at the shoulder, and the tail is down and still, and it is looking at the light and not at any of the people. No dog in Delhi sits the way this sits. No dog in Delhi is that size when it is sitting.

At the neighbour's door two of them come out. The elder is Nandini, twenty-one, in a kurti and a towel round her wet hair, and she has a steel plate in her hand with the last of the evening's rotis on it. The younger is her brother Rohan, nineteen, in a college t-shirt with a printed name across it, in his pyjama, and his slippers are inside the house and he does not go back for them.

"Kya hai?" Nandini stops on the step.

"Didi, vo neeche ek dog baitha hai." Rohan is already off the step. "Cute sa. Usko roti aur doodh de do please."

"Tujhe jo karna hai kar."

"Arre ja yaar."

"Ye kya karne hai?" She says it to his back.

"Arre yaar." He takes the plate out of her hand and starts across, and their mother's voice comes from inside, and she does not come to the door.

"Rohan, chappal pehen ke jao!"

Vihaan takes one step into the lane. And then another.

Nothing about the animal has changed. That is the thing he will remember afterwards: nothing about it changes. The ears do not lift and the tail does not move. It sits and it looks at the light, and then its head comes round to the boy with the biscuits, slowly, the way a thing comes round when it has decided.

"Rohan."

Rohan does not hear him. He is crooning at it, holding a biscuit out, and taking one more step.

"Rohan!" Vihaan is already across the middle of the lane, and his voice comes out of him at a size no part of him chose. "Woh kutta nahi hai! Andar jao!"

There is a half second in which the lane is very quiet. The mother's face appears at the door. Nandini stops kicking the bricks. Rohan turns his head, and the biscuit is still in his hand, and the animal comes off the step.

The animal is not fast. It is worse than fast. It goes across the lane low and gets the boy's forearm in its mouth and takes him down onto the step, and the plate goes and the biscuits go, and Rohan's scream is not a boy's scream, it is a sound going through a wall.

Vihaan moves. He does not fumble. He gets a hand on Nandini's shoulder and puts her back through her own door, and he does not have anything in his hands except a steel tray, so he takes the tray.

Vihaan puts it between the animal and the boy and he goes in low with it, the flat edge first, and it clangs and the animal's weight comes into his shoulder and he goes back two steps and stays standing.

"Andar! Andar le jao!"

Nandini has her brother by both arms and is dragging him up the step. Their mother screams from inside the door. The animal lets go and does not run. It stands there and looks at all of them and its shoulder is working under the coat.

Then it turns its head down the lane.

Vihaan looks where it looks.

Purvi and Ananta are coming round the corner of the Suri house, and Purvi has her dupatta in her hand, and Ananta is on her heels with a clutch bag under her arm, and neither of them has seen it yet.

"Ruko!" Vihaan shouts it down the lane at the two of them. "Ruko — peeche!"

"Bhaago, Purvi! Khadi ke paas!" It is Ananta, and she is pointing, and her voice goes up over the lamp.

Purvi looks up. And that is the moment the animal goes.

## Run

The lane at the back of the Suri house at ten to eleven, with the moon down towards the ridge and the lamp swinging on its bracket and the generator going on behind the wall.

Ananta screams before her sister does.

The animal comes down the lane and Purvi gets in front of her, and the two of them turn to run the wrong way, into the dark part, past the lamp, and Ananta's heel goes at the first step and she goes down hard on one knee with her clutch under her.

"Ananta!"

Vihaan runs. There is no plan in it and there is nothing in his hands, and the wind takes the lamp and the shadow moves under it, and he runs at the shadow.

The animal is between Purvi and the road. Purvi does not go back for her sister, and she does not go to the road. She goes deeper into the dark, past the wall, past the rubbish, towards the plot where the buildings stop, and she is shouting as she goes, and the shouting is the thing that works: the animal turns.

"Ise bachane ke liye mujhe ise yahan se door le jaana hoga." She says it to herself, at a run. "Door. Door le jaana hoga."

"Purvi!" Ananta is on the road on one knee with her hand up, and her voice cracks in the middle.

Vihaan gets to her. He goes down on one knee and has her by the arm, thumb steady on her forearm, jaw tight, breath fast and not showing it, and her hand closes round his wrist so hard that it will be bruised tomorrow, and she is looking at him, and there is blood on her knee and dust in her hair and her face is wet and her mouth is open on absolutely nothing.

"Woh uske peeche gayi." She says it to his face. "Woh uske peeche gayi, Vihaan."

"Ghar jao." He gets her up. "Andar jao, darwaza band karo, aur phone—" He hears himself and stops and takes a breath and starts again, low, because his voice is doing something he does not want it to do in front of her. "Andar jao. Main dekhta hoon."

Then he runs into the dark.

The lane has no proper light past the second turn. There is a plot with an empty plinth and a watchman's fire not yet lit, and beyond that the ground falls away to the road that will be the bypass in ten years, and there is scrub and broken brick and the smell of the drain.

Vihaan goes through all of it at a full run and finds: a broken tea stall, two frightened watchmen with a torch, a dog that barks at him properly and hides.

Vihaan does not find her.

Vihaan comes back to the second turn and the street lamp is buzzing again and the road is lit and there is blood on the asphalt in a line of little separate drops, and it is Rohan's, from the boy being carried inside, not hers.

There is a piece of cloth on the road. It is a green dupatta with a gold edge, torn at one end, and it is wet with something, and it is the only thing in that lane that came off her.

Vihaan stands in the middle of the road with it in his hands. He smiles small and broken at it, the smile of a man who has been given the only thing left of someone he was too late to keep whole.


Vihaan is not shaking. His hands are quite steady and the cloth hangs between them and he is not shaking. Nothing in twenty-four years has done this to him, and he has no name for it, and the name he would reach for is not a name for his own sake.

In the Suri house, at the gate, Ananta is standing in the light with her clutch under one arm and one shoe in her hand.

Ananta looks at him for a long time as he comes up the drive, and then she looks at the cloth in his hands, and she says nothing, and neither does he.

Behind them, the animal is somewhere in the dark beyond the plot, and it is not making any sound at all.

## The Jacket

Half past one, and the Suri house has one light on in it.

The generator is off. The lawn is dark and the chairs are still out on it, and the marigold along the veranda has begun to smell of the end of a party. The back hall smells of wet shoes and of the blue disinfectant the sweeper uses at the end of a night.

Vihaan comes in through the service door with his jacket over his arm and his shirt wet through at the shoulder, and there is mud on both his shoes up to the laces.

The study door is open and the light in it is the one light.

Parmeet is at the desk with his glasses off and a glass in front of him that he is not drinking.

"Kahan tha?"

"Lane mein." Vihaan does not stop walking. "Ek ladke ko kutta kaat liya. Nandini ke bhai ko."

"Kutta?"

Vihaan stops at the foot of the stairs.

"Haan." He says it to the newel post. "Kutta."

The clock in the study does its two notes and stops.

Parmeet comes to the doorway with his glass in his hand and stands in it, and he looks at his son from the shoes to the shoulder, and the look takes its time.

"Lane mein khoon tha?"

"Tha."

"Aur tum wahan kya kar rahe the?"

"Khaana de raha tha." Vihaan turns round. "Do thaliyan. Sukhriya kehne ke liye ruk gaya."

Parmeet's mouth does something at one corner of it that is not a smile.

"Jacket de de."

"Nahi."

The word is in the hall before either of them has decided about it.

Parmeet's glass comes down on the desk behind him without his looking away from his son.

"Kya kaha?"

"Maine kaha nahi." Vihaan's voice is the voice he uses in the office. "Jacket meri hai."

Parmeet laughs. It is one note and it does not go anywhere, and it is worse than a shout in that hall.

"Bada aaya." He picks his glass back up. "Theek hai. Rakhiye apni jacket." He drinks. "Karan ki shaadi ki date tay ho gayi hai. Pandrah tareekh. Tum wahan khade rahoge, apne bhai ke saath, aur tumhara chehra dekh kar log kahenge ki is ghar mein sab theek hai." He sets the glass down again. "Aur us ladki ko ghar mein mat laana. Na naukri ke bahane, na rishtedaari ke bahane."

"Kaunsi ladki?"

"Tumhe pata hai kaunsi ladki." Parmeet goes back to his chair. "So jaao. Subah chhe baje gaadi ja rahi hai."

Vihaan goes up the stairs with the jacket over one shoulder.

At the third step he stops, and comes back down two of them, and says, into the study door, at a volume that is almost polite: "Wo ladki abhi bhi wahan hai. Us lane mein. Aur kisi ko uski khabar nahi hai."

"Toh?" Parmeet does not turn round. "Uske ghar waale hain na. Unko fikr karne do."

"Uska naam Purvi hai."

The glass stops halfway to the desk.

"Aap use 'wo ladki' kehte hain." Vihaan's voice is flat and quiet and it does not go up at the end. "Main use naam se bulaunga. Is ghar mein bhi."

Parmeet puts the glass down and looks at his son for a long moment, and then he laughs again, and this time the laugh has something else in it, and it is not pleasant.

"So jaao." He sits down. "Subah chhe baje gaadi ja rahi hai."

Vihaan goes up, and the light in the study stays on under the door, and the stairs take his weight one at a time and stop.

In his room he does not turn on the big light. He puts his jacket on the bed and takes out of it a green dupatta with a gold edge, torn at one end and stiff with something that has dried in the wind on the way home, and he holds it for a moment at arm's length.

There are four more cartons of the same thing downstairs in the dining room, stacked against the wall where the sideboard used to be, and nobody in this house has asked him a single question about them.

It is warm.

Vihaan turns it over once and the sleeve of his shirt comes out of his cuff. The cloth is warm in a cold room, and it stays warm, and the second time he turns it, it is still warm.

The lamp in the passage outside the door dims and comes back up.

Vihaan takes down from the shelf the fat house book with the brown cover and the gold on it and opens it in the middle, and folds the cloth once, and lays it in the fold of the pages, and shuts the book on it.

His hand stays flat on the cover after the book is shut.

The score comes up under it. One string, and out.

Vihaan puts the book back between the two others.

Vihaan puts his shoes by the door and his shirt over the chair and he sits down on the edge of the bed with his elbows on his knees and does not move.

Then he gets up, and puts the shoes back on, and takes the torch out of the drawer, and goes down the back stairs, and out through the service door, and pulls it to behind him.

## Above the Lane

The lane below the Suri house at two o'clock, between the out-house wall and the wall of the next plot, with one light on in the house and the rest of it in the dark.

The sky over that part of the city has been clear all night. It is not clear now.

Cloud comes up over the water tank of the Suri house and over the two towers behind it, going black at the edge, and the wind turns twice and comes in hard and takes the marigold off the gateposts and lays it in the road. Somewhere two lanes away somebody's tin sheet goes over. The street lamp swings. And there is lightning under the cloud, deep in, no thunder with it yet, three flashes that light the inside of the sky.

On the flat roof of the house behind the Suri plot, an old woman stands with both hands on the parapet.

She is very old and it is not a thing a doctor could account for. Her hair is white and thin and pulled back, and she wears a white sari with the pallu over her head and a woollen shawl over her shoulders, and there is a brass stick hooked over her arm which she has not leaned on in sixty years. Her forearms are so thin that the bangles stand away from the bone. Her eyes in the lightning are not an old woman's eyes.

Behind her, a girl of about twenty is holding her own pallu at her throat against the wind. Bharani, in green, with a red bindi and quick nervous hands, and bare feet on the wet concrete.

"Ye achanak mausam kaise badal gaya?" Bharani says. "Aur ye bijli—"

"Abhi chaubees ghante bhi nahi hue ise." Uttara does not turn round. Her voice is dry and carries without being raised, the way old voices do in a temple. "Maharani ne pehli baar kechuli badli hai. Aur ab se aisa lag raha hai ki unki pariksha shuru ho chuki hai."

The storm rolls and does not break. A rain begins somewhere over the next colony and stops again.

"Kya wo ghar se akele bahar nikli hain?" Bharani's voice goes up. "Humein unhein is haalat mein aise akela nahi chhodna chahiye tha. Kahin wo kisi bade sankat mein hongi to?"

A bucket stands on the roof by the stairhead with a cloth over it and a stone on the cloth.

Uttara's hands stay flat on the parapet. Her bangles knock once against the stone, and the wind lays the marigold it has been carrying down on the parapet between them.

"Pehla charan nikal gaya." Uttara says it to the lane, not to the girl. "Doosra bhi." The lightning goes inside the cloud again. "Aaj raat teesra shuru ho gaya hai."

Bharani's hands come together at her throat.

"Shiv vardani." Uttara begins it the way the old ones begin it, flat and without hurry, and the wind takes each word and puts it down. "Naaglok ki Rani. Anant kul ki Naagrani. Ichchhadari naagin."

"Maa—"

"Shesh Naagin." The old woman turns her head at last, and her eyes in the lightning are not an old woman's eyes. "Aur woh apne chautha charan se ek raat pehle apne ghar se akele bahar hai. Aur use pata nahi hai ki wo kya hai."

Then, from the dark behind them, a sound that has no place on a Delhi rooftop: a hiss, dry and short, with nothing in the frame that has a mouth for it. Bharani crosses herself with her thumb. Down in the lane the street lamp goes round on its bracket, once, and steadies.

And far off, on the black ground beyond the plot where the lane stops, there is a small sound: bones, and gravel, and then nothing.

## Two Men and a Bus

The depot yard at half past two in the morning smells of diesel and wet cardboard, and the tank of the bus nearest the gate is ticking as it cools.

A register lies open on the counter in the office window with a pen chained to it, and the pen has not been used since the last bus in.

A tin of tea stands on the step between the two men.

The glass beside the tin has a chip out of the rim, and nobody has offered the other man one. Two buses stand in a row with their lights off and the yard light above them with a moth going round it. One of the drivers is on the step of the first with a glass of tea and his cap pushed back, and the other is under the second with a torch and his vest over his shoulder.

"Ae bhai, kal tu chhutti mein tha, warna dekhta." The one on the step drinks. "Ek ladki kal humari bus ke saath-saath aise daud rahi thi. Aise, aise."

He does it with two fingers along the rim of the step: a run that no woman runs.

"Arey kaise?"

"Jaise wo…" He tries it again and cannot do it and stops and looks at his fingers. "Arre. Chhod."

The man under the bus shines the torch up at the axle and says nothing for a while.

"Kaun bhai, koi bhi to nahi hai." He comes out from under it, and there is nobody on the lane behind the depot, and no sound but the diesel humming in the tank.

"O bhai." The driver puts his tea down on the step. "Nasha karna band kar de please."

## Sampark

The Naag Mandir on the hill at four o'clock, and the lamp in the outer hall burning with the wind coming through the door.

Bharani has both hands round the flame. She has put a shawl over her head now and she is kneeling, and the wind keeps taking the flame sideways and it keeps standing up again.

"Ab tak ye kabhi bhi is tarah se pehle…" Uttara says, and stops, and the word does not come. She stands at the door with the rain on the steps behind her and her stick in her hand.

"Kya aisa koi tareeka hai?" Bharani says. "Jisse hum jaan sake ki wo kaisi hai aur kahan hai?"

The lamp steadies. The flame stands straight up and stays.

"Sampark," Uttara says.

The word sits in the hall. Bharani looks up at her.

"Sampark," Uttara says again, and puts her hand out, and the flame leans into that hand and does not go out.

Down the hill, a watchman on a scooter with his helmet undone passes a girl in a green sari walking up the road with a shawl over her head, and he raises a hand to her and she lifts hers.

Half a mile on, at the bottom of the hill, where the road turns, the same girl in the same green sari comes out of the dark and crosses in front of his headlight and does not look at him. It is the same face. There is no second girl on that hill and there is no road round it, and it is the same face.

He brakes. He looks in his mirror. There is nobody on the hill behind him and nobody on the road in front of him, and the light of his own headlight is the only thing moving.

He rides the rest of the way home without turning his head once.

## The Third Step

Half past four. The study on the second floor at the back of the Suri house, over the kitchen, and one lamp on the desk and the rest of the room in the dark.

Parmeet sits at the desk with his back to the door and his shoulders out of the shape a man's shoulders are in at that hour. His shirt is off and a shawl is round him and his glasses are off and lying on the page. The gift book is open in front of him, and the London file under it, and the pen where his son laid it.

The phone is on the desk and it is on speaker, and the voice out of it is not a voice from this country.

"Ab samay aa gaya hai." Parmeet says it slowly, and looks at the pages. "Woh dono ghar mein hain. Bahar nahi."

"Wo teesra kadam." The voice from the phone is patient and old and there is a distance in it. "Jo aakhri kadam se bas ek kadam peeche hoga — magar kathinai badhane aur qahar dhaane mein sabse aage. Ab asli pralay aayega jab Mahadev ka teesra netra khul jayega."

Parmeet's thumb goes along the edge of the gift book.

"Aur tab tak?"

"Tab tak sab kuch waise hi chalta rahega." A pause, and the sound of something being set down on a table made of wood, a long way away. "Jaise ab tak chala hai. Chhupa hua. Sadak pe yahan tak ki koi shak na kare."

The lamp on the desk gutters once, and stands up.

Parmeet reaches out and turns the phone over and puts his palm flat on it and leaves it there for a while, looking at nothing.

Then he gets up and goes to the window and stands at the edge of it with the shawl round him, looking down at the lawn, at the gate, at the dark past the gate.

Down there, at the end of the drive, somebody has left the garden lamp on all night.

## The Hymn

The open ground where the lane stops, at ten past five in the morning, with the sky going grey above it and the buildings stopping behind.

The rain comes at ten past five, and it comes down in one piece, across the whole plot at once, and then across the whole city.

It comes down on the plot where the lane stops and the buildings do not, on the broken brick and the scrub and the plinth nobody ever built on. The ground turns to water and the water turns to mud and the mud runs.

She is on the ground in it.

A sheet of tin lies flat in the mud a little way off, and the rain is drumming on it.

Purvi lies on her side with her arm under her and her salwar torn at the knee and one sandal gone, and her spectacles are somewhere in the dark and she cannot see, and she is not moving.

And the ground around her is moving.

Snakes come out of the scrub and the brick into the rain — not two, not a dozen: the ground is thick with them, black and grey and one that is pale as ash. They come out of the wet earth and they come out of the wall and they come over the plinth. They do not come for her. They come to her. They arrive and they settle and they lie against her arms and her throat and her open palm.

One of them lifts its head and strikes her on the inside of the wrist. Another on the ankle. Another under the ear. She does not wake up for any of it and her body takes each strike with a small jolt that does not reach her face.

The venom goes in where the wrist is thinnest, and it goes in cold, and the cold goes up the arm and into the shoulder and stops at the jaw, and waits.

The rain goes on. The lamp that no watchman has lit stands in the middle of that ground with no flame in it. Somewhere behind the wall the animal is standing in the wet with its head down and its shoulder working, and it does not come on.

The score comes up at the bottom of the sky.

*Om.*

A woman's voice begins to scream, and it is not a scream of a woman in pain; it goes up and up and does not stop and does not break, and it goes out over the plot and the road and the rain. Under it, the choir comes in.

*Om.*

The snakes lift off her all at once. All of them, the whole ground of them, and they go back into the scrub and the wall and the wet earth, and the ground is empty.

The moon is behind the cloud and the field is the colour of a wet slate, and the rain comes down on it straight.

Purvi's fingers move in the mud.

The skin at her wrist lifts in one corner and comes up off the flesh under it in a strip, and under the strip the new skin has the sheen of a wet stone and a pattern on it that the rain fills and does not stay in.

Her jaw sets first, and two fangs come down past her lip — the first teeth in that head that belong to nothing human — and the rain on her face turns pink where it runs off them.

Her hand closes. Her spine arches off the ground and stays arched, and her head goes back, and the rain falls into her open eyes and her eyes do not blink and are not her eyes.

The chant comes up under the choir, half words: *ki shakti*, and then something that is nearly *Krishna ki aachar*, and the words are not for the audience to keep.

*Om hawa om.*

Her eyes open gold.

The cost is on the ground under her and nobody is looking at it: two of her nails gone, the inside of her wrist bloody where the skin has come off it in a strip the length of a finger, and the cold in her so deep that the rain is warmer than her. She will not remember any of it. She will remember waking up in a field with a torn salwar and no spectacles, and the whole of that day she will believe she fell.

And the last frame of it is not her face and not the animal and not the rain. It is the ground she is standing on, wet and black and lit from under her, and the print of one bare foot in it that is not a woman's print any more, and the whole of that plot, and the city beyond it, and above the city the cloud coming apart.

Then the title, and the score over it, and the frame goes.

- **The tray, the gate, the tray again.** He is the one carrying the last of the party out to the man who has taken this family's leftovers for eleven years — the only person in the house who says *sukhriya* to him — and he is therefore in the lane when the animal sits. He gets Nandini over the wall and puts a steel tray between it and the boy. **He is not brave in a way that has a power in it**: the tray clangs, his shoulder takes the weight, he goes back two steps, and he stays standing.
- **The run.** He goes after her into the unlit part and does not find her, and comes back to the road, and the blood on the asphalt is the boy's. **He is frightened and the page says so** and does not say it in a way that makes him smaller.
- **The cloth.** He stands in the middle of the road with her torn dupatta in his hands, steady-handed and unable to name what is wrong with him, and Ananta sees him like that in the gate light and says nothing.

**What he does not do.** He does not see the snakes. He does not see her change. He does not know anything at the end of this chapter that he did not know at the beginning of it, except that a thing came out of the dark in his own lane and took a woman away, and that his hands were not enough.

**Where the episode is not certain, and this chapter does not invent**

- **Who Rohan is.** Canon: a neighbour, not a Suri. The page gives him a mother, a sister of six, a plate of biscuits and a printed t-shirt, and no more.
- **How badly he is hurt.** Nothing says. The page stops at the blood on the asphalt.
- **What Ananta does next.** Nothing in this stretch. She is left standing in the gate light, and the chapter does not follow her inside.
- **Where the animal goes.** It is heard and not seen after the plot. Whether Parmeet's household ever knows what it did: canon's to answer.
