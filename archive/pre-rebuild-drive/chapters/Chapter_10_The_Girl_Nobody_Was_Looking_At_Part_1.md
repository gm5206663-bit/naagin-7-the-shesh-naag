The logotype comes up over thundercloud, with something red falling through the frame too slowly to be rain.

A man's voice asks two words in the pitch of a been: *"Kaun hai woh."* A choir of women answers underneath him and leaves the answer unfinished.

The been stops. One low string is struck and left hanging over the black.

A drum, once. Mahadev's trishul is not shown, but the thunder is his. Then the story, and no music under it until the story asks.



## Tina and Roma

The Suri house has its gates open and its lawn lit, and the marigolds are strung in loops from the gateposts to the veranda, and the men at the gate have been told which cars to let through. The mehendi has been on the aunts' hands since four in the afternoon and is still dark at the edges. Sheshnaag's hood is not shown, but the house feels like a temple that has forgotten its god. There is haldi on one of the doorframes, put there that morning by somebody's mother, and a handprint of it on the wall that nobody has wiped off.

A tray of welcome sherbet stands at the top of the steps with a boy behind it in a white coat, and the glasses on it are filled and none of them taken.

Tina's family comes up the drive at a quarter past eight, and Parmeet is at the bottom of the steps to meet them, and Dimple is behind him with her pallu in her hand.

Tina comes up the steps in front of them, because she has been told to. Twenty-two, and tall for it, in a lehenga the colour of the inside of a rose, with the dupatta pinned so that it will not move whatever the evening does. Her hair is half up and the rest of it down her back, and there is a diamond on a fine chain at her throat. She is holding her skirt off the stone with two fingers the way she has been taught. Her eyes go along the veranda, over the marigolds, over the waiters, and stop on nothing, and she smiles at the right second.

Roma comes up behind her, in green, with her spectacles on. Roma reports for the channel's evening bulletin and is here as Tina's people, and nobody at the door is looking at her, and she settled what she thinks of that a long time ago.

"Tina and Roma, you all are looking so pretty tonight." Someone at the door puts his hands together. "Bhai sahab, hum log to dar gaye the ki kahin aap logon ko late na ho jaye aane mein." "Arey, idhar aao."

"Mera chhota beta." Parmeet has his hand on the shoulder of a young man and is saying it to Tina's father in the doorway.

The son stands a head above his father and does not stand the way a man stands who knows it. Twenty-four. Short hair pushed back off his forehead where he has run his hand through it once too often tonight. The eyes are long and quick and black and they take in a stranger in one movement and give nothing back; the jaw is square and set. Nothing about him asks the room for anything, and the room has already given it: the two men at the gate have stopped talking to each other, and Tina's aunt has turned round, and neither of them could tell you why they did it. A white shirt with the collar still stiff, because his mother bought it on Thursday; the sleeves turned back twice; dark trousers; black shoes somebody else has polished. His hands are empty and he does not know what to do with them.

"Vihaan. London se aaya hai — kal subah ki flight hai iski."

"Achha, achha." Tina's father shakes his hand and looks at him for the time it takes to price a shirt. "London mein rehta hai?"

"Kaam ke liye." Vihaan smiles with his mouth. "Rehna zyada, ghar kam."

Tina's father laughs at that, once, and something in the boy's shoulders comes down half an inch.

"Andar aao, andar." Parmeet takes his own hand back. "Photo ke liye sab log khade ho jao."

The two families arrange themselves on the steps for a photograph, and the photographer moves them a step to the left twice, and Tina stands at the edge of the group with her hands folded in front of her and her eyes going once to the gate.

"Vihaan." Parmeet says it over the heads of the guests, and does not lower his voice. "Tumne study kholi?"

"Khol di."

"Mahine ka hisaab aaj raat hi band karo. Subah ki flight hai." Parmeet turns back to Tina's father before the answer comes. "Ladka hai, par hisaab uske haath ka saaf hai."

"Achha." Tina's father looks at the young man for as long as it takes to price a shirt. "London mein kaam karta hai?"

"Ji. Hamare office ka London side."

"Bahut achha."

The photographer puts him at the end of the back row, and Dimple pulls him forward by the sleeve until he is in it properly, and the bulb goes off twice.

One more for Karan and Tina, with her mother's hand on Tina's shoulder in it. Karan stands beside his fiancée with his arms at his sides and does not touch her, and then the whole party moves inside, and Vihaan goes up the back stairs to the study.

## The Gift Book

The study is on the second floor at the back, over the kitchen, with one desk lamp on and its light lying across the open book, and it smells of the food and of old paper, and there are two things open on the desk: the month's London file and the gift book, with a pen laid across it.

Guests have been in and out all evening. Vihaan sits down and takes the pen and enters what is there: a watch, two sets of bangles, a cheque from Tina's uncle, a chain, and the envelope Dimple has set on the tray with the others and left shut.

Vihaan opens it. It holds a bank slip, and on the slip the amount is written in figures, and beside the slip, in the hand that keeps this book, the amount is written again as a word so that no one can add a nought to it.

Vihaan looks at the figures for a while. Then he turns the gift book back four pages and looks at the entry for another wedding, and back four more, and the same hand has written the same kind of slip for the same family.

Vihaan puts the pen down and sits back.

"Kitna?" His father is in the doorway with his glass, and nobody in the study has heard him come up the stairs.

"Bees."

"Likh do."

"Likh diya."

Parmeet looks at the book, then at his son, and takes the slip out of it and puts it in his own pocket and goes back down to the party, and the door swings shut on its hinge and does not latch.

Vihaan sits at the desk for a moment with the pen in his hand. Then he writes the entry out again, the same amount in the same figures, on the one page of the book that has been ruled for a second copy, and closes it, and puts the pen across it exactly where he found it.

## Purvi Looking

The hall at nine in the evening with the chandelier on and the canvas up over the garden doors, and the party going on in three rooms at once.

Purvi moves through the rooms with a tray she is not carrying and a phone in her hand she is not using.

Purvi is twenty-three and looks younger in this house, and that is the layers: a cotton salwar in a green that has gone a little grey with washing, a dupatta pinned at the shoulder, her hair in a plait that she has redone twice already tonight and that is coming loose again at the crown. Her spectacles have a mended hinge and the mending shows. Her ears are pierced twice on the left and once on the right. She has put on the only good sandals she owns and they are one size too narrow, and she is not complaining about them, and it is the sort of thing that shows on a face before it shows in a walk.

Vihaan comes the other way through the same rooms, a message open on the phone in his hand. He is smiling, small and private, at the screen. She moves out of his road with the tray she is not carrying, and he says sorry to the tray and not to her, and goes on. His jaw is steady, and his hand is steady on the phone.

Purvi looks at the door. She looks at the stairs. She looks at the group by the sideboard, and the group by the sofa, and the men on the veranda with their glasses, and Ravish is not in any of them. She looks at the bookshelf where he stood, and her hand is steady on her own tray, and she smiles at nothing, small and sad.

"Didi ne kaha tha ki Ravish sir function ke baad apni family se baat karenge." She says it to Roma, who is standing at her elbow with a plate. "I hope aisa hi ho. Par didi hai kahan?"

Her phone goes in her hand, and she looks at it, and her face changes.

"Hello? — Vikram?" She turns her shoulder to the room. "Haan, haan, main sun rahi hoon."

Roma watches her over the plate. "Tum aate hi nahi ho aaj?" she says, to Purvi's back. "No, boss, aaj nahin. Magar jaldi aaunga. Den, I am all yours." Purvi's voice has gone up an octave. "O ho, Vikram ka call. Madam blush kar rahi hai."

"Chup kar."

"Lagta hai Tina ke baad tera hi number lagne waala hai."

"Mera number tere baad hai. Isiliye to keh rahi hoon, der mat kar."

Roma puts the plate down on the arm of a chair. "Roma, tu reporter se kuch zyada hi ban gayi hai." "Arey bhai ki life mein — main to full try mode mein hoon. Lekin tera hi number aayega, dekh le."

## The New Channel

The far end of the hall, under the chandelier, where the men stand with their glasses and the noise of them is a wall.

At the far end of the room Aryaman stands with his glass and a group of men who are laughing at everything he says.

"Bhai, zero chance road mein baitha hua hai." He turns his glass. "Finally, teri shaadi ke baad kahan tak pahunchi? Mujhe to honestly lagaa tha ki teri shaadi us ek cheez se ho sakti hai."

"Kis se?"

"Iski ego se."

The men laugh. Karan's face does not move.

"Arre man, tum bhi na." Tina's mother says it from the sofa, and Aryaman inclines his glass to her. "Achha, by the way, congratulations — tumhare new channel ke liye. Congrats, bro."

"Shaadi yahan meri ho rahi hai, magar mere sasural waale tohfe tujhe de rahe hain."

Aryaman drinks. "Karan, yahi baat to tu samajhta nahi. Main spotlight ke peeche nahi bhaagta." He puts the glass down on the tray of a boy going past without looking at the boy. "Spotlight khud samajh jaati hai ki kis taraf dekhna hai."

He lifts his chin at the back of the room. "Vihaan. Bol, main bhaagta hoon?"

"Bhaagta nahin hai koi," Vihaan says from by the bookshelf, both hands resting on the wood beside him. "Bhaagne ka maqsad hota hai. Aap yahan khade ho isliye ki yahan camera hai."

Karan laughs, and Aryaman laughs, and the men with them laugh a half-beat after Aryaman. The woman at the sofa laughs one beat later than all of them, and knows she has, and says nothing about it.

Roma, from the sofa, says it loudly enough: "Karan bhai apni engagement mein itna bem se aaye hue kyun lag rahe hain? Ye hamesha aise hi kyon rehta hai?"

"Bhabhi aa gayi," someone says at the door.

Aryaman turns, and Tina is beside him with her hands folded, and he says it to her quietly, with the corner of his mouth: "Karan bahut possessive hai. Apne success ke baare mein bhi, aur apne pyaar ke baare mein bhi. Sambhaal lena ise."

Tina smiles at him and does not answer. Vihaan smiles small and sharp at Aryaman from by the bookshelf, the smile of a man who has measured this man and found him lighter than he pretends.

## Two Sisters in the Crowd

The hall at the window end, with the curtains open on the lawn and the light of the drive lying flat across it.

A tray of small brass lamps stands on the sill with every one of them lit.

Vihaan comes along under the curtains with two of the aunts behind him, and one of the aunts is telling him about London in a voice that carries into the passage.

Ananta comes in with her dupatta on her shoulder and her eyes going round the room once, and finds Ravish at the far side by the window with his back to the party, and takes one step towards him.

Ananta is the best-dressed woman in that hall tonight and she has done it on purpose: a dark red sari with a gold border, the blouse cut high at the back, her hair down and heavy, a small diamond at each ear. Her lipstick is the red she wears when she intends to be looked at. She has been crying, and she has fixed it, and it takes knowing her to see it: the skin under her eyes is a shade darker than the rest of her face, and the fixing has been done quickly in a car.

Purvi's hand closes on her wrist.

"Didi." She pulls her into the passage between the dining room and the stairs, where the music is quieter. "Tumne kaha tha ki Ravish sir aaj sab bata denge. Wo kar denge na?"

"Kar denge." Ananta straightens her dupatta. "Purvi, sab kuch ho jayega."

"Baad mein baat karungi, sorry — par baat to aaj Ravish sir karenge."

"Tu ek percent to us pe bharosa rakh."

"Chal, shuruaat ho gayi." Ananta looks back into the room, at the cake, at the flowers, at her sister's face. "Ab aage ka raasta seedha hai. Aur wo seedha kya hai, sirf hum log samajhte hain."

The band goes quiet and comes back with a shehnai, and the room turns toward the middle of it, because a shehnai in a hall means somebody is about to be married or engaged and everybody in that room has been trained by a hundred such evenings to look.

The rings are exchanged in the middle of the room. Dimple's hand comes out sideways and finds her son's sleeve while she watches, and holds it there, and he stands still and lets her.

Everyone claps, and Tina's mother cries, and Parmeet puts his hand on Tina's father's shoulder and says something into his ear that makes the older man laugh and shake his head.

Then Parmeet says it out loud, to the room, with his arm out: "Abhi to sirf shuruaat hai."

## Baad Mein Baat Karenge

The window at the far side of the hall with the curtain half across it, and the light of the drive coming in through the gap and standing on the sill.

Ananta gets to Ravish at the window.

"Ravish." Her voice is light. "I'm so happy. Finally hum ek ho gaye hain, aur mujhe poora yakeen hai ki hamara ye rishta hamesha ke liye barkarar rahega."

"Haan." He looks past her at the room. "Yahan pe log hain. Baad mein baat karen."

"Mujhe isi baat ki to fikr hai." She takes his hand. "Log kya kahenge, jab unhein pata chalega."

"Relax."

"Itna sab kuch ho jaane ke baad bhi tum relax kaise ho?" Her fingers tighten. "Tum isliye itne relaxed ho na — kyunki tumne sab kuch bata diya sabko. Hai na?"

"Nahi." He takes his hand back out of hers and puts it in his pocket. "Nahi, abhi abhi ghar waalon ko kuch nahi bataya. But I have patience, baby. Main bataunga na. Maine bola na — abhi to sirf sagaai hui hai."

He goes along the window and into the group of men and his glass is in his hand before he is three steps away.

Parmeet is across the room with his own glass, and he is not looking at the men, and he is not looking at his wife. He is looking at the window.

* * *
## The Wrist

The hall at half past nine with the chandelier on and the tables being carried out and the floor opened up.

The rings are on and the hall is working again, and two of the caterer's boys are taking a long table out through the middle of it, one at each end, both of them looking at their own feet.

Purvi stands at the side of the hall with a tray of empty glasses against her hip and her back to the wall, waiting for a gap.

Vihaan is coming down the last three stairs with his phone at his ear, saying something about a payment that has to be made in London before the banks there close, and he is not looking at the hall at all.

The table comes round.

His hand closes on her wrist and takes her back two steps, into the gap between the pillar and the wall, and holds there while the table goes past, and the glasses on her tray do not touch each other.

Then the table is gone and his hand is still there. His jaw is tight, and his thumb brushes her wrist once, light, before it should.

It comes off her wrist a moment after it should have, and he puts it in his trouser pocket, and says into the phone, "Haan. Aaj hi." His breath is steady but his ears are red.

"Sorry, sir." She says it to the floor. "Main dekh rahi thi."

"Aap dekh rahi thin." He is already going past her, and he says it without stopping and without turning his head. He is smiling, small and private, at the floor. "Table dekh raha tha."

Pinky, four feet away with a plate, says to Roma, without lowering her voice at all: "Dekha? Humare Vihaan kitne acche hain. Staff se bhi aise baat karte hain."

The band starts the next song on the dholak.

## Danger Zone

The side of the hall by the sideboard, in the chandelier's light, with the bottles standing on it and the ice bucket sweating onto the cloth under it.

A plate of small kebabs stands at the end of the sideboard with the sticks still in them.

A tray of clean glasses stands under the sideboard, and the cloth over it has been pulled back.

Vihaan comes to the sideboard for ice with his glass held out, and the tongs go in and come out.

"Kaunsa zone?" he says into the ice bucket, and his voice carries the length of the hall and no further. "Aur kiske liye. Batana toh padega."

He takes the glass away, and the ice in it has begun to go, and neither of them turns round.

"Danger zone mein." Roma says it into her glass. "Haina? Oh, danger zone."

"Sweetie, tu already uski entry gate pe khadi hai." Pinky has her arm through Roma's. "Bas entry ticket nahi mil rahi tujhe. To tu dilwa de — kahin se sauce lagwa de na."

"Main abhi lagati hoon." Pinky turns round. "Bhai, assistant kahan hai?"

Purvi is at the table with the plates.

"Assistant main yahan hoon."

"Arey, your Aryaman bhai's new assistant, right? Jao, unhein dance ke liye bula ke na. Hum logon ne bulaya hai."

* * *
## The Dance

The hall at ten with the chandelier on and the floor cleared back to the pillars.

The band in the hall has gone into something with a dholak and a synth under it, and the aunts have started moving their shoulders, and the children have been cleared off the middle of the floor.

Purvi goes across it to him.

"Sir, sab logon ko aapke saath dance karna hai. Please."

Aryaman looks at her the way he looks at a bad cut in a package — head down, eyes up — and says something about death, and about who exactly is dying, and takes a drink.

"Sir, sab wait kar rahe hain." Her voice is quiet and her ears are hot. "Please."

Aryaman turns his shoulder to her and says something to Roma that makes Roma laugh, and it is not unkind, and it is worse than unkind: it is a man finishing with a subject.

Three hundred people have watched her walk out onto that floor. Three hundred people are watching her stand on it.

And then the aunts begin the game, because the aunts begin the game at every sangeet in this country and nobody in that hall is going to stop them tonight.

"Jodi banao! Jodi banao!" Pinky is on her feet with her glass up, and her voice goes over the top of the band. "Jiski jodi nahin bani, uski shaadi der se hogi. Ye riwaz hai — main nahin banaya."

The floor fills up in a rush. The aunts take their husbands. The uncles take their wives. Two of the children are pushed together and scream. Tina's cousin goes to Roma and Roma goes without a word. Pinky drags Arjun off a chair by his wrist and he lets himself be dragged. Even Dimple is standing with her hand out, and Parmeet gets up out of the big chair and takes it, and the room claps that, and the band plays for it.

And there are two people left on the floor.

Purvi is still on the floor, with her hands at her sides. Vihaan is by the chairs with the house book open on the seat beside him and a pen in his hand, and he has not moved, because he came into that room to get a signature and not to be in a photograph or a dance or anything else in it.

The whole hall turns and looks at the two of them at once, and the noise it makes is not cruel. It is worse than cruel. It is delighted.

"Arre!" One of the aunts has both hands on her cheeks. "Ye do bache hue hain! Jodi adhoori hai!"

"Vihaan." Dimple says it from the middle of the floor, over her own dance, without turning round, in the voice of a woman who has run this house for twenty-four years. "Jodi poori kar. Ek gaana."

The band takes the hint and the dholak comes up.

Vihaan puts the book down on the seat of the chair and the pen on top of it.

Then he crosses the floor to her, and he stands in front of her, and he smiles small and private at her, and his jaw is loose, and his breath is slow, and his hand is steady on his thigh, and he holds his hand out. His hand is steady and his face is open, and the whole of that hall has just decided this for the two of them, and he does not look away, and he does not know why his ears are hot, and he does not know why his chest feels full all at once.

"Aapki marzi hai," he says. "Par agar aap na bolengi, to mazaaq meri bhi udayenge."

"Aapki?"

"Main abhi tak jodi mein nahin tha." He says it plainly. "Aap aa gayin to main bhi aa gaya."

Purvi's hands come off her sides and go behind her back. Her ears are hot and her chin is up and she does the arithmetic of a girl who has been staff in that building for one evening, and who knows what a photograph is worth in a house of this size.

"Sir—"

"Vihaan."

"Vihaan." She says it, and her voice does something on it, and she puts her hand in his.

One string, low, and then the song the band is playing takes it back.

The hall makes a sound of its own, all round them, the way halls do.

It is not a slow song. It takes them a bar and a half to find the beat, and she is stiff as a new door, and her palm is damp, and the aunties have already started talking behind their hands.

"Aap count kar rahi hain." He says it at her ear, at a volume that belongs to two people and not to a hall.

"Kya?"

"Aap count kar rahi hain. Andar se." He turns her, and the room goes round them, and her heel comes down on the marble and does not slip. "Teen pe."

"Main—"

"Doosre pe nahin. Teesre pe." He is not smiling. "Aap chhoti si ek uchhal leti hain teen pe. Aur usse hi aap theek lagti hain."

Her face does nothing for a moment, and then everything at once, and she laughs, once, on the beat, and turns her face into his shoulder and hides it there.

And then, three turns later, with her hand in his and the dholak going, the hall goes away.

The lamp at the end of the hall gutters — once, and then twice — and comes back. The doors at the far end stand open on the lawn and the wind comes through them and takes the marigold off the pillar and lays it on the marble, and nobody in that hall is looking at the marigold.

Purvi's hand goes cold in his.

And under the dholak, under the singing, at a volume below the band: a hiss. Dry and short, one second, and there is nothing in that hall with a mouth that could make it, and nobody in that hall hears it but the two of them, and neither of them says anything about it.

It starts at the base of her spine and goes up, the way it went up in the dark of the temple three nights ago. Her skin does the thing her skin has done twice in her life. She has to hold on to a man's shoulder in a room full of three hundred people to keep her feet.

"Aap theek hain?"

"Haan." It is not a yes. Her voice has gone thin and her eyes are wide and she is looking at nothing at all, at the far doors, at the lawn beyond them. "Haan. Main— haan."

"Aankhein band kar lijiye."

She does it, with her chin up, in the middle of the floor.

"Ab dholak sun lijiye." His hand is flat and steady on her back. "Bas wohi. Aur kuch nahin."

She listens to the drum. The cold goes down off her arms. Somewhere behind them a child laughs, and the band comes back up, and she opens her eyes, and the gold at the edge of them has gone out.

Four bars of the dholak.

His hand on her back does not move.

Her pulse is under his fingers and it is fast, and it does not slow down.

The lamp over the mandap gutters once and comes back up, and nobody in the hall looks at it except one man.

The photographer is up on a chair at the side with the long lens, and he takes it at that moment, because the light goes and comes back and the two of them are in the frame with the lamp behind them and her hand is white on his shoulder. He is paid by the picture and not by the hour, and he takes four more of the same pair before the song ends, and the flash goes across the hall each time, once, hard, and the hall squints and goes on dancing.

"Aryaman." Tina's uncle has his glass up and his voice pitched to carry across to the sofa where Aryaman has gone to stand. "Yeh kya ho raha hai? Apni assistant ke saath?"

"Wo humare channel ki reporter hain." Vihaan says it without turning round, still dancing, and he has not raised his voice, and it carries anyway. "Aur unhein dance achha aata hai."

The laugh goes round the room and stops being a laugh at the far end of it.

Aryaman steps out of the light of it and straightens his cuff and walks off to the far side of the hall without a word to anyone.

And in the corner of the room, in the big chair, Parmeet has stopped dancing and is standing with his glass on the arm of the chair and his hand still round it, and he is watching his younger son dance with the girl who came to ask for a job. It is the face he keeps for a set of figures that do not add up, and he keeps it there for the rest of the song.

The song ends on the dholak and the room claps, and the clap has a shape in it that everybody hears and nobody names.

"Mujhe pata hi nahi tha, tu itna achha dance kar leti hai." Ananta is at the edge of the floor with her dupatta in her hand and her eyes shining and her voice doing too much. "Aur aaj tu pehli baar ghar ke bahar logon ke saamne nachi hai."

"Haan Deep, mujhe bhi nahi pata tha ki main itna achha dance karti hoon."

"Practice mat chhodna." Ananta takes her by the wrist and says it low. "Ho sakta hai meri aur Ravish ki shaadi mein tujhe isi Vihaan ke saath hi dance karna pade."

"Vihaan." Dimple's voice comes over the top of the room. "Tere papa ka sign chahiye na? Abhi le le, phir wo baith jayenge."

"Aa raha hoon, chachi." He picks the book up off the seat of the chair and goes to his father with it open at the page, and his father signs without looking up from the man he is talking to, and the boy stands there a second longer than the signature needs.

In the passage, with the book shut under his arm and the hall going on behind him, he stands still for a moment and looks at his own right hand.

He turns it over. He puts it in his pocket.

Then he goes back into the hall and gets the caterer's signature on the second copy, and he does not look across the floor at the girl by the chairs, and he knows exactly where she is standing.

Purvi is at the edge of the floor with her hands at her sides and her ear still warm, and she is looking at the back of him while the room talks about something else.

Purvi turns to go, and her dupatta does not come with her.

The edge of it is caught on the arm of the chair with the book on it — caught under the brass cap at the end of the arm, and she pulls once and the cloth holds, and if she pulls again the cloth will tear, and it is not her dupatta to tear.

Purvi stands there with her hand on her own shoulder and the room going on around her.

Then he is back for the pen. He comes the three steps from his father's chair with his hand out for it, and he sees the cloth, and he does not say anything and neither does she.

He puts one hand flat on the chair arm and presses the brass cap down with his thumb, and the cloth comes free in her hand.

"Shukriya, sir."

"Pen." He picks it up off the seat. "Main pen lene aaya tha."

Roma says it across the room at exactly the wrong volume, to Pinky and to anybody else who wants it: "Bhai sahab ka beta. Kitne sanskaari hain. Ek pen ke liye khud aaye."

Vihaan puts the pen in his shirt pocket and goes back to his father with it, and the two of them bend over the same page, and Purvi walks out of the hall with her dupatta over her arm and does not put it on until she is in the passage.

## Two Houses Away

The sofa end of the front room at eleven, with the veranda doors open, the lamp on the side table lit, and the party going on outside them.

Vihaan comes in from the veranda and sits on the arm of the sofa with his glass, and Roma turns her squint on him and asks him where her spectacles are, and he says he has not seen them, and he has not.

"Madam, chashma kahan hai?" Roma, from the sofa, squinting. "Tujhe dikhai kaise de raha hai bina chashme ke?"

"Deep, wo mera chashma pehna hai."

Dimple comes through the room with a list in her hand and stops at Purvi's chair.

"Purvi, tum Anant ki piye ho na?"

"Ji."

"To ye saaman humare out-house ke store room mein rakh ke aa jao."

"Kahan?"

"Out-house humare ghar se do ghar chhodkar hai. Seedhe jao, phir left lo, phir right lo — aur phir bhi samajh mein na aaye to kisi se poochh lo."

"Theek hai." Purvi gets up and takes the two bags. "Main ye thoda rakh ke aati hoon. Left, right."

"Haan, haan."

Purvi is at the door with the bags when Roma comes past her. "Tum abhi tak gayi nahi?"

"Main ja hi rahi thi, bas."

"Jao."

Left at the peepal. Right at the gate with the tin sheet. The out-house is dark, and the store room smells of mothballs and cement dust, and she puts the two bags on the shelf and the door does not lock properly, and she pulls it to twice.

## Superman

The edge of the lawn at eleven at night, where the garden wall ends and the lane's houses start, and the third house's balcony lit by one bulb.

There is a child hanging from the balcony of the third house.

Both his hands are on the rail. One foot is on the pipe below it and the other foot is in the air, and there is nobody on the balcony behind him, and the rail is old and the screws have pulled out of the plaster on the left side, and the whole thing leans.

Purvi drops the key.

"Yashu!" A woman's voice comes from inside the house, and there is the sound of a chair going over.

The rail leans further and the boy's other foot comes off the pipe.

Purvi does not decide. Her shoulder goes down and her feet leave the ground.

Purvi goes up the wall of that house with her hands on the window frames and the drainpipe and the crack between the bricks, and the balcony rail comes level with her chest. Her arm goes round the child and takes his weight. The rail comes off the plaster on the left side with a sound of a tooth being pulled.

They land on the lawn together, her back first.

The boy is on top of her, and the boy is laughing, and the woman is in the doorway with her hand over her mouth.

"Oh god. Oh god." She gets down on the grass. "Bach gaya. Yashu. Yashu, theek hai tu? Kuch hua?"

The boy is not looking at his mother. The boy is looking at the woman under him.

"Wao." He sits up. "Aap to superman ho."

"Nahi." Purvi's voice comes out wrong. "Superman to nahi." She gets her elbow under her. "Par haan, super woman zaroor ho sakti hoon."

"To aapki cap kahan hai? Wo jo shoulder pe hoti hai."

"Gharka kaam mein pehen ke aana padta hai."

The boy's eyes come down her arm. "Itna bada to snake hota hai." He holds his hands apart, wide. "Kya aap naagin ho?"

"Nahi." She says it fast. "Wo sirf TV serials mein hoti hai."

She gets up. Her knees are shaking and her palms are wet, and there is a line of blood on the inside of her wrist with no cut to go with it. She puts her hand up to her head. There is no hood; there is hair. The things at her wrists are going back under the skin, and she will spend the rest of the night failing to describe the sensation.

"Thank you so much." The mother is up and holding her son by both shoulders. "Agar aap nahi hoti to mera bete ko—"

"Koi baat nahi."

"Par aap bina sahare ke itni oonchai par aayi kaise?" The woman looks at the balcony, and then at the wall, and then at the girl. "Kaise?"

"Main…" Purvi puts her hand on the wall behind her. "Maine darwaza khola. Andar se."

The woman stops looking at the balcony, and puts her hand on the girl's arm instead. "You are looking really pretty, bhabhi."

"Main bhabhi?"

"Bhaiya ki shaadi hai na ghar mein."

"Nahi." Purvi shakes her head, and her voice is steady and her hands are not. "Main sirf kaam karne aayi hoon."

An old man is coming up the lane with a sack of cut grass on his shoulder, and he has seen a girl come down off the wall of the third house, and the question is already in his mouth when Purvi stops. "Woh—" "Kuch nahin, chacha." Vihaan is out of the gate behind her, and he is not loud. "Party andar hai. Sab andar hai aaj." The old man looks at the sleeve of the man who has spoken, and takes the question with him up the lane and into the next house. Then Vihaan puts a key into Purvi's hand — out of the gravel, with the dirt on it still — and goes back in at the side door without a word to her, and she closes her fingers on it and does not thank him, because there is nothing in it to thank anybody for.

Purvi goes back down the lane with the key in her fist and the blood drying on her wrist, and she walks past the house and stops at the corner, and she stands there for a moment and looks at her own two hands.

Nothing hurts. That is the thing. She fell from a first-floor balcony with a child in her arms, and her back took the lawn, and nothing hurts. She turns her hands over. She looks at them.

## The Study Door

The back stairs are dark and the study door is ajar, and there is a light in the crack of it.

Purvi goes up with her hands still wet from the tap at the side of the house, and the wrist she has been holding under the water, and she stops on the landing because the boy is at the desk inside.

Vihaan looks up.

Vihaan does not say anything for a moment. He looks at her face, and then at the hand she is holding, and then he closes the book on the desk, unhurried, with the pen laid across it exactly where he found it, and stands up. His jaw is tight, and his thumb brushes her wrist as he lets go, and his ears are red at the tips. There is a line between his eyebrows that is new tonight.

"Aap theek hain?"

"Haan."

"Kuch hua tha neeche?"

"Nahi." She says it too quickly. "Bachche ka case tha. Girne waala tha."

"Achha."

He comes round the desk.

"Haath dijiye."

"Sir—"

"Haath." He puts the desk lamp round with one finger so that the light falls on the corner of the desk. "Main kuch nahin karunga. Dekhna hai."

She puts her hand out, and he takes it at the wrist and turns it over, and looks at it the way he looks at a proof sheet: the whole of it, then the place that matters. The wrist is red and clean and there is nothing on it but water.

"Yeh tap ke neeche se aaya hai."

"Haan."

"Aur neeche bachcha tha."

"Haan."

He lets go of her wrist.

Then he pulls out the desk drawer and takes out a flat tin with a cross on it, and opens it, and takes out one strip of plaster and puts it on the edge of the desk, and pushes the drawer shut with his knee.

"Aap jhooth bolti hain," he says, "par imaandaari se." He sits down. "Isi liye pakda nahin jaata."

She laughs. It comes out of her in the dark of that landing before she can do anything about it, one short laugh with nothing amused in it, and she puts her free hand over her mouth at once.

"Sorry, sir."

"Plaster le lijiye." He is already looking at the book. "Aur agli baar jab neeche kuch ho, toh mujhe bata dijiye. Main is ghar mein sabse kam kaam ka aadmi hoon, par main sun sakta hoon."

"Ji."

"Aur Purvi."

Purvi stops on the landing.

"Aaj raat aap paidal nahin jayengi." He turns a page. "Driver ko bol diya hai. Wo gate ke bahar khada hai."

"Sir, main—"

"Maine kuch poochha nahin." He says it without lifting his head, and it is not unkind and it is not a question. "Das minute. Uske baad wo chala jayega."

She goes past the door and up, and at the top of the stairs she looks back, and he is still standing at the desk with his hand flat on the closed book, looking at the door she has gone through.

When she is gone he sits down again and puts both hands over his face for a moment, and then takes them away, and opens the book, and does not write anything.

## The Letter He Asked For

The veranda at midnight with one bulb on over the door and the party's noise coming round the corner of the house.

A cushion lies on the bench against the wall, and a glass stands on the floor under it, half full.

Vihaan comes round the corner of the house and stops when he sees the two of them on the bench, and goes back the way he came, and Arjun calls after him twice and gets nothing.

Arjun is on the veranda with his glass when Ananta comes out for air.

"Bhabhi." He moves up on the bench. "Baithiye."

"Main bhabhi nahi hoon abhi."

"Ho." He grins. "Arre, Ravish bhai mujhse kuch nahi chhipate. Mujhe aapke aur unke baare mein sab kuch pata hai." He lifts his glass to her. "Bro is really lucky ki unko aap jaisa partner mil raha hai."

"Thank you."

"Arre mujhe to aap jaisi koi mili hi nahi." He puts his glass down. "Nahi, aisa nahi hai. Tumhein mujhse better mil jayegi. Sachcha pyaar sabko mil jata hai." He looks at his own feet for a moment. "Ek ladki pasand to hai mujhe, par himmat nahi hoti." He looks up. "Aap meri help karogi?"

"Mujhe kya karna hoga?"

"Chairs, chairs." He sits forward. "Bahut jald hamara ek teer do nishane laga lega. Bas sab kuch safe aur secret hai. Don't worry. Sab under control hai."

Ananta laughs, because he is laughing.

"Haan, Sukhinder." His phone buzzes on his thigh and he gets up with it and steps off the veranda into the dark, and his voice goes on for a while, low and friendly, and then stops.

Across the lawn, at the window of the side room, Ravish stands with his hands in his pockets and watches his cousin take her hand.
