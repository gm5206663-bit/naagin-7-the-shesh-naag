# Chapter 17 — The Reed and the Brass Knife

*Episode reference: Naagin, season 7, Episode 7, second part. The ledger calls it by the two things the men say out loud before they do them.*

*Draft; not yet accepted.*

### TITLES

*The twelve seconds the season plays on every night after its first: the theme, then straight in (`canon/TITLES_SEQUENCE.md` §I). The full forty seconds are written once, at the head of Chapter 1, and are not repeated below it.*

The logotype comes up over thundercloud, with something red falling through the frame too slowly to be rain.

A man's voice asks two words in the pitch of a been: *"Kaun hai woh."* A choir of women answers underneath him and leaves the answer unfinished.

The been stops. One low string is struck and left hanging over the black.

A drum, once. Then the story, and no music under it until the story asks.


## The Reed

The hall at four in the afternoon, with the chandelier on and the tent's canvas going up outside the windows.

"Ek minute."

There is a chair in the second row with two coils of the sound men's cable on the seat of it and nobody sitting in it, and when one of Tina's uncles asks where *chhote sahab* is, Parmeet says *Ghaziabad* and does not turn his head.

Parmeet Suri stands up out of the chair by the fire and puts both his hands behind his back, and the hall waits for him the way a hall waits for a man who has not yet said the useful thing.

The tray of the wedding's last tea stands on the sideboard with the cups against each other.

"Bahu rani ne kaha ki woh naagin hai." He says it without any triumph in it at all. "Toh phir main bhi ek baat bata doon."

Parmeet turns round to the men who came with him.

The been lies across the knees of the man who carries it, and the case for it is at his feet with the lid open.

"**Reedh ki haddi pe maarunga na, to us second mein mar jayegi.** Bardasht nahi kar payegi." He touches the back of his own neck, lightly, to show them where. "**Kamzori hoti hai naagin ki. Reed ki haddi pe [maar], aur uska kaam tamaam.**"

"Papa." Ravish's voice. "Papa, aap kya—"

"Beta, tu chup reh." Parmeet does not look at his son. "Aaj tera din nahi hai."

Ananta stands in front of the fire with her hands at her sides and does not turn round.

## "Been Bajao"

The hall at half past four, with the chandelier on and the two rows of chairs filled to the walls.

"**Koi nahi hilega.**"

Parmeet says it to the room and not to the men, and the men move anyway.

The tray of tea stands at the side of the hall with the cups against each other and nobody has taken one.

"Koi nahi hilega. Main sabka yahi haal kar dunga." He walks the length of the fire and comes back. "Wahan se mera been lao. **Isse naagin ko paane ke liye humein ise naagin roop mein laana padega.**"

In Parmeet's pocket the phone begins to ring against the cloth, and he puts his hand flat over it and does not take it out, and the name on the screen is Vihaan's, and it rings until it stops, and then it begins again.

The man at the passage does not move.

The tent's canvas comes in and goes out at the window and the light through it moves on the floor.

A glass stands on the sideboard with the water in it and nobody has touched it.

"Been bajao."

## The Sound From the West

It comes from the west side of the house, past the tent wall and the store room and the small room at the back where the case has been standing open on the floor since before dawn.

Two notes. Then a third, and then the fourth, and the fourth is the one that stays.

The case on the floor of the small room has its cloth turned back, and the reed in it is not there.

In the hall the men put their hands over their ears and the women at the back stand up, and Ananta's shoulders come down half an inch, and nothing else in that room happens at all.

Purvi's hands go to the sides of her own head.

"Ise band karo." She says it to nobody. "Kisi ne— ise band karo."

"Purvi." Her mother has her by the arm. "Purvi, baith ja. Baith ja, meri bachche."

The been goes on. It is not loud. It is the wrong kind of quiet.

## Outside the Wall

At the west wall, on the other side of the tent, in the dark, an old woman in a sari and a younger one with her dupatta drawn over her head stand with their hands folded and their faces turned to the sound.

"**Bahut badi aafat aa rahi hai.**" Uttara's voice is not steady. "**Mujhe andar jaana hoga.**"

"Main aapke saath chalungi."

"**Nahin, Bharani.**" Uttara's hand comes out and stops her. "**Tum yahin ruko. Wahan se khatra aaye to tumhein sambhalna hai.**"

"Ma."

"Yahin ruko." The old woman looks at the gate, and then at the wall, and then at the girl. "**Is dhun se Purvi ka sach in sabke saamne aa jayega.**"

Uttara puts her hand flat on the wall.

The old woman's stick stands against the wall beside her and she does not lean on it.

The tent's rope is tied to a peg at their feet and the peg is going out of the ground.

Beyond the wall the lane is empty and the lamp at the corner is on.

"Mujhe in logon ka dhyan bhatkana hoga." She says it to the brick. "Purvi ko bachana hoga."

## "Yeh To Naagin Roop Mein Aa Hi Nahi Rahi"

The been stops on a note that does not resolve, and Maa Sapela's man comes into the hall in the chandelier's light with it in his hands, and Parmeet looks at his daughter-in-law.

Ananta stands where she stood. Her sari is on the floor. Her hands are loose. Her chin is up. Nothing about her has changed except the breathing.

The kechuli lies on the floor by the bride's chair where it came off her.

A pen lies open on the register by the fire with its cap off.

"**Yeh to naagin roop mein aa hi nahi rahi.**"

The man plays again, four bars, and the room watches a woman stand still through it.

"Aayegi." Parmeet says it with his eyes on her. "Meri been ki dhun ko sun kar bardasht nahi kar payegi, aur apne naagin roop mein aayegi."

Parmeet takes one step towards her.

"**Maan ja, ichchhadhari naagin.** Aur naagmani humein de de."

Ananta does not move.

"Naagmani ko nahi." His voice drops. "Apne aap ko bacha. De de."

Ananta looks at him, and then past him, at her sister, and she says nothing at all.

## "Naagrani Se Door Hat"

The hall at five in the afternoon, with the chandelier on and the canvas beating at the windows.

The service door comes off its hinge.

The service door's hinge has torn the frame, and the splinters are on the floor.

It comes off because a woman puts her shoulder into it and walks through, and she is old, and her sari is dark, and she is across the floor before the two men nearest the door have decided to move.

"**Naagrani se door hat.**"

"Kaun hai yeh?" Parmeet turns round. "Kahan se aayi yeh? Pakdo ise."

The old woman's hand comes up and the fire goes out.

The hall is dark and then it is not: the two lamps at the far end come on with a sound, and the room stands where it stood, and the men have not moved, and the woman is between the fire and the bride.

"**Yeh kaun hai?**" Ravish's voice, at the back.

"**Is desh ki khasiyat.**" The man with the been says it, and he does not have to think about it. "**Ichchhadhari naagin.**"

## The Order

The hall at five in the afternoon, with the chandelier on and the floor cleared back to the pillars.

"**Reed tod do is naagin ki.**"

Parmeet's voice is not loud and the whole hall hears it, because he has said the sentence once already today.

A register lies open on the table by the fire and the pen on it has rolled to the edge.

Then, more slowly, the way a man says a thing he wants written down:

"**Aur uske baad peetal ke khanjar se iske seene pe waar karo — aur ye naagin mar jayegi.**"

The old woman's hands are up.

"**Jab tak meri been bachti rahegi, ye naagin nahi bachegi.**"

Somebody puts the brass knife into the hand of the man nearest the fire. The knife is old and it has no edge on it. An edge is not what it is for.

Then the hall moves, all at once.

The old woman is not where the knife is meant to go. The bride is standing in front of her, and the bride does not move out of the way, and what happens in the next twenty seconds happens to a woman in a wedding sari.

## The Strike

Purvi is held by two men at the wall in the chandelier's light and she does not see it. What she hears is a sound she will be able to describe for the rest of her life and never does: a short, flat thing, and a long one after it.

Her mother is on the floor with her hands over her head.

The reed is on the floor in two pieces, and one of them has gone under the chairs.

The lamps go out again.

The brass knife stands on the tray at the side of the hall with nothing else on the tray.

When they come back on, the old woman is down on one knee by the fire, and the bride is on her side on the floor with her sari under her and one arm out, and the brass knife is standing in the ash at the edge of the fire with the handle up.

"Ananta!" Purvi's voice comes out of her at a pitch that is not her own. "Didi—"

"Chhodo ise." Parmeet says it to nobody in particular, and the men let go of Purvi's arms. "Iske paas kuch nahi hai. Ise utha lo."

## Twenty Minutes

The inspector is at the door in the afternoon light with his cap in his hand and his car running in the drive.

"Sir." His constable is at his shoulder. "**Bees minute mein police ki doosri van aa jayegi.** Phir main kuch cover-up nahi kar paunga. Jo bhi karna hai, jaldi kijiye."

"Lekin mujhe abhi tak naagmani nahi mili hai." Parmeet's voice comes from behind him, from the passage, where he has been standing for a while.

"**Bhai sahab, naagmani to hum baad mein bhi le lenge.**" The constable says it to both of them. "**Lekin usse pehle inka marna zaroori hai. Agar ye zinda rahega na, hum apna kaam kabhi poora nahi kar payenge.**"

The inspector looks at his watch, and then at the hall, and then at the drive.

A pen lies open on the register at the door and the ink on the nib has dried.

The inspector's cap has a badge on it with the pin lost, and it sits crooked on him.

"Chal." He puts his cap on. "Kya koi dekh raha hai?"

"Koi nahi."

The car goes out through the gate.

The drive has the car's dust all over the flagstones and the gate is open wide, and the gate's register lies open on its table with the pen on its string.

## Ananta Sweet

Ravish comes round the fire in the hall and stands over his bride with his hands in his pockets.

"**Ananta sweet.**" He crouches down. "Kahan jaa rahi ho tum?"

Her eyes find him. It costs her something to do it.

A tray stands at the side of the hall with the sweet boxes on it still tied.

The chairs by the wall are pushed out of line and one of them is turned to face the wall.

"Mere bachche ki maa banne wali thi na?" He smiles. "Hum kitna pareshaan kiya. Mujhe mere dad ne." He looks over his own shoulder at his father. "Lekin ab afsos — tumse chhutkara mil jayega. Finally."

"Ravish." Ananta's voice is a thread.

"Seedhi si baat hai." He puts his hand out. "**Naagmani mujhe de do, warna maari jaaogi.** Hum suna nahi tumne?"

Ravish waits.

A glass of water stands on the floor by the bride's chair with a straw in it, untasted.

"I said, naagmani de."

## What She Says Instead

Purvi is on the floor beside her sister in the chandelier's light, with her sister's head in her lap and the two men have stopped holding her, because there is no longer any reason to.

"Didi." She is wiping blood off a face with her own dupatta, and she is not doing it well. "Didi, phir se utho. Didi—"

Ananta's hand comes up and finds her wrist.

The kechuli and the torn sari are on the floor within reach of her hand, and nobody has taken them up.

"Papa ne kaha tha." Her voice is very quiet and very clear. "Tu naagin hai. Ichchha nahi — naagin."

"Didi, chup karo—"

"**Tujhe chuna gaya hai. Tu hai. Isliye lagta [hai] main nahi.**" Ananta's eyes do not close. "Papa ne kaha tha, mujhe tujhe bachana hai."

"Didi."

"**Mujhe maaf karna.**" Her hand comes down off the wrist. "**Main tujhe bacha nahi payi.**"

"Nahin nahin." Purvi's hands are on her sister's face and her sister's face has gone the way faces go. "Didi, didi, phir se utho. **Didi, phir se utho.**"

The hall is quiet. Somebody has put the fire out and nobody has lit it.

The tray of water stands by the wall with the glasses still full.

## Neeche

Purvi comes down the drive at a run in the afternoon light, with her dupatta gone and her hands red, and the inspector's car is halfway out of the gate, and she puts both her hands flat on its bonnet.

"Sir." She is out of breath and she does not stop. "Sir, rukiye. **Bacha— bas bacha hua sir.**"

"Madam—"

"**Woh neeche kya aapke ghar waale mere ghar waalon ko maar rahe hain?**" She says it to the windscreen. "Jaan-jaan se. Woh phool se khoon mein… meri mummy. Papa. **Please. Please rok lo. Bacha lo, sir.**"

The window comes down four inches.

The gate's book lies open on its string with the day's page wet at the corner, and the last line on it is in Shyam's hand — *chhote sahab, gaadi, gyarah baje* — and there is no line under it.

The gate's boom is up and the dust hangs in the afternoon light behind the car.

A file lies on the car's seat with the window down on it.

"**Kaun maara hai?**"

"Woh log."

"Kya bol rahi ho tum?" The inspector's face does not change. "Arre, Purvi. Purvi, tum woh dekho—"

"Sir, **ye is desh ke nahi hain.**" Her hands are still on the bonnet. "Par aap unmein se nahi ho. Please. Please bata dijiye. Meri family ko zaroor mujhse maar denge. Woh jaan se maar denge. Zaroor, sir. Please."

The window goes back up.

Her sandal has come off on the drive and lies where it came off.

A tray stands on the gate's table with the visitor cards on it and the pen beside them.

The car goes out through the gate and turns left, and its light goes down the lane and is gone, and behind her the tent wall blows in the wind once and settles.

## Upstairs

The inspector's car is out of the gate in the afternoon light, and Purvi goes back up the steps and through the front room and into the passage where the phone is, and her hands are shaking so much that she gets the receiver off the stand and has to hold it with both of them.

There is no dial tone.

The phone's cradle is still rocking on the stand, and the cord has been pulled out straight.

At that hour the man she is trying to call is on a kerb outside a sweet shop in Ghaziabad with three sleeping children in the car behind him, telling a boy at his own assignment desk to pull a tape.

The passage light is off. The light from the hall comes as far as the first step of the stairs and stops.

Then Vihaan is standing at the top of the stairs.

"Tum." Purvi puts the receiver down on the stand and it misses and hangs by its cord, swinging. "Tum aa gaye. Tum—"

Vihaan comes down the stairs slowly, in his shirtsleeves, with his hair dry and his cuffs buttoned.

"Suno." He takes her by both arms. "Suno."

His hands are cold through her sleeves, and she does not notice it.

"Main kisi ko kuch nahi hone dunga." He says it evenly, in the flat way a man says a thing he has said before. "Especially tumhein. Trust me."

"Woh log phool le kar aaye the—"

"Trust me." His eyes do not go past her face at all. "Main kisi ko kuch nahi hone dunga."

And then Vihaan laughs.

It is not a big laugh. It comes out of him at the end of the sentence and it goes on a little longer than a laugh should, and it is one note lower than the man she knows.

"Tumhein kya laga?" He lets go of her arms and steps back on the step. "Ki main kuch jaanta hoon?"

"Kya—"

"Main London ka hisaab dekhta hoon." He puts his hands in his pockets. "Tumhare ghar ka nahin. Is ghar ka nahin. Aur tumne mujhe kis liye bula liya?"

"Kyunki tumne kaha tha." Her voice is very small in that passage. "Kal raat. Lobby mein. Naam list mein likhwao."

"Koi list nahin hai." He smiles. "Koi Manoj nahin hai. Koi aath baje nahin hai."

The receiver swings on its cord and touches the wall and swings back.

"Tumhare ghar waale mar rahe hain"—he says it lightly, and he is looking at her face while he says it—"aur tum apne premi ko dhoondh rahi ho."

Her hand goes out and hits him in the chest, and he takes half a step back and puts his own hand where she hit him.

"Aanh." He straightens his collar. "Zor se lag gayi. Dard ho raha hai."

Her fist has something in it when it comes away. One button, off the placket of his shirt, and the thread on it is not a shirt's thread, and she closes her hand on it.

Then Vihaan takes her by the shoulder and turns her, and the knife is in his other hand, and it goes into her back just to the left of her spine, and it is quick enough that she does not shout.

"Tumne insaan chun liya." He says it with his mouth at her ear. "Woh tumhara nahin tha kabhi."

Purvi goes down onto the third step with her hands in front of her and her chin on the wood.

There is a hand flat on the side of her head, holding it up so that her mouth is not in the dust. It is warm and it does not let go until her breathing has come back to something that will keep going.

The passage light goes on.

There is nobody on the stairs. The receiver is still swinging on its cord, and her blood is going down between the third step and the second in a thin line and stopping at the carpet where the carpet starts.

Purvi is still awake. Purvi stays awake for a long time.

## The Newsroom

Two in the afternoon, and the car is standing under the shade of a peepal outside a sweet shop on the Ghaziabad road with three children asleep in it and one awake and asking for a cold drink.

Vihaan is on the kerb with his elbows on his knees and the phone at his ear.

"Rukh, rukh, rukh." He puts a hand over his other ear. "Yeh bulletin kaun likh raha hai?"

"Sir, woh subah se ready hai." The boy at the other end is at the assignment desk of his own channel, and he is twenty-three, and he sounds pleased. "Aapke ghar ka case hai na. Do dulhan, jewellery, aur poora parivar thaga hua. Do bajey ki khabar mein chala."

"Do bajey?" Vihaan looks at the road. "Kitni baar chala?"

"Sir, ek baar two o'clock, aur repeat teen bajey."

"Repeat rok do."

"Sir—"

"Roko." He says it with no heat at all and the boy stops talking. "Aur teen bajey ka tape mere paas rakho. Kisi ko mat dena. Samjhe?"

"Ji, sir. Sir, ek baat—"

"Kya?"

"Yeh story hamare paas aayi kahan se, woh poochhne pe"—the boy's voice goes down—"Parmeet sir ka office. Bola ki unki taraf se hai."

Vihaan sits on the kerb with the phone at his ear and does not say anything for a moment.

"Sir?"

"Tape sambhal ke rakho." He stands up. "Aur mujhe do minute mein assignment desk ka number message kar do. Main khud baat karunga."

"Ji, sir."

Vihaan puts the phone in his pocket, and then takes it out again and calls a man in Bombay.

"Mr Kessler. Vihaan Suri." He puts a hand over his other ear. "Main jaanta hoon aap nikal gaye. Main aaj subah aapke conference room mein nahin tha, aur wajah main aapko khud bata raha hoon, kyunki woh aapko shaam tak kisi aur se sunni hogi." He listens. "Ghar mein police aayi thi. Meri family ke khilaaf warrant tha. Yeh khabar aapke desh ke akhbaar mein kal subah hogi."

The children watch him through the glass.

"Isliye main aapko woh de raha hoon jo aapko chahiye." His voice does not change. "Mahamahakumbh ka jo feed mera channel dega, woh feed do hafte pehle aapki platform pe jaayega, aur sirf aapki platform pe. Aapko content chahiye, mujhe signature. Aaj shaam chhe baje, hotel wahin." A pause. "Nahin. Main aapse kuch chhupa nahin raha. Main aapse theek woh baat kar raha hoon jo aapko kal akhbaar mein milegi, aur usse pehle."

Vihaan puts the phone away and stands a moment with his hand on the roof of the car.

"Chhote bhaiya." The awake child has the window down. "Cold drink?"

"Ek minute." He opens the door. "Pehle ek zaroori kaam."

Vihaan takes his phone out again and calls his own house, and it rings four times and goes to nothing. Vihaan calls Shyam's phone, and it rings and rings. Vihaan calls Shyam's phone again.

On the third ring somebody picks it up, and it is not Shyam, and the line is bad, and whoever it is does not speak.

"Hello? Shyam?" Vihaan's hand is flat on the roof of the car. "Hello. Kaun hai?"

The line goes dead.

Vihaan stands in the road with the phone in his hand for a long moment. Then Vihaan opens the door and gets in and starts the car, and pulls out into the road the wrong way for the turn, and a scooter honks and Vihaan does not notice.

## The Final Plan

By three the hall has been swept and washed and the windows are open on the afternoon light, and the two rows of chairs are standing in it the way they stand at the end of a wedding.

"**Yeh saari laashein dafna do.**" Parmeet is at the sideboard with his spectacles on. "**Koi saboot nahi milna chahiye.**"

"Tauji." Karan is at the table with the register open. "Purvi?"

"Baad mein." Parmeet does not look up. "Pehle mitti."

"Ji."

"**Ab waqt aa gaya hai humare final plan ko anjaam dene ka.**"

"Kaun sa final plan, Tauji?" Karan has his feet up. "Wohi Kumbh wala?"

"Wohi."

"**Saara RDX, ek-ek hathiyaar — saara Kumbh pahunchega.**" Parmeet takes his spectacles off. "Pichhle das-pandrah saalon se India ne Kumbh ke infrastructure par bahut kharch kiya hai. Aur is baar ka Kumbh to **Mahamahakumbh** hai."

The register goes on being written in.

The chairs are in two rows with the aisle down the middle and the marigold on the posts going brown.

The tent's canvas is down at the west side and the poles stand in the grass.

"Yeh mauka nahi chukna chahiye. Is baar is desh ki tabahi pakki hai." He looks round his family. "Mahamahakumbh mein is saal duniya bhar ke CEOs, global investors aur bade-bade political leaders pahunch rahe hain. Isliye security ko poore high alert pe rakha gaya hai."

"Toh phir?" Karan's pen stops.

"**Kal shahi snan ke din, tum ke beechon-beech, RDX ka dhamaka hoga.**" Parmeet says it the way he says a date. "**Laakhon laashein bik jayengi.** Aur ek saath itni mauten is desh ki kamar todne ke liye kaafi hongi. Phir koi bhi videshi palat kar yahan nahi aayega."

Parmeet sits down.

A bucket stands at the side of the hall with a cloth over the top of it.

The electrician's ladder is against the wall with its cable still coiled on the rung.

"**Yahi plan banaya tha humne chauvees saal pehle.**"

"Kaise jaayega?" Karan's pen stops. "Pichhle saal sangam pe ek truck bhi nahin ghusi bina pass ke."

"Teen OB vans." Parmeet puts his finger on the register. "Channel ki teen gaadiyan, pontoon pe parking pass ke saath, chaubees ghante ke liye. Aur ek generator van jo kabhi nahin chalti."

The fan turns over the table.

The register for the evening stands open on the table by the fire with the pen beside it.

"Pass pe signature kiska hai?" Karan's voice is careful.

"Chhote sahab ka." Parmeet takes his spectacles off and polishes them on his sleeve. "Woh Kumbh ka feed London bech raha hai. Uske pass pe teen gaadiyan hain, aur ek generator jo kabhi nahin chalti. Woh apne hisaab mein woh teen gaadiyan dekhega, aur hisaab mein jhooth nahin likha hoga."

"Aur agar woh gaadi khol ke dekhe?" Ravish sets his glass down. "**Ye tabahi aap unhi hathiyaaron aur RDX se karoge na jo humein office mein mile the?**"

"Haan."

"**Lekin Tauji, agar hum pakde gaye to?**"

Parmeet looks at Karan for a moment and then he smiles.

The pandit's things are on a tray by the fire, and the brass of it has been polished.

"Kumbh ki security ko halke mein nahi le sakte. Kumbh bahut bade level pe hota hai. Isliye India security high rakhti hai, aur wahan pe koi gadbad nahi ho sakti." He puts his spectacles back on. "**Toh phir uske liye mere paas plan bhi hai.**"

## The Dragon

The small room at the back of the Suri house at four in the afternoon, with the table cleared and one lamp on it.

Parmeet sends for the file and it comes, and it is not a file, it is a folder of plates and a single sheet, and he puts the sheet down on the table with the register.

"Yeh dragon sadiyon pehle darbaar mein charchayein hoti thi."

Ravish puts his feet down.

The folder of plates lies open on the table with one plate face down.

"Kehte the ye dragon naagon jaisa hi hota hai." Parmeet's finger goes along the top line of the sheet. "Lekin usse laakhon guna shaktishali. Aur tez."

"Dad—"

"Naagon ko devtaon ka vardaan mila tha." He does not look up. "Lekin dragon ko nahi. Padosi desh ki dharti par ye paida hue, panpe, aur nafrat ki aag mein jalte hue yahi sochte rahe ki naagon ko vardaan kyun mila aur inhein kyun nahi."

The men in the room have stopped moving.

The lamp on the table has its shade crooked and nobody has straightened it.

"Apne munh se aag ugalta. Aur apni poori duniya mein raaj karna chahta tha." He turns the sheet over. "Lekin dheere-dheere inka ant ho gaya."

"Toh phir—"

"**Magar humein apne mission ke liye dragon chahiye the.**" Parmeet looks up. "Iski vinashakari shaktiyan chahiye thi."

"Tauji, woh mar gaya—"

"**Isliye humne iski gene badal kar ise phir se paida kar diya.**" He puts his hand flat on the sheet. "**Humara dragon.** Jo is Bharat desh par kahar banke tootega."

The lamps over the table do not move. Outside, in the drive, a man is hosing the gravel down.

A tin of the powder the plates are kept in stands open at his elbow.

## What Will Not Wake

The house at dusk, with the lights coming on in the front rooms one after another.

It is not in the house.

The bed in the small room at the back is turned down and nobody has been in it, and a bottle of water stands on the stool beside it.

It is in the hall of stone, where the lamps are lit and the cloth is off the seat, and where a woman's body lies on a slab with her sari arranged and her hands folded and her face washed, and where the two of them stand over her and cannot make themselves stop.

"**Aapko kuch nahi ho sakta.**" Uttara's voice is going. "**Aaj aap apne poorn naagin avatar mein aane wali thi. Aapko aapki saari shaktiyan milne wali thi. Aapka is tarah se ant nahi ho sakta. Aapko kuch nahi ho sakta.**"

The younger one puts her hand out and touches the dead woman's hair and takes her hand back.

"Ma." Bharani's voice is very small. "Ma, woh—"

"Chup."

Uttara looks at her.

"Hum is dragon ko attack karne ke liye bhej rahe hain, jo khud jaag nahi raha hai."

"Woh nahi jagega." Uttara says it to the body. "**Jab tak ichchhadhari naagin ki [garn] nahi aayegi, woh nahi jagega.**"

The hall of stone is quiet. The lamps stand.

The lamps do not answer her, and neither does the woman on the slab, and outside the storm that has been over the city since the morning goes on being a storm.

## The Hall at Night

Half past nine, and the last car comes up the drive in the porch light with four sleeping children in it and one man who has been on the Ring Road since four.

The gate is open and the white police car is not there.

The chairs are stacked against the wall and the marigold on the posts has gone brown.

Vihaan stops the car where the wedding cars stood, and sits with the engine off and his hands on the wheel and looks at the house through the windscreen. The hall is dark. The lawn is dark. There is one light on in the front room and one in the passage, and the marigold is still up on the poles.

The fire at the side of the hall has been lit and the room smells of it.

Vihaan gets out.

"Chhote sahab." Shyam comes across the gravel with his cap in his hands and he does not look at Vihaan. "Bhai sahab kahe the ki aap aate hi unse milne."

"Theek hai." Vihaan opens the back door and takes the smallest of the children up in his arms. The child's shoes come off and stay on the seat. "Shyam. Subah teen baje maine tumhe phone kiya tha."

"Ji, sahab."

"Teen baar." He walks towards the house. "Kisi ne uthaya aur kisi ne kuch bola nahi."

"Network ki dikkat thi, sahab." Shyam's voice is flat. "Aap andar chaliye."

In the hall the floor is still wet in one corner and the wet is a clean wet and the whole hall smells of phenyl.

Vihaan stands in the middle of it with a sleeping child on his shoulder.

"Aaj yahan kya hua?" Vihaan says it to the empty hall, and then, when Shyam does not answer, Vihaan says it again, without any force in it at all. "Yeh floor kyun dhoya hai?"

"Bhai sahab kahe the." Shyam's cap is in his hands. "Shaadi ke baad sab saaf."

"Sab saaf."

"Ji."

Vihaan walks to the passage where the phone is, and the receiver is hanging off its stand on its cord, and he puts it back, and it rings once in his hand and stops.

The light in the passage is on. At the third step the carpet starts, and where the carpet starts there is a dark place in the pile that has dried brown.

Vihaan stands and looks at it for a moment, and then he goes down on one knee and puts two fingers on it and looks at them.

"Shyam." He says it without turning round. "Yeh kya hai?"

"Sahab"—Shyam is at the end of the passage with his cap in his hands—"bhai sahab kahe the ki safai kal subah hogi."

"Bhabhi kahan hai?"

"Woh—" Shyam stops. "Woh dono chali gayin, sahab."

Vihaan looks at him for a moment.

"Kahan?"

"Bhai sahab batayenge." Shyam goes out to the car.

Vihaan carries the child up the stairs.

Vihaan puts the boy in his bed and takes his shoes off properly and puts the sheet over him, and then he stands in the corridor for a moment, and then he goes down again past the hall with the wet corner in it, past the passage, to the lift.

The lift is standing on 1 with its door open. The small hinged box in the wall beside it is hanging out on its hinge, and the key is in the lock, and the grey paint on the edge of the box has been broken off in a fresh line.

Vihaan takes his wallet out and takes a folded slip of paper out of it, and opens it, and looks at it under the corridor light. The fold has gone soft. His own hand, small and flat, from the accounts office at half past one in the morning: *chaar stop, paanch ka bill, poochho kyun.*

Vihaan asked why.

Vihaan turns the key. The fifth button comes up under the row of four.

The basement is empty.

Not stripped, not broken into: emptied. The crates are gone and the stains where they stood are clean. The map is off the wall and the nail is still in it. The clips are gone from the shelf. The straw is swept into one corner in a little pile.

Vihaan stands in the door of it with the corridor light behind him, and then he takes the camera out of its case.

Vihaan shoots the room the way he shoots a site: from the door, from the two corners, close on the nail, close on the marks where the crates stood, and the last one standing in the middle of the floor while the flash goes up the walls and puts his own shadow on all four of them.

The flash is the first thing that has happened in that room in a long time.

Vihaan looks at the small screen, one frame at a time, and takes the card out and puts it in his shirt pocket and puts a fresh one in.

"Chaar saal," he says to nobody, at last, in Hindi, quietly. "Chaar saal."

Vihaan turns the key back. The fifth button goes out. Vihaan closes the box and puts the key in his pocket, and the slip of paper goes into his shirt pocket and not back into the wallet.

In the passage he takes out his phone and writes to Verma, standing up, at that hour: *Lift ka contract, 1998 se aaj tak. Sab bill. Sab revision. Aur jo aadmi woh lift service karta hai, uska naam aur address. Kal subah nau baje file mere desk pe.*

Then he adds one more line, and this one takes him longer than the rest: *Aur kisi ko mat batana ki maine poochha.*

Upstairs in his own room he puts the card in the drawer with the folder that has *bina vivaran* written twice in its margin and the file with no name on it, and turns the key, and then he sits down and opens the laptop and answers Verma's eleven messages in four lines.

The last thing he writes, at one in the morning, goes to a man in Bombay: **Chhe baje nahin pahunch saka. Ghar mein kuch hua hai. Kal subah aath baje, main khud aata hoon.**
