The logotype comes up over thundercloud, with something red falling through the frame too slowly to be rain.

A man's voice asks two words in the pitch of a been: *"Kaun hai woh."* A choir of women answers underneath him and leaves the answer unfinished.

The been stops. One low string is struck and left hanging over the black.

A drum, once. Then the story, and no music under it until the story asks.


## The Door That Never Opens

The tenth floor at a quarter to eleven, and the building emptying from the top down. The bulletin is over. The studio is dark behind its glass. Somewhere below, a man is dragging a mop across marble and singing to himself.

Purvi comes along the corridor with the bag hooked on her elbow and her pass swinging.

"Mayuri?" She stops at the corner. "Mayuri?"

Mayuri is not in the corridor. She is not at the water cooler, and she is not at the board, and she is not in the pantry, where she said she would be — *yahin rukna, ek minute* — and where a cup stands on the counter with the tea gone cold in it and the spoon still in it and the chair pushed back.

The end of the corridor gives on to a service passage, and in the wall of that passage there is a lift, and the lift is old. Brushed steel. A plate with nothing written on it. No floor numbers. The doors shut.

The singing stops at the turn of the corridor, and the man with the mop comes round it with his bucket, and he is old, and he is in a blue apron, and he does not look up from the floor.

"Yeh lift ka darwaza toh kabhi khulta nahin." He says it without stopping, in the flat way of a man saying the time. "Aur yeh darwaza toh basement ki taraf jaata hai."

She turns round.

"Kabhi matlab?"

"Kabhi." The mop goes past her feet and comes back. "Baaki teen lift hain. Woh chalti hain."

"Ek ladki thi. Mayuri. Yahan khadi thi."

"Main ne kisi ko nahin dekha." He wrings the mop out over the bucket. "Aur aap bhi ismein mat jaana." The mop goes on down the passage. "Woh apne aap band ho jaati hai."

"What?"

The bucket handle rings once, and the man is round the corner, and the passage is empty, and a long way off his singing starts again.

Purvi stands in front of the shut doors.

"Yeh Mayuri kahan chali gayi?" She says it to the steel. "Ab main usey kaise bataun ki meri Alim Ansar se baat nahin ho paayi. Par main unke ghar jaakar zaroor baat karungi."

The lift doors open.

There is nobody inside it. The light in it goes orange and then white. The floor of it is warm, and the warmth comes out into the passage and touches her shins.

Purvi looks down the corridor. She looks at the time on her phone.

Then she steps in.

The doors close on her without a sound. There are eleven buttons on the panel and no numbers under them, only letters in a script that does not sit straight, and there is a keyhole where the emergency bell should be. She presses the bottom button. Nothing.

"Yeh neeche jaane ka kaun sa button hai?"

She presses three more, and then the four under them, and the panel clicks and does nothing at all.

The air in the cabin comes up warm from the floor and it smells of iron and of something else that has no name in a lift, and the light goes down the wall in a slow band.

"O kya aafat hai."

The lift goes down.

The voice comes up out of the floor of the lift, and out of the shaft below it, and it comes in Hindi, and it comes from a long way under the building.

"Karodon saalon ki dushmani ke baad bhi ek dragon ek naagin ko door se sungh leta hai. Naagin ki sunahri khushbu agar kabhi bhi is dragon ke aas-paas bhi aa jaaye, toh uske andar ki aag aur gussa hamesha ke liye badha deta hai."

The letters on the panel are lit from behind. She puts her face close to them and reads one, and it is not a letter she knows, and the others change places when she looks at them again.

The lift stops. The doors do not open.

"Dum dum dum dum." Purvi presses the buttons with the flat of her hand. "O yahan bas system hi kuch alag tha. Kuch samajh nahin aaya. God, kyunki kya hai yeh?"

Under her feet the floor hums and then knocks, once, hard, and the whole cabin shifts an inch and comes back.

"Oh God, yeh kya ho raha hai kahin?" Her hands go flat on the wall. "Kahin mere ko galat button toh nahin laga diya?"

The voice comes again, and this time it is not patient.

"Naagin ki sunahri khushbu agar kabhi bhi is dragon ke aas-paas bhi aa jaaye, toh uske andar ki aag aur gussa hamesha ke liye badha deta hai."

Something hits the other side of the shaft, low down, far below, and the cabin rings.

The doors open.

The guard of that floor stands in the passage with his cap in his hand and his radio going, and behind him the passage is full of steam from the pantry, and the steam moves the whole length of it and does not settle.

"Arre, us lift mein kahan ja rahi ho?" He puts his hand out. "Bahar aao. Ab woh pata nahin hai. Ye sirf maalikon ke liye hai." He reaches past her and takes the panel and turns a key in it. "Waise bhi aaj lift ke maintenance ka kaam chaalu hai. Jao. Sorry."

She comes out. The doors go shut behind her and the light of them goes out.

"Mayuri—" She turns on the guard. "Ek ladki thi. Mayuri. Yahan khadi thi."

"Koi nahin tha."

"Main usey dhoondh rahi hoon."

"Madam." The guard puts his cap on. "Neeche jaaiye. Aaj yahan koi nahin rukta."

"Yeh Mayuri kahan gayi?" She says it to the shut doors, and then to the passage, and then to the guard's back. "Yahin toh rukne ko kaha tha maine. Sirf yahin."

The steam goes along the passage and takes the shape of the wall and lets it go.

## The First Time Here

The Suri house at nine the next evening, and the lane outside full of cars with their headlamps on and their drivers standing in groups at the gate, and the gate lit with a string of bulbs and a man on a ladder taking one of them down.

The hall is a wedding. The mandap stands against the far wall with its poles in white and marigold on the crossbar, and the floor is covered, and there are three hundred chairs now and men going between them with glass jugs. The score comes up over the crowd and goes under it again. Perfume and ghee and diesel from the generator at the side of the house.

Under the mandap at the front, Karan stands with a garland round his neck and a girl in pink beside him, and the photographer is at the step with his flash going off and the flash lighting the marigold from underneath. The aunt goes down the middle of the hall with her hands up, arranging people who are already arranged. Pinky stands at the table by the door with a list and a pen and three men waiting on her pen.

The aunt has a young man by the elbow now, and she is not asking him for anything. A boy goes past with a tray of garlands and she takes one off it while she is still talking, and she does not let go of the young man's arm to do it, and he stands still and lets her. Somebody behind them says *chhote sahab* and he turns his head.

Then the wind comes through the side door and takes the corner of the cloth off the table, and the hall turns round.

Purvi comes in through that door with her invitation card in one hand and her bag in the other, in the same kurta she wore to work, and her pallu over her arm, and the bulbs in the chandelier stand up in their fittings and come down again, and she stops.

The hall does not turn round. Three hundred people go on with the evening, and a girl carrying her own card between two pillars of that family is not a thing the hall looks at. At the pillar nearest the mandap the young man has stopped with his hand in the air and nothing in it, because the boy with the tray has gone on without him.

"Pehli baar aa rahi hoon yahan pe." She says it to the hall. "Kuch samajh nahin aa raha kahan jaana hai."

Purvi has been in this house once before, in the room at the back of it, with a file against her chest that nobody in that room looked at, and nobody in this hall remembers it. This is the first time she comes in through the front of it, with a card, in the clothes she came to work in.

Two women stand at the pillar to the left of the mandap with their heads covered, and they have been standing there for some time, and they are watching her and not the mandap.

She goes to them. She folds her hands. Then she goes down and touches their feet, one and then the other, and stays down.

"Ye kya kar rahi hain aap?" The elder one pulls her shoulder up with both hands. "Mere pair chhod ke mujh pe paap mat chadhai, Maharani. Ji maa, mera matlab hai"— she looks round at the room and lowers her voice — "aap hamari Rani beti jaise lagti hain. Aur waise bhi hamare yahan na, betiyon ko pair nahin chhune dete."

"Oh achha." Purvi straightens with her dupatta in her hand. "Waise actually pata nahin kya ho raha hai. Mujhe lagta hai mera na chashme ka number badh gaya hai." She takes her spectacles off and puts them on again. "Kuch bhi saaf nahin dikh raha. Kuch baat hai."

The women look at each other.

"Achha pata nahin kyun." Purvi looks at them properly for the first time. "Aap dono familiar lag rahi hain. Hum mil chuke hain kahin?"

"No." The younger one says it at once. "Hum pehli baar mil rahe hain."

"Haan." The elder one's hands come together. "Pehli baar."

"Pata nahin kyun." Purvi unfolds the card. "Aap mein se kisi ko pata hai ki Arya Mansoori kahan honge? Actually main unke office se aayi hoon. Mujhe kuch kaam tha."

The two of them step apart and open a way between themselves, and the elder one puts her hand out to the passage at the far side of the hall.

"Haan please aao." Her voice is low and it carries under the score. "Maharani, ab zyada der nahin hai. Jab aapki shaktiyan aap tak pahunchengi—" She stops. She looks at the girl's face and not at her glasses. "Par woh safar aasaan nahin hoga. Isiliye hum parchhai ki tarah aapke saath chal rahe hain."

"Sorry?"

"Maharani." The elder one's hands come together at her chest and stay there. "Hum aapke ghar se nahin hain. Hum aapke log hain."

"Kya?"

"Please aao." The younger one is already going. "Is taraf se."

Purvi follows them three steps and then stops, because a woman with a tray comes between them, and when the tray has gone the passage is empty and the two women are not in it and not in the hall.

At the top of the hall, on the landing where the stairs turn, Parmeet stands with a glass in his hand and one arm along the rail. He is not watching the mandap.

## Such a Big House

The passage behind the hall at half past nine, under its one bulb, and the kitchen end of it loud with the caterers, and the other end stacked with the chairs that are not being used yet and smell of the cloth they came wrapped in.

A stack of gilt chairs stands against the far wall with a cloth over the top of them.

A tray of empty glasses stands on the floor by the kitchen door with a fly working at one of them.

"Itne bade ghar mein main sir ko kaise dhoondhungi?" Purvi says it to the passage, and then to the wall, and then to nobody at all.

The caterers' radio is on somewhere behind the noise, and nobody is listening to it.

"Pehli baar?"

There is a young man at the end of the passage with his back against the wall and a glass of water in his hand that he is not drinking, and his jacket is folded over his arm, and his sleeves are turned back twice. He is the tallest thing in that passage and the stillest. The tray boys come round him without being told to and do not look at him twice, and the two men from the caterer's have already decided he is somebody and have no idea why. His collar has gone soft at the edge from a week of wear, and somebody else has polished his shoes.

There is a second glass of water on the arm of the chair beside him, full, and he has been holding it for a while. He does not explain it. There is nobody in that passage to hold it for except the one person in the house who does not know her way around it.

"Sir—" She stops. And then she does not stop, because it is him, and it has been him since half past one in the morning in the channel's lobby, when the crate of glasses went between them and he had nothing in his hands. "Aap."

"Main." Vihaan puts the glass down on the arm of a chair. "Aap aa gayin."

"Card aaya tha." She holds it up: the gold border, her name on the second line. "Aapne diya tha."

"Main ne diya tha." He looks at her, and then at her face, and he does not say anything for a moment, and then he says, "Naye chashme." It comes out of him before the rest of it does, and he is pleased with himself for noticing and embarrassed to be pleased, and it shows at the corner of his mouth and is gone.

"Didi ne banwa diye." She touches the frame. "Maine aaj pehni hain. Poora din inhe pehen ke—" She stops. "Aaj subah se poori duniya mujhe dekh rahi hai. Ajeeb lag raha hai."

She looks at him over the new glasses, the way she looked at him in the lobby over the old ones with the hinge gone.

He does not answer that.

"Aryaman sir kahan honge?" She asks it the way she asks it at her desk, and then hears herself asking it in his house, and her hand comes up. "Sorry. Aapke ghar mein—"

"Baithak mein." He puts a thumb over his shoulder. "Ya baramde pe. Woh aaj pi rahe hain."

"Toh main unse baat kar loongi."

"Woh aaj pi rahe hain," he says again, and this time it is slower.

"Sir, mujhe unse zaroori baat karni hai." She says it to the card. "Kal subah late ho jaayega."

Vihaan looks at the passage for a moment, and then at her.

"Aap andar aaiye." He picks his jacket up off his arm. "Main aapko dikhata hoon kaun kaun hai. Baaki aap khud samajh jaayengi."

"Main yahi pehni hui hoon." She says it suddenly, and lifts one shoulder. "Aapne kaha tha. Jo pehna hai woh theek hai. Aap court nahin jaa rahi hain."

"Woh theek tha." He is looking at the end of the passage and not at her, and his thumb is going along the edge of the glass he has put down, back and forth, and it is the only part of him that is not still. "Aur aaj bhi theek hai."

"Vihaan!" Pinky's voice comes down the passage from the hall, and there is nothing in it that is asking. "Vihaan, kahan ho? Tauji bula rahe hain. Mandap pe jaana hai."

"Main aaya." He does not raise his voice. He goes, and at the turn of the passage he stops, and looks back once at her standing there in the light of the kitchen door with her card in her hand.

Then the hall takes him, and the marigold comes off the tray of a boy going past and lies on the marble where he stood.

## Water

The hall at ten, with the chandelier down and the lamps burning on the pillars, and the score up, and the engagement a thing that has happened and is now being celebrated by three hundred people who have not stopped talking.

Purvi stands at the edge of the floor with her card in her hand and her bag strap wound twice round her wrist.

"Waiter bhaiya." She catches one by the elbow. "Zara paani dena please."

The boy looks at her. He shakes his head.

"Mundi kya hila rahe ho?" She lets go. "De do na. Paani toh maanga hai." She puts her hand up to her own face. "Itna kya ho gaya hai mere chashma ko?"

The crowd at the middle of the hall opens along a line, and Aryaman comes down that line with his jacket open and his tie gone and a glass in his hand, and the men on either side of the line put their hands out to him and he takes four of the hands and not the fifth.

Aryaman stops at the boy with the tray and takes a glass off it and drinks it where he stands.

Then he sees her at the edge of the floor, with her card in her hand and her strap wound twice round her wrist, and he comes over.

"Water."

Aryaman holds his own glass out.

"Aa, thank you." She takes it and drinks half of it standing up.

"Saath mein thoda zeher bhi doon?" He lifts his own glass.

"No, no, sir." She puts the water down. And then she stops, and her hand comes up, and she looks at him. "Sir— sorry. Woh, woh aap mujhe dikhe kaise?"

"I beg your pardon?"

"Sorry sir. Mera matlab—" She takes her spectacles out of her bag and holds them up, and looks at them, and looks back at him. "Sir, aap mujhe dikh rahe ho. Poore ke poore. Bina chashme ke." She puts them down on her nose and takes them off again. "Aapki aankhen theek theek ho gayi hain."

"Good." Aryaman drinks. "Then look properly."

"Sir, main—" She puts the spectacles back in her bag because her hands have nothing else to do with them. "Main theek hoon. Main bilkul theek hoon."

"You are shaking."

"Woh—" She looks at her own hand. "Woh thand hai, sir. Bahar hawa hai."

Across the floor, at one of the pillars with a tray of glasses going past them, the two women stand with their heads together and their lips moving, and only the nearest of the three hundred would hear it.

"Dekha? Yani ab woh naagin ban rahi hai. Ye toh shuruaat hai, kyunki humne unki kechuli ko singhasan par virajit kar diya hai. Toh jab tak woh apne poorn swaroop mein nahin aa jaati, shaktiyan aati jaati rahengi."

The score comes up under the hall and takes the words away.

"Sir—" Purvi puts the water glass on the tray of a boy going past. "Sorry. Matlam main— nashe mein nahin hoon, sir."

"No." Aryaman looks at his own glass.

"Papa ne kal ek tonic diya tha."

"Phir toh zaroor woh Papa ka shot raha hoga."

"Nahin sir, sorry." She is walking beside him. "Main sachchi mein nashe mein nahin hoon. By God. Agar main nashe mein hoti, toh kya main aapko yeh baat batane aati ki mujhe aapse kitni urgent baat karni hai?" She gets in front of him. "Aap baat chhodo. Main aapko dikhati hoon sir. Ye dekho."

The phone comes out. The screen comes up. The photograph is there: a body, and the mark on it, and the light of the hall on the glass of the phone.

Aryaman looks at it. He looks for two seconds.

"Kya dikhana hai?"

"Sir, yeh—" She holds it closer. "Yeh nishaan. Ye—"

"Done." He gives the phone back. "Pakki nashedi ho tum." And then he sees her face, and he does something with his own that is not quite a smile and is not quite anything. "Par actually ye buri baat nahin hai. Achhi baat hai. Tumhare liye bhi, aur tumhare aas-paas ke logon ke liye bhi."

"Sir, main sach mein nasha nahin karti." Her hands go out. "Bahut sorry."

There is a crash at her feet. The tray, the bottle, the glass, and the dark going out along the marble between three hundred pairs of shoes.

At the edge of the crowd a man in a dark shirt has come out three steps and stopped, and a woman's hand comes out of the crowd behind him and closes on his elbow and holds it, and the score does not stop at all.

At the table by the door Pinky looks up at him and her pen stops on the list.

"Aapki bottle toot gayi." She goes down on her knees with her dupatta in her hands. "Sorry, sir. Sorry. Sorry."

The shoes move away from the sound on both sides. Nobody helps. A boy runs with a cloth and goes down on his knees beside her and starts on the marble, and the score does not stop at all, and the photographer's flash goes off at the front of the hall for the couple still standing under the mandap in the same place, in the same pose, with their garlands straight.

"Sorry." Purvi is picking glass out of the wet with her fingers and putting it in her own dupatta. "Sorry. Sorry, sir."

Aryaman stands over her with his glass and watches her do it, and does not put his hand out.

"Utho." He says it quietly. "Yeh unka kaam hai."

At the edge of the crowd Vihaan takes Pinky's hand off his elbow and puts it back into her hand.

"Ruk." He says it to her without turning. "Main aaya."

He goes across the marble through three hundred people, and the floor gives him to her the way a floor gives way to a man who is not going to stop, and he comes up beside his brother and looks down at the girl on her knees in the broken glass.

"Aryaman." He says it quietly. "Yeh ghar ka kaam hai. Tum andar chalo."

Then he goes down on one knee himself — not on both, and not in front of the room, and it is not a courtesy, it is a man getting a thing off the floor — and he puts one hand under her elbow and the other flat on the wet marble beside her hand.

"Haath do." He says it flatly. "Glass hai. Utho."

"Sir, main—"

"Haath do, Purvi." It is the first time he says her name in that hall, and it comes out of him without any weight put on it.

Her hand comes up and closes round his wrist, and he lifts her off the marble and puts her on her feet, and her dupatta comes up with the glass in it and he takes the corners of it out of her fingers and gives it to the boy with the cloth.

Then he does not let go of her wrist for a moment longer than the lift needs.

Neither of them says anything about it. The score comes back up at the far end of the hall and goes on.

Purvi stands there with her wrist in his hand and her breath going, and something goes up her arm from where he is holding it — warmth, and then heat, and then a turn in her stomach that has nothing to do with the glass — and she looks up at him and her mouth opens and nothing comes out.

"Bahut garmi hai andar." She says it at last, and it is not what she means, and she knows it is not what she means.

"Haan." Vihaan lets go of her wrist and stands back. "Garmi hai."

Vihaan turns to Pinky at the table by the door. "Iski chot dekho. Aur glass ka hisaab subah mere naam pe."

Then he stands up out of the glass, and he does not take the cloth out of the boy's hands, and he looks at his brother across the hall.

"Glass saf karo." His voice carries to the front of the hall and no further and every head in the middle of it turns. "Aur isse aaj koi cheez uthane ke liye nahin kahega. Kisi ke liye bhi."

Aryaman's glass comes down and his arm goes behind his back.

Vihaan is already walking out, and the photographer's flash goes off at the couple under the mandap, and the score does not stop at all.

## Who Wants to Live Longer

The side of the hall afterwards, where the music is quieter and the kitchen door gives out its heat, and the two of them stand at the wall with the crowd behind them going on with its evening.

"Waise aapko bhi itni nahin peeni chahiye, sir." Purvi says it to the floor. "Sorry. Overstepping."

"Kyun?" Aryaman has one shoulder against the wall and his glass in his hand. "Kyun nahin peeni chahiye? Zyada jeene ke liye?" He lifts the glass to the room. "Kaun idiot zyada jeena chahta hai?"

"Main sir." She says it without looking up. "Main nahin marna chahti jaldi."

A tray of used glasses passes at the height of her elbow and goes on past.

Across the hall, at the side of the bar, Vihaan stands with a glass he is not drinking and his shoulder against the pillar, and he does not come over, and the two of them look at each other for the length of one sentence and then not at all.

Aryaman turns his head and looks at her for a while.

"Mera toh clear hai." He drinks. "Healthy khao piyo, khoob jiyo. Bachche toh chhodo, paravaton ko bhi marte hue dekho. Nahi?" He waits, and she does not answer. "Humm. Vriddhashram mein pade-pade hain. Hai na? Kyun? Yahi karte hain na bachche? Jab maa-baap buddhe ho jaate hain toh unhein old age home mein daal dete hain."

A boy goes past with a crate of bottles on his shoulder and says something to the man behind him and laughs.

"Aur kya kya dekhna hai budhape mein?" Aryaman's voice does not go up. "Akelapan. Bimariyan. Chhurriyan. Doston ki arthiyan. Tanga mein asthiyan." He turns the glass round. "Ye jo zindagi hai na, ye jawani mein ji jaati hai. Budhape mein bas saanse chalti hain. Insaan nahin."

"Sir—"

"Tum meri baat maano." He is not looking at her. "Jo bhi nasha kar rahi ho, karti raho. Chhodo mat. Tabhi is duniya ko jald se jald chhod paogi."

The hall goes on behind them. Somebody's child goes past under the tables.

"Duniya toh maine kab ki chhod di, sir." Purvi lifts her head. "Ye toh meri aatma bhatak rahi hai. Jo aapke liye duty kar rahi hai."

Aryaman looks at her.

"What?"

"Kuch nahin, sir." She straightens her dupatta. "Sorry. Main chai laati hoon. Aap chai peete nahin. Main— main kuch laati hoon."

Purvi goes, and the kitchen door takes her, and Aryaman stands at the wall with his glass and does not drink from it.

At the top of the hall, on the landing where the stairs turn, Parmeet has come out of the passage with two men and stands with his hand on the rail. He looks down at the room, and at the girl going in through the kitchen door with her dupatta over her arm, and he lifts one finger and says something to the man on his left.

The man goes down the stairs and out through the crowd towards the gate.

## The Song

The hall at one in the morning with the chandelier off and only the work lights on, and the hall being taken apart.

The chairs go down in stacks of six. The marigold is swept off the marble into a heap by the door, and the heap still smells of the morning it comes from. Somebody has turned the band's speakers off but nobody has moved them, and the biggest one stands in the middle of the floor on its stand, dead, with a garland over one corner of it.

The love theme comes up for the first time in this story.

It is one instrument at first — a single string, low, and under it the drone the titles use, which does not resolve. It is not a song yet. It is the thing a song is going to be.

She carries the tablecloths out to the store room in armfuls. The heavy crate, the one with the brass in it, went out of the hall a while ago on the caterers' trolley, and nobody in this house has asked Vihaan to move a thing tonight, and he is out on the marble at one in the morning with his hands empty for a reason the household would not believe. They pass each other four times on the same bit of marble and they do not speak, because it is one in the morning and there is nothing that needs saying, and because in this house the two of them speaking in an empty hall is the most dangerous thing either of them could do tonight.

On the third pass the top cloth slips off her armful, and he puts his hand flat on it to keep it where it is and lets go, and she does not slow down for it, and neither of them says a word.

Somewhere behind the hall a door goes and the work lights at the far end are switched off one row at a time.

The rain comes on the lawn at twenty past one. It comes all at once and it makes a noise on the tent.

She is out under it with her arms full, going the wrong way, towards the out-house. He comes off the verandah with a sheet of plastic the size of a door and holds it over her head and walks her in under it. The plastic drums on itself with the weight of the water, and neither of them says anything about the rain.

Her spectacles go. She takes them off with her wrist and holds them in her fist and her hair is flat to her head, and he is wet through one shoulder and does not move the sheet off himself to fix it.

They come into the store room. One bulb on a wire, and shelves to the roof.

The shelf she needs is above her head. She puts a stool under it. He puts his hand flat on the stool.

She climbs, and takes hold of his shoulder to do it, and the love theme finds her hand and the vocal comes in — a woman's voice, one line, in Hindi, and the words are not for the audience — and it goes out again the moment both her feet are back on the floor.

There is a plate on the shelf with two of the cook's cutlets on it, gone cold, meant for whoever is still working. They eat it standing up, one each, and he takes the smaller one.

At half past two the house is asleep. She is asleep sitting up in the verandah chair with her back against the pillar and her chair tipped against the wall. Her mouth is open and her spectacles are folded in her hand.

He stands there with a cup of tea gone cold in his hand and looks at her for four seconds longer than a man looks at a colleague, and then she moves, and he is looking at the lawn.

The rain has stopped. The garden lamp is doing what it does.

## The Owner's Lift
