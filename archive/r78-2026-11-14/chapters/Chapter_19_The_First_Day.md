## Kachcha Chaba Jayenge

The sheds at the river end of the plot at four in the morning, with one bulb over the gate and the water beyond it going from black to black. The river is low and the bank under the sheds is mud that holds a foot. A cycle stands against the wall with a sack over its seat. There is a bucket by the gate with the rope still on it, and the rope goes down into the dark and nobody pulls it up.

The crocodile man walks the bank with his hands behind his back and eleven men stand at the gate and watch him walk it. The men have been brought here from three different places in three different cars and none of them has been told why.

One of the eleven is an old man. He has been with this work for thirty years. He carries the torch because his eyes have gone and he knows the animals by their sound, and tonight he holds the torch on the water and says what he has said in this yard for thirty years.

"Bhai sahab, woh bachche nahin hain. Woh—"

"Kuch bhi karke humein Naagmani laani hogi." The crocodile man does not turn round. "Warna ye magarmach humein kachcha chaba jayenge."

The gate opens and the old man is walked through it by two of the others, and he does not shout, because he has been in this trade for thirty years and he knows what shouting is worth at the river. The bucket by the gate goes over on its side.

Two of the eleven came down in the same van as the torch, and the van has a pass in a plastic sleeve on its dashboard, and the pass is good for fifteen days and says nothing about crocodiles. Nobody at the gate is told what the paper says. The yard's month runs at six lakh, the cheque for it is signed on the channel's fourth floor where none of the eleven has ever been, and without the paper in that sleeve the eleven are standing on a riverbank at four in the morning with a bucket and a torch.

Behind the sheds, where the light does not reach, the water takes a long time to go quiet.

A man's phone rings at the gate and the man looks at it and does not answer it. The phone rings a second time and he holds it out to the crocodile man, and the crocodile man puts it to his ear.

"Bhai sahab, woh Arora waala mamla—"

"Baad mein."

Then, into another phone, with his voice changed all the way through: "Dad, Aryaman, mujhe tumse bahut zaroori baat karni hai. Jaldi aake milo."

The bulb over the gate is still on when the cars go, and the bucket is still on its side, and the mud at the bank has one set of marks going down into the water and none coming back.

## Beta, Ye Kahani Real Hai

The front room of the Suri house at six in the morning, the chandelier off, the light coming in flat and grey from the garden doors. The wedding is one day gone and the room still smells of it: marigold gone brown in the vases, a sweet crushed into the carpet where somebody walked on it, the wedding silver still on its sheet in the corner with one fold turned back. Two men are on their knees at the far end with a roll of carpet between them and a bucket of water gone cold beside the bucket that is warm. A tray on the side table holds four glasses and all four are empty, and the tray leans on one corner where a hand has come down hard on it.

Parmeet sits at the head of the table in his dressing gown with a glass of water in front of him and the morning's paper beside it, and the paper has not been opened. He is tired and he is frightened, and he is a man who has spent his life not showing either, so his face does nothing at all while his jaw works once under the skin.

The hiss comes out of the half of the room where the chandelier is off.

It is not long and it is not loud, and it comes from a place with nothing in it but the shut garden doors and the dark behind the sofa, and every head in that room turns to it. The two men at the far end stop with their hands in the carpet. The tea boy stands in the doorway with the cup still on the tray and does not come into the room. Aryaman's shoulder comes away from the doorframe and he looks, and there is nothing to look at, and the room knows it heard a hiss and says nothing about it.

"Net pe search kar, apne logon ko dauda." Parmeet puts the glass down without drinking from it. "Itna bada channel hai hamara."

"Dad, main aisi kahaniyon mein believe nahin karta."

"Beta, ye kahani real hai." Parmeet's hand comes flat on the table, and the tray of glasses knocks once against the wood. "Woh naagmani agar koi beemar chhu le to theek ho jata hai. Mara hua insaan bhi zinda ho jata hai."

Outside, all at once, the crows go up out of the garden — a whole tree of them, black into the grey, screaming over the wall and away down the lane. The two men on the carpet flinch where they kneel, and the tea boy's hand comes up in front of his face, and Parmeet does not turn his head. He does not turn his head because he has been waiting to hear it.

"Dad—" Aryaman says, and stops. He is angry, and under the anger he is a boy in his father's drawing room hearing crows at the wrong hour, and this house has always had a story about why. "Dad, yeh sab—"

The phone rings in Parmeet's pocket.

Parmeet's hand goes flat over the pocket and stays there and he does not take it out. The ring goes on against his dressing gown, muffled and patient, and the room hears it and the room is careful not to.

"Aur ye baat humein kaun bata raha hai?" Aryaman asks it with his eyes on his father's pocket rather than his father's face, and his own phone is face down in his other hand and has been face down all morning.

"Do log. Ek woh jo bolta hai, ek woh jo darta hai." The phone stops. Parmeet picks the paper up at last and opens it and does not read it. "Tum apna kaam karo. Naam chahiye — kis ke paas hai, kahan hai. Baaki main dekh loonga."

The phone rings again, and this time it does not stop, and Parmeet lets it ring through the room while he holds the paper open at a page he is not looking at, and the two men at the far end lift the carpet up between them and carry it out past him in silence.

On the drive outside, the last of the wedding's cars is being washed down with a hose and the water goes into the gutter and away. The washer is a boy of seventeen and he works with his back to the house. He works with his back to the house because the gate dog will not come up the drive this morning. It sits in the road with its ears down, looking at the garden doors, and it does not come when it is called, and does not come when it is shouted at.

The page of the paper lies open on the table after Parmeet has gone, and the wind is not moving anything else in that room.

## Attari

The tenth floor of the channel at nine in the morning, with the lights on over half the desks and the rest of the floor still dark, and the shutters up on the glass so that the corridor outside is brighter than the office.

Roma comes along the floor with a printout in one hand and a drive in the other, and she puts both down in front of Aryaman and stands with her knuckles on the desk.

"Aapne jo bola tha, sir. Log jo dawa karte hain ki unhone use dekha hai." She turns the printout round. "Attari border ke paas ek gaon. Ek factory jo band hai. Aur ye photo—"

The file on the screen loads and stops and loads again and shows a grey square.

"Yeh upload nahin ho rahi." Roma clicks it again. "Teen jagah se bheji hai. Sab jagah yahi."

Aryaman leans in. In the grey square there is a white place that could be a light on a wall, and there is nothing else at all.

"Kisne bheji?"

"Ek aadmi jo wahan kaam karta tha. Woh bhi ab nahin hai." Roma takes the drive back. "Sir, purani photo hai. Bais saal purani. Logon ke paas iska ek hi print hai aur woh print ek almari mein band hai."

The printer at the end of the floor wakes up and runs three sheets and stops in the middle of the third one. Nobody at the floor gets up to take them.

"Photo se pehle baat karo," Aryaman says. "Ye factory ka owner kaun hai? Ye mine kahan hai?"

"Ahana Dhawan." Roma says the name without looking at her notes. "Ye hai property ki maard."

At the desk behind them, on the other side of the partition where the tapes are kept, Vihaan sits with a headphone round his neck and his sleeves turned back and a printout of the same file in his hand. He landed this morning and has been at that desk since nine, and nobody in the building has asked him why. The printout in his hand has the same grey square on it, and he looks at it for a while, and then he puts it down on the desk and turns it over so that the square faces the wood.

"Us photo ka bhejne wala kaun tha?" Vihaan says it over the partition without getting up, and the printout in his hand goes down face-wood on his own desk. "Aur woh aadmi jo ab nahin hai — uska naam file mein kya hai?"

Roma turns her head towards the tapes. "Naam nahin hai, sir. Sirf likha hai ki wahan kaam karta tha."

"Toh naam ke alva sab kuch likha hai," Vihaan says. "Teen jagah se bheji, teen jagah se ruki. Kharabi ek jagah hoti hai, Roma ji. Teen jagah toh haath hota hai."

He gets up and brings his own printout across and lays it next to the grey square on Aryaman's desk, and the two of them look at the same white place in it for a second without either of them saying what it might be.

"Sir." Roma leans on the desk again. "Milne ka time maang loon?"

"Time nahin." Aryaman picks his jacket up. "Uska address."

## Dost

The canteen on the ground floor at eleven in the morning, with one tube light on over the counter and the shutters open on the road, and the tables wiped down and still wet. A boy at the counter is cutting a lemon into quarters and the knife is blunt and he does it slowly. There is a tin of biscuits on the counter with the lid off and one biscuit left in it.

Vikram sits at the corner table with a cup he is not drinking from and a small velvet box in front of him that he has opened and shut three times. He is in a good shirt and his hair is combed with water and his shoes have been polished that morning.

"Tu chhah mahine baad aaya." Vikram turns the box round with one finger. "Aur main tujhse pehle apni baat poochh loonga, bhai. Ye main uske liye laaya hoon."

"Kis ke liye?"

"Tujhe pata hai kis ke liye." Vikram's hand comes flat over the box. "Yeh duniya mein wohi ek banda hai jise main as my brother-in-law—"

"Vikram."

"—nahin dekh sakta." Vikram laughs, and the laugh goes off him at once. "Aur main jaanta hoon ki tu mere saath khada hai. Bas tu meri baat samajh."

The boy at the counter puts the lemon down and goes to the back and the tube light over the counter hums.

The tin of biscuits on the counter has one biscuit left in it and the lid beside it.

"Shaadi kab hai?" Vihaan sits down across from him.

"Sagai aaj hai." Vikram looks at the door. "Ghar mein mehmaan aa gaye hain. Pinky tayyar hokar baithee hai."

"Aur tu?"

"Main yahan baitha hoon." Vikram puts the box in his shirt pocket and pats it flat. "Aur main mere dost ko batana chahta hoon ki aaj ke baad is ghar mein jo bhi ho, mera faisla mera hai."

Vihaan looks at him for a while and does not say the thing he is thinking. What he says is: "Toh chal. Kapde badal."

A boy comes in from the road with a crate on his shoulder, and a bottle stands up out of the crate, and he sets it down by the counter and goes out again.

The chair Vikram sits in has a torn seat and the stuffing is out at one corner.

## Wapas

The gate of the Suri house at six in the evening, with the porch light on and the sun still standing on the wall, and the drive full of cars parked nose to tail with their mirrors folded in. The marigold is up again on the gateposts and it has been up long enough to be going brown at the ends. A register lies open on the guard's table with the pen on its string, and the string has been tied and re-tied.

The guard looks up from the register and then stands up so fast that his stool goes back on its legs.

"Sahab—"

"Shyam." Vihaan puts his bag down on the table, on the open page, and the bag has an airline tag on the handle and the tag has been through two connections. "Andar kaun hai?"

"Ji, sagai hai." Shyam's hand is at his cap. "Bhai sahab ka order hai."

The drive is swept. Two boys are carrying a stack of chairs across it and one of them stops in the middle of the sweep and stares, and the other one walks into him.

In the hall the light is on and the marigold is on the pillars and the chairs are out, and the family stands about in twos and threes with their glasses, and Aryaman is coming down the stairs with his cuff not yet fastened.

"Bhai, Karan ki shaadi ho gayi aur tu gayab raha." Aryaman stops four steps up. "Main kuch mahine pehle aana chahta tha par life mein kuch cheezein—"

"Chhod." Vihaan comes up the last of the stairs and stands in front of his brother and puts a hand on his shoulder and holds it there. "Main aa gaya."

At the foot of the stairs, at the side where the staff wait, a boy goes past with a tray and does not look at either of them, and the tray has a ring of wet on the cloth where a glass has come off it.

Vihaan's bag stays on the guard's table until ten o'clock that night, with the pen lying beside it.

## Sagai

The hall at seven in the evening, with the chandelier on and every chair filled and the pandit's things laid out on a tray by the fire, and the drum outside under the tent doing its one-two for the guests coming up the steps. The carpet has a fresh edge along the third step and the marigold on the pillars is strung high.

Parmeet takes the floor with his hands together and his voice warm all the way through.

Pinky's dress has been chosen for the photographs and it has a border of gold at the hem, and the hem has been pinned at one place and the pin is still in it.

Two of the aunts have their phones up already, and one of them has the camera turned on herself and is fixing her hair in it.

The drum outside under the tent stops in the middle of its one-two and starts again a beat late.

"Aaj hamare ghar mein ek bahut badi khushi hai." He turns so that the room turns with him. "Pinky aur Vikram ki sagai. Aaj."

The room claps. Pinky stands up where she is sitting, in a red that has been chosen for the photographs, and the room claps again.

Vikram does not stand up. He takes the box out of his shirt pocket and sets it down on the arm of the chair beside him and stands, and the room goes quiet in the way a room goes quiet when it has heard something under a sentence.

"Main is sagai ke liye haan nahin kehta." Vikram's voice carries to the back of the hall without being raised. "Ye main uske liye laaya hoon jisse main sachcha pyaar karta hoon, aur woh tum nahin ho. Main kisi aur se pyaar karta hoon."

Pinky's hand goes to the marigold on the pillar beside her and holds it.

Karan is out of his chair before anybody in that hall moves, and he goes for Vikram with his shoulder down, and two men take hold of him at the third step and he pulls them two steps further.

Vihaan comes across the hall at a walk and puts his hand on the shoulder of the man who is holding Karan, and the hand is not heavy and the man lets go of him anyway.

"Idhar chhodo," he says. "Dekho — idhar. Jo tootega na, uska daam tumhe dena padega, isliye nahin ki main bol raha hoon, isliye ki yeh ghar ka kharcha mera hai."

Karan's tie is pulled half off his collar. Vihaan reaches over and straightens it for him, which is not what anybody in the hall expects him to do.

"Shaadi ka ghar hai," he says to him. "Baarish mein bhi log naachte hain. Kal subah tumhe ye yaad rahega, aaj kuch bhi na sahi."

A woman at the back of the room gets up and takes her child by the hand and goes out through the garden doors.

Pinky's glass is still full and it stands on the floor beside her chair where she has put it down without looking.

"Tujhe jitna maarna hai maar le." Vikram does not put his hands up. "Main tujhko ulta haath nahin uthaunga. Pyaar pe kisi ka zor nahin chalta."

"Radhika ne bhi yahi bola tha." Karan says it with his chin up and his voice breaking in the middle. "Usne bhi yahi bola tha, bhai. Aur tu usi din se—"

The hall stops. Aryaman is on his feet at the side of the room with his glass still in his hand, and the glass has come down to his side, and the ring of wet it leaves on the chair arm stays there for the rest of the evening.

Somebody at the far end of the hall says something to the man beside him and the man beside him does not answer.

The marigold on the pillar beside Pinky has come loose at her hand and hangs down the length of it.

Parmeet turns and finds Vihaan standing at the back of the hall between the door and the wall, with his hands at his sides, counting the chairs.

"Bees kam," he says, to his father and to nobody. "Aur box usne pocket mein rakh diya hai. Koi usse le ke rakh de, warna kal tak poora ghar usey khol chuka hoga."

## Ahana Dhawan

The hall at half past seven, with the chandelier still on and the room still standing where it stood, and the doors to the drive open on the blue of the evening outside. The pandit's tray has been taken away. The ring box is on the chair where Vikram put it and nobody has picked it up.

The car at the steps is long and dark and the driver gets out and opens the door and stands at it, and the woman who comes up the steps has a trunk behind her that two men carry and set down on the porch.

The car's door shuts with a sound the drive has not made all evening, and the two men with the trunk wait at the bottom of the steps until somebody tells them.

The porch light is on and the woman who comes up the steps has a watch on her wrist that the porch light catches once.

"Ye wohi hai jisse main pyaar karta hoon." Vikram walks to the door and takes her hand and turns with her to the room. "The love of my life."

Dimple looks her up and down from the sofa and then says it to the room, pleasantly, the way a price is said in a shop.

The room has gone quiet in its chairs. A boy at the side of it is holding a tray of small glasses and he has stopped in the middle of the floor with the tray in his hands.

"Lakhon mein nahin, crore ki baat karti hai. Chhoti rakam mein maanegi ye ladki."

The woman in the doorway looks at Dimple for a moment and then at the room, and the room has seen her shoes and her watch and the trunk on the porch, and the room has made its mind up.

"Baby, I don't think so." She takes her hand back from Vikram's and pats his chest with it, once, and it is not unkind. "Welcome. I should better leave."

"Rukiye."

Parmeet is standing with his phone in his hand and his face changed entirely, and the phone in his hand has the grey square on it, with the white place in it that could be a light on a wall.

"Ye to Ahana Dhawan." He says it to the room without taking his eyes off her. "Humein is ladki ko kisi bhi haalat mein rokna hoga."

At the back of the hall, between the door and the wall, Vihaan stands with his hands at his sides, and something in the room goes quiet in him that has nothing to do with the room. The lamp on the side table beside him dips and steadies. He looks at it, and then at her, and then at the floor.

He has been away six months. The woman in the doorway is a name off a property file, with a trunk on the porch and a watch on her wrist, and there is no reason at all for the back of his neck to know her before his face does.

Vikram takes her hand and turns with her to the room, and Vihaan's hand comes down flat on the back of a chair, once, the way a man uses a chair when his hand has nowhere else to be.

"Chair bech ke nahi laane hain," he says to the boy beside him, and his voice is a size too big for the hall. "Number ke hisaab se lagao, side mein do line chhodo. Aurat aayengi, unhe uthna baithna hai."

Two men stand by the trunk at the bottom of the steps waiting to be told. He goes down to them and tells them himself, which is four steps and a job that is not his, and he points at the stairs the servants use and then takes his hand back as if the pointing had cost him something.

A low string is struck once under him and left hanging, and the boy with the tray says "sahab" to him, and it stops.

He walks back into the hall and decides, on the way, that it is the heat and the crowd and the fact that Vikram has combed his hair with water and is standing in the middle of the floor holding a stranger's hand like a man who has found something. All of that is a reason. It is not the reason, and he does not take it out and look at it.

## Do Teen Din

The hall at eight, with the chandelier still on and the chairs pushed back to the walls to make the floor bigger, and the trunk from the porch standing at the foot of the stairs with its straps still on it. A boy goes past with a tray of small glasses and the tray has four left on it.

Parmeet crosses the floor at speed with both hands out.

"Main gusse mein tumse kuch zyada hi bhool gaya." His hands come together in front of his chest. "Mere ghar mein bhi betiyan hain."

Ahana looks at his hands and not at his face.

"Bachche, tum humare mehmaan ho." Parmeet's voice is warm again and it does not stop being careful. "Do teen din. Ghar dekho, shaadi mein shamil ho. Aur uske baad—"

"Uske baad?" Ahana's head goes to one side.

"Uske baad jo tum kaho." Parmeet smiles. "Sagai turant karni hai. Vikram ko samjhaana hoga, woh bachcha hai."

Behind him, at the side of the hall, Pinky stands with her glass held in both hands, and her face has nothing on it at all.

The pandit's tray has been carried out and the tray's cloth stays on the floor by the fire.

"Ji." Ahana picks up her own glass from the boy's tray and does not drink from it. "Do teen din."

At the door of the hall Vihaan stands with the phone at his ear and one hand flat on the back of a chair, and the boy with the tray has to wait for him. "Saat," he says, and then into the phone, to Verma, with the room there to hear him: "Aath do. Aur saat ke saamne wala band karke chhodo, chaabi board se hata do."

He listens to what the other end makes of a change at eight at night on a wedding day, because it is a change somebody has to answer for. "Saat ki khidki east pe hai," he says. "Subah dhoon dhoom ho jaayegi. Aur kal subah us corridor ka bulb naya chahiye, meri taraf se." The phone comes down on the chair arm and he does not look up again. "Aur register mein naam main khud likhunga."

The room opposite seven is not shut because of a draught. On the list it has a designation in the town's column, and the man it belongs to sleeps under this roof for two nights in the fortnight, because he is the one who signs for the ground. The key comes off the board because the board is in the pantry, where the house can see it.

The boy asks which room that is, and Vihaan tells him, and then says, "Aur kisi ko mat batana ki maine kaha," in the voice of a man who has just heard himself say a thing he cannot explain.

The trunk is taken up the stairs by two men and set down in the guest room at the end of the corridor, and the room has been opened for the first time in a year, and the smell of it comes out into the corridor and stays there. There is no key on the door of it when they leave it, and there is not going to be one, which is a thing the house will have to explain to itself in the morning.

On the chair arm the phone has two calls waiting on it, and the diary has eleven in the morning still empty in it, which is where the last of his own morning went.

One low string is struck once in that corridor and left hanging, and it stops when the boy says thank you, and the hang of it goes down the stairwell with him.

## Naagmani Humari

The passage behind the hall at nine at night, where the caterers' trolleys turn, with two bulbs on the ceiling and one of them flickering at the far end. A steel tray stands on the floor by the wall with the evening's used glasses on it and the cloth over it has been pulled half back.

Parmeet stands with his hands behind his back. Dimple stands beside him. Pinky stands against the wall with her arms folded.

"Bas ek baar ye ladki humare ghar mein rehne aa jaye." Parmeet says it quietly. "To bhai sahab, naagmani humari."

"Uske paas kya hai?" Dimple's chin comes up.

"Uske paas woh zameen hai jiske neeche woh cheez hai." Parmeet looks at the flickering bulb at the end of the passage. "Kitne ki hai?"

"Chalis lakh, tau ji. Ek bigha nadi ki taraf." Pinky's voice comes off the wall without any waiting in it. "Aur us aadmi ka hisaab alag hai, jo magarmach ko khilata hai. Chhe lakh mahina. Do hafte chahiye toh teen."

"Toh chalo khareed lete hain." Dimple says it as a woman ending a conversation.

"Zameen bikti nahin hai wahan." Parmeet does not turn round. "Aur chalis din mein nikalte hain kagaz. Humare paas chalis din nahin hain, aur us mahine daam bhi badh jate hain, kyunki poora desh wahi aata hai."

"Chalis lakh is ghar ke paas nahin hai, tau ji." Pinky says it the way she reads a bill. "Jo hai woh mere gale mein hai, aur woh bhi mere naam pe nahin. Aur kal subah printer wale ko chaar lakh pandrah hazaar dena hai."

"Phir bhi ek hisaab toh banta hai." Dimple lifts her chin. "Teen crore aadmi aate hain us mahine. Ek din ka bhi hisaab na lagao?"

"Ek din ka hisaab lagta hai." Parmeet's voice goes flat, the way his does when the number is bigger than the room. "Us din wahan ka rate doona hota hai, aur phir bhi koi bechta nahin." Parmeet looks at the door and then at the floor. "Woh file is ghar mein nahin hai, Dimple. Do ghante baith kar zameen ka kaagaz nahin banta."

"Guest room tayyar hai." Pinky says it to the floor, and she does not look at the man in the doorway as she says it. "Lehnga bhi."

"Lehnga kal." Parmeet starts walking. "Pehle woh ghar mein rahe. Ek raat bhi kaafi hai."

Vihaan comes out of the pantry at the end of the passage with the phone in his hand and a man talking into it about a van in Ghaziabad. He stops inside the door because the tray is in the way.

"Bulb kaun badalta hai yahan?" he says. "Ye flicker karegi toh raat ko catering wala bill mein daal kar layega. Aur tray utha lo — neeche bachche daud rahe hain."

"Hum baat kar rahe the." Dimple says it to Parmeet's back, not to him.

"Haan." He takes the earbud out of his ear and puts it back in again half a second later, which is the whole of his answer. "Ghaziabad wala van kal subah char baje aayega. Driver poochta hai ki ground ka entry kaun dega. Main usse yahi bataunga: mujhse. Aur nadi wale ka bill main bharunga. Aap apna kaam dekhiye."

He says it the way he would say anything about a van, and then he is back on the phone, and the three of them let the second go by without repeating the sentence he walked into. Nobody in that passage asks him to leave.

Parmeet's hand stays on the back of a chair. Dimple looks at the man in the doorway the way a woman looks at a room she has already arranged in her head and cannot re-arrange now. Pinky has moved off the wall and is standing in front of it, which nobody notices.

Two bulbs are on over the trolleys' turning and one of them is dying at the far end. Under the cloth on the steel tray there are the evening's glasses, and on the mela's list the corridor they came through is counted like everything else. The count runs on one sheet of paper, good for fifteen days, and it buys the right to put a generator on that ground and to be standing on it at two in the morning with a digger and six of his friends. The paper is in the carton that left this house in November. It is on the channel's fourth floor now, in a drawer he keeps shut, and his name is printed on it four times.

## Chhupana Mushkil Ho Raha Hai

The guest room at the end of the upper corridor at midnight, with the lamp off and the light of the garden's tent coming in through the window and lying on the floor in strips. The trunk stands at the foot of the bed with one strap done and one still loose, and the bed has not been turned down, and the room has the smell of a room that has had its door shut for a year.

A glass of water stands on the dressing table where the cup stood, and the ring of the cup is still on the wood beside it.

In the lid pocket of the trunk there are two file folders folded the wrong way round, which is how a man folds them at an allotment counter when he has been standing in a queue. They have her father's name printed on them and a number of a plot, and the number is small, and one of them has a red stamp on the corner that says the paper is three months from being renewed. She has carried both of them in the train's overhead rack for eleven hours and has not opened either since the hall.

She has both hands on the frame of the mirror and she is looking at the face she came in with.

"To kaisa lag raha hai?" She asks it in her sister's cadence, the chin up, a pause left in it before the last word, the laugh taken out. "Finally aa hi gayi is ghar mein. Chhupana mushkil ho raha hai na tere liye?"

"Haan," she answers in her own voice, and does not change it for the other one. "Aa hi gayi."

The strips of light move on the floor when the canvas outside is let go and comes back.

"Tujhe yahan chahiye kya?" She gives that one the same way. Then she answers it, and she is quicker than the question. "Paisa. Is ghar ka paisa. Aur jo beta mile."

"In logon ne mujhse mera sab kuch cheen liya." Her hands come off the frame. "Mamma, Papa, Rohan, aur aap."

At *aap* she turns round, and there is nobody in the room but her, and the room is the room they gave her, with somebody else's sheets on the bed and somebody else's flowers going over in the vase.

"Vihaan se pyaar Purvi ne kiya tha." The first of them she gives in her sister's voice, with the laugh taken out of it. The second she says in her own voice, to the dark of the room, in the voice she uses downstairs. "Ahana Vihaan se sirf nafrat karti hai."

Then she says it again, and the second time is louder than a guest room at midnight carries to anybody, and it is thrown at a door that is shut. "Sirf nafrat."

Nobody in the room answers her.

She sits down on the edge of the bed to take her shoes off, and the door is what she sees, because she has left it unlocked and it shuts without a sound.

A room of this house at a wedding has a key in its lock and the house keeps the keys. There is no key here, and there is a new strike plate on the frame, and the brass on it has not gone soft yet. Somebody in this house has paid a man to change a lock in a guest corridor at eight o'clock, on a night when every man in it is standing in front of a tent, and that man has been paid more than the plate costs.

The window is open an inch, which she has not done, and the latch has the dust of a year on it with a clean line through where two fingers moved it. Outside, on the wall, the corridor's own bulb has been changed and is a size brighter than it needs to be, and it will be burning at four in the morning for a woman who cannot sleep, and nobody in this house gets up at four for anybody.

She heard the number said in the hall, with the whole room there to hear it said, and the number in her head is seven. The corridor she walked has eight painted on the inside of her door, high up, where only a man who has just moved something looks.

The register comes up to her at ten minutes past ten on a boy with a jug, because a guest of this house signs for her room, and he stands there with the pen in his hand and does not offer it. She reads the line they have made for her — Ahana Dhawan, and in the column for the town, where a house like this writes a caste and a district, somebody has put a small question mark instead. The name above it has been written twice, the first one crossed through in a line drawn once and hard, and she puts her finger on it without meaning to.

"Yeh naam kis ka tha?" The boy does not look at the page. "Pata nahin, madam. Unhone kaha tha ki main poochhun nahin."

Somebody has taken a name off this page to put her on it, and the boy has been told not to say whose.

She signs it. Then she stands with her hand on the window's glass and looks down at the drive and the last of the cars and the wet place on the lawn where the sprinkler has been going since before dark, and she does not move for as long as the glass holds her.

She has said two sentences aloud in a room with nobody in it, and there is a pen in the corridor going down the stairs.

The only person in this house who would pay a man at eight at night to leave a door unlocked so that a stranger would never have to ask anybody for anything is the one man she came here without having worked out how to sit across from at a breakfast table.

Outside, boots go along the passage and stop at the stair. They do not come on and they do not go back, and she stands with her head turned toward the door and waits for whichever of the two it is going to be. The tent's band takes its last song down and stops with them, and the waiting is the end of it, and she is still sitting there with her hands flat on the mattress and one shoe off.

On the other side of the door the corridor lamp dips once and comes back, and there is nothing in the corridor. Then the lamp in the room is turned up, and the face in the glass is the face the gate let in, and the clock on the bedside table goes on ticking, and the strips of light go out and come back when the canvas is let go, once.


## Mudde Ki Baat

The small room at the back of the Suri house at two in the night, with the door shut and one bulb on over the table and the rest of the room in the dark, and the table cleared except for a register and a glass. The window has a grille on it and somebody's dupatta hangs over the chair back.

Parmeet sits at the head of the table with his hands folded. Dimple sits at his right. Aryaman stands.

"Mudde ki baat ye hai ki Ahana ko koi problem nahin honi chahiye." Parmeet's finger comes down on the register. "Humein woh naagmani chahiye, aur us tak pahunchne ka raasta woh ladki hai."

"Aur agar woh na maane?" Dimple says it without lifting her eyes from the register.

"Woh maanegi." Parmeet sits back. "Uska zameen ka kaam hai. Uske paas paper hain, humare paas paisa hai. Aur agar phir bhi—"

He does not finish it. The glass on the table has a fly in it and nobody has emptied it.

"Aryaman." Parmeet looks up at his son. "Tu uske saath rehna. Ghar mein, mehmaan ki tarah. Aur jo woh poochhe, sab bata dena."

"Main usse jhooth nahin bolunga."

"Jhooth kaun bol raha hai?" Parmeet's hands come open. "Hum sirf uska swagat kar rahe hain."

There is a knock at the panel, and Pinky comes in sideways with her dupatta over one shoulder and her phone in her hand, and she does not wait for the room to ask.

"Woh upar kya kar rahi hai, akele andhere mein?" Pinky says. "Main darwaze se guzri toh bol rahi thi kuch. Kuch nahin, bas do baar ek hi baat."

"Kaunsi baat?" Dimple's eyes come up off the register for the first time.

"Paise wali baat." Pinky says it like a woman reading a report. "Ki use is ghar ka paisa chahiye, aur is ghar ka beta — jo mile. Ki is ghar se nafrat karti hai, yeh do baar boli. Main kya jaanu, bhabhi ka kaam hai."

She goes, and the door is left open a hand's width, and Dimple looks at her father-in-law with that left at the table between them.

"Toh do baar boli hai." Parmeet puts his finger down on the register and leaves it there. "Ek baar dil se hoti hai, ek baar zubaan se. Jo do baar bolti hai, woh samajh gayi hai ki kaun sun raha hai." He turns the glass on the table round with one finger and lets it go. "Aur jo aurat apni keemat do baar batati hai, usse lena aasaan hota hai. Beta kaun, yeh hum tay karenge."

Aryaman goes out of that room without a word, and the door shuts behind him, and the fly goes round the glass on the table and settles again.

## Mamuli Sa Pitt Saanp

## Meri Been Hi Nahin

The small room at the back at four in the night, with the bulb on over the table and the case open on the floor, and a been lying across the case with its reed end towards the door. The room smells of the box and of the smoke that never leaves it.

Maa Sapela sits on the floor with her legs folded under her and the basket in front of her, and the cloth and the rope are on the floor beside it. The paper is in her hand and she has read it twice.

The basket's lid comes off. The snake in it comes up and hangs in the air and turns its head from one side of the room to the other and does not settle.

Maa Sapela picks the been up. She puts the mouthpiece to her lips and blows three notes, and the three notes go round the room and come back off the wall and stop in the middle of the air.

The snake does not move.

"Woh jo aaj is ghar mein aayi hai." She looks up at her man. "Uske paas woh cheez hai jo mere paas nahin hai."

## Pehla Din

The lawn at the side of the Suri house at ten past five in the morning, with the sky going grey over the wall and the tent's canvas down at the west side and the grass wet to the ankle. The chairs are stacked at the edge of the lawn in twos and fours. A bucket stands by the wall with a cloth over the top of it, and the cloth is wet through.

At the edge of the lawn the chairs stand in stacks of six, and one stack has gone over in the night and nobody has set it up.

"Mere badle ka yeh pehla din." Her voice is low and it does not shake. "Aur unki khushi ka aakhri."

Behind her the house stands with its lights going out one by one as the cleaning ends, and the tent's poles stand in the grass, and the drive has the last of the night's cars in it.

"Jung ka aagaaz ho chuka hai." She turns her face up to the grey. "Mere Bharat desh ke dushman, mere parivaar ke dushman, aur Naaglok ke dushmanon ka ant."

The sprinkler at the far end of the lawn turns and hisses and lays its water down in its long arc, and the woman stands in it and does not move out of the way of it.

The water goes over the path and onto the drive and into the gutter, and the gutter runs all the way to the gate.

A man at the gate has his hand on the boom and he has stopped with it half up and he does not bring it down.

The marigold on the gatepost beside her has a garland come loose and one end of it in the wet grass.

A glass stands on the lawn's edge where somebody set it down in the night, and the rain has filled it.

A tray stands on the step with the last of the wedding's sweet boxes on it and the string still round them.

Then the sky comes up properly over the wall and the first of the house's doors opens, and she is a guest standing on a wet lawn at five in the morning with her hands at her sides.

Vihaan stands in the doorway with his coat on over the shirt he has slept in. He looks past her at the lawn, and back at her, and at the wet grass round her feet, and his face does what it does at the office and at the gate and at every desk in this house, which is nothing at all.

"Nashta side hall mein hai," he says. "Aap yahan khadi rahengi toh bheega hi rahega. Andar aaiye, aur woh glass utha ke rakh dijiye, hawa se gir jaayega."

She does not move at once, and he does not ask her why. He stands in the door long enough for his arm to have tired, and then he moves back and holds it, and the two of them go in past each other with four feet of wet between them, and the door stays open behind until the wind takes it.
