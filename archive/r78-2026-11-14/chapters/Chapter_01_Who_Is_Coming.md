## The Kumbh

*Prayagraj, Uttar Pradesh. 2001.* Dark, and a conch, and then water, and light lying on the water on the ghat.

Clay diyas burn in rows down the wet steps on both banks, and the river takes their light out into the middle of itself. Orange flags hang over the tents and lift and fall in the wind. Somewhere a bell is ringing and rings itself out. Pilgrims stand in the shallows with their clothes held up in their fists, and the cold comes off the water into their legs.

A copper pot stands on the top step with a diya set in the mouth of it, and the flame leans and comes upright again.

Behind the last tent there is a stack of crates and a coil of rope for the flags, and a man asleep across them with his arm over his face.

## The Akhara

The akhand akhara at night, and one round tent, and the canvas of it lit from the fire outside, and the open ground between the tent and the river packed edge to edge with the shoes of every man who has come.

Nobody is sitting.

Monks in maroon and yellow stand in a knot at the back. Sadhus in saffron crowd together under a parasol with a gold fringe. A naga sadhu taps his staff on the ground, three times, and the dust goes up off it.

"Shant raho! Shant raho!"

"Hamein yahan is tarah kyun bulaya gaya hai? Kya wajah hai?"

"Hum sab tay tithi par hamesha pahaadi ilaakon mein milte the na. Toh aaj is Kumbh ke akhand akhade mein kyun? Koi toh badi wajah hogi."

"Aisi bhi kya museebat aa gayi?"

"Museebat aayi nahin hai." The voice comes from the dark at the back of the tent. "Abhi tak aane waali hai."

The crowd turns.

He walks out into the open ground at the centre of the tent. Bearded, round glasses, a beige blazer over a black polo-neck. The fire outside throws his shadow up the canvas behind him, twice his size. He lifts both hands.

"Baith jaiye. Aap sab log baith jaiye. Baith jaiye."

They sit, a row at a time, the whole tent going down, and the noise goes with them. The score goes out. Nobody speaks. The anger leaves the room and fear takes its seat.

"Mujhe maaf kijiye ki maine aap sab ko is tarah yahan bulaya." He turns as he speaks, so that each side of the tent has his face in its turn. "Hamare dharm bhale hi alag-alag hon. Par desh ek hai. Khoon ek hai. Junoon ek hai. Desh ki raksha. Dushman deshon se raksha. Yahan tak ki dushman desh se aayi beemari ka vaar. Narsanhar. Is desh par aayi har museebat ka tod nikaala hai humne. Lade hain hum."

A log settles in the fire outside. Sparks go up. Nobody moves.

"Jab jab vinaashkari grah nakshatra saath hote hain, tab tab is duniya mein pralay aati hai. Aur jab jab sitaaron ne hamein yeh sanket diye hain, hum isi tarah mile hain." He looks round the tent. "Aaj phir wahi sankat aaya hai."

"Kaisa sankat?"

"Sankat abhi aaya nahin hai. Aane waala hai." A beat. "Chaubees saal baad."

The tent erupts. A guru in a saffron turban is on his feet with his finger up.

## The Suri House

The Suris keep a house behind a white picket fence, and on this night every window in it is lit, because the boy turns one in the morning and his mother has decided that the lights should be on for him before he can see them.

A wooden plate by the gate says SURI, and under it the same name in Gurmukhi, cut by a man in the market who charged for the second line and threw it in. The lawn has been cut. The gate stands open the way it stands open all evening in that lane.

Inside, the front room has been got ready for a birthday and has lost the argument. Yellow bunting hangs across the wall in two long loops, and one end of it has come down and been put back up with tape. Balloons lie where they fell — on the floor, in among the cushions, along the foot of the stairs. On the table, on a stand, there is a cake: white icing, one candle in it, and a name piped across the top in blue letters, **Vihaan**. One candle, because there is one year to count. Blue, because he will have nothing else, and his mother has been up since five o'clock for it.

A woman comes out of the passage with an armful of clothes still on their hangers and puts them into an open case on the sofa. Her plait comes loose over one shoulder and she puts it back with the back of her wrist. Nimrat is small and quick, with grey at her temples that the henna has not taken and hands that are the busiest thing in any room she is in; she folds in threes, and she is being scolded, and she is enjoying it.

The scolding comes from the floor, where a big soft-shouldered man in a lilac kurta-pyjama is down among the balloons straightening cushions and then moving every one of them an inch. Parmeet has a beard going white at the chin and his spectacles pushed up on his forehead, and he is the kind of man who cannot be in a room without putting the room right. His slippers are a size too big and he has never once cared.

"O Nimrat!" He says it to the cushions. "Teri packing-shacking hui ki nahin? Phir wahan Kumbh mein jaake mat bolna ki yeh bhool gayi, woh bhool gayi."

"Parmeet ji, tu chup na hua na—" A kurta comes off its hanger and goes onto the pile. "—toh main apne aap ko bhi bhool jaaungi."

"Oye, sheti kar." He points at the table without turning his head. "Tere puthar da cake bhi toh kaatna hai. Aur phir subah room mein bhi toh nikalna hai."

"Kar toh rahi hoon!"

Parmeet laughs and the dholak on the radio goes with him, and then the song drops to one flute and the two of them work on in it without saying anything.

She shakes her head at the case, and folds, and looks up at the cake, and her hands stop. She looks at the blue letters for a while. Then she looks away, at nothing, quickly, and goes on folding.

The house has the sound a house has at this hour: the fridge in the kitchen, the ceiling fan going round with one loose blade, a dog three lanes off and another dog answering it. Under all of it, from the back of the house, a radio is playing a song about a girl in a blue dupatta, quiet, at a volume that is not meant to be listened to.

Upstairs a door closes, and a moment later the rail of a cot shakes twice. The mother's hand goes up flat in the air without her turning to it, and the shaking stops, and nothing comes down the stairs.

The bell rings.

Nimrat's hand stops on the cloth.

"Itni raat ko kaun hai?" She says it to the passage and her eyes go up the stairs and stay there, and nothing comes down them.

"Woh doctor janaab honge." Parmeet is already up, his slippers loud on the floor. "Khota itni late. Main dekhta hoon."

"Parmeet ji—"

Parmeet is at the door with his hand on it. Behind him the music has stopped and the house is down to its fan and its fridge.

Three men stand at the gate in the dark with bags on their shoulders. One is grey in the beard and wears a khaki scarf. One is wrapped in a dark shawl with his hand laid flat on his chest. The third stands a little in front of the others — shaggy hair, a heavy moustache, a brown scarf at his throat — and the moon is behind his head, and he is looking at the house and not at the man in the doorway.

"Ji?" Parmeet looks out. "Aap log…?"

The man with the moustache looks back at him, and takes his time about answering.

*"Bahut mushkil se sarhad paar karke tumhare desh mein aaye hain. Tumhare desh ki barbadi yahin se shuru karte hain."*

## She Has to Come

The fire outside has gone down to red on the open ground, and its light comes in under the canvas and lies on every face in the circle: ash, sandal paste, saffron, one pair of round glasses.

A brass thali of ash goes round the circle from hand to hand, and nobody takes from it.

"The vinaash shuru ho chuka hai." The professor stands in the red light of the tent. "Par asli vinaash laayega 2025."

The tent is on its feet again.

"Is museebat ka koi hal toh hoga. Hamein usse kaun bachaayega?"

"Woh bachaayegi." He says it quietly, and the tent goes quiet to hear it. "Usse aana hi hoga."

"Kaun hai woh?"

"Hamara desh bahut mahaan hai. Jab jab Kaal aata hai, tab tab Vishnu aur Shiv kisi na kisi roop mein aa jaate hain. Desh ko bachate hain. Us hamle se ladne ke liye bhi wahi bhejenge use."

"Kise?"

"Use." His finger is up. "Usse aana hi hoga. Chaaron dishaayein. Teenon lok. Yahan tak ki khud Brahmand bhi uske saamne khada ho jaaye use rokne ke liye. Woh tab bhi nahin rukegi. Woh aayegi."

"Kaun?"

"Na nari, na nar." The score comes up under him. "Na jaanwar, na insaan. Na shaitaan, na farishta. Na bhagwan. Par bhagwan ki sabse badi bhakt. Sashakt. Shiv ki vardani."

A sadhu grey with ash speaks from the floor.

"Par Sheshnaag ke is kul ka toh ant ho chuka hai."

The professor turns on him, and his face changes.

"Jis Shesh Naag par Vishnu ji ka haath ho. Shiv ji ka saath ho. Nageshwar." He lets it sit. "Uske kul ka ant kabhi nahin ho sakta."

A golden god with many arms sits in burning cloud. A Shivling stands alone in a hall of dark blue pillars, and the floor round it is swept clean, and a single bel leaf lies wet on the stone.

"Hamein woh milegi kahan?"

The dark room again, and the sage close: the ash stripes, a white mark between the brows.

"Anantpur ki Naagrani, Ananta. Aapko milegi." He turns the leaf. "Uttari pahaadiyon ke khatam hone ke baad, shehar ki sarhad ki pehli gali. Jis ghar ke bahar ek chhota Shivling hai. Us ghar mein. Wahin woh milegi. Ananta."

The professor stands where he is with his hands at his sides.

"Aapko uske paas jaana hoga. Aur batana hoga, chaubees saal baad jo Mahavinaash aane waala hai. Usse ladne ke liye taiyaar ho jaaye."

The tent. "Par use dhoondhne jaayega kaun?"

"Main jaaunga." He looks round the whole circle. "Main dhoondhunga use."

Stone steps climb between broken walls in blue hills. Carved serpents stand on either side of the stair, hoods raised, and one small light burns at the top. Mist sits in the valley below the steps.

"Woh Naaglok ki dhadkan hai. Shesh Naagin ke saare gunon se sampann hai woh."

## Papa's Research

A study at night with one lamp on the desk and its green shade throwing the light down onto the page, and the rest of the room standing in the dark round it: books to the ceiling, a whiteboard, a wall of papers pinned in rows. The professor is bent over a desk heaped with more books, a navy blazer now over a red polo-neck, his glasses off and lying on the page.

"Papa."

A boy stands among the shelves. Eight or ten years old, a mop of dark curls, and he has come in without knocking.

The professor goes down on his heels in front of him, and takes the boy's face in one hand, and looks at him.

He puts a book into the boy's arms. It is large, with a metal cover worked all over in raised patterns, and it is heavy; the boy needs both arms to hold it against his chest.

"Ise sambhal ke rakhna." His hand is flat on the metal. "Yeh, yeh Papa ki research hai. Ise kabhi kisi ko mat dena."

"Papa—"

"Ho sakta hai ki Papa ki jaan ko khatra ho."

"Papa, abe kya bol rahe ho?" The boy's voice goes high. "Aap kahan ja rahe ho? Mat jaiye!"

"Mujhe jaana padega, beta. Ananta ke paas." He fixes the boy's collar without looking at it. "Meri calculation ke hisaab se yahi sahi waqt hai. Usse batana padega ki tabahi desh ke darwaze par dastak de chuki hai."

The boy's eyes are full. He is frightened and he is trying not to show it, and his chin goes up the way a boy's chin does. His hands are shaking against the metal cover of the book and he grips it harder to make them stop, and the gripping is what makes them shake.

"Ek baat hamesha yaad rakhna, beta." The professor's hand moves to his head. "Desh ka rishta sabse bada rishta hota hai. Mujhe kuch bhi ho jaaye, bas ek baat yaad rakhna. Ki tere pita desh ke liye shaheed ho gaye."

The boy's mouth opens and nothing comes out.

"Aur tu bhi hamesha sabse aage apne desh ko rakhna."

The hand stays on his head. The score comes up, one flute and a woman's voice under it, and holds while neither of them moves.

The window behind the desk is black, and the room is in it, doubled and backwards: the lamp, the desk, and the two of them.

The door of the study stands open behind them. Beyond it is a dark passage, and the rest of a house asleep.

## The First Lane

An old white car on a road at night. The professor drives it himself, both hands on the wheel, and the windscreen takes the light of each lamp he passes.

The sage's voice goes with him.

"Uttari pahaadiyon ke khatam hone ke baad. Shehar ki sarhad ki pehli gali. Jis ghar ke bahar ek chhota Shivling hai. Us ghar mein. Wahin woh milegi. Ananta."

A television stands on a dark cabinet against an ochre wall, the picture red and blue, the sound low. A strip along the bottom says BREAKING NEWS, and above the words a worn gold "SN".

"Ghuspaitiye hamare desh mein ghusna shuru kar diya hai. Dushman desh se bahut log hamare desh mein ghuskar hamare logon ko maarkar unki jagah le rahe hain."

A room at the head of a staircase. Yellow bunting shows below, down the stairs. A small boy stands in a cot with both hands on the rail. A paper cap has slipped to the back of his head. His face is wet and the crying has worn down to a croak. His eyes are long and black and too quick for a face that small, and they come to the man in the doorway and stay there after the crying has stopped. Round the cot the room is dark, and the door stands open on to the landing.

The man with the moustache comes up the last stair and stops in the door.

He looks at the boy. The boy looks back at him and goes quiet.

The man crosses the room and turns the child a little towards the window, and the light from the landing comes up the small face. It is a good face. It is the kind of face a room turns to look at without deciding to, and it is looking at him now with no fear in it at all.

The man holds him a moment longer than he needs to. The boy's breath is still going in little jumps against his shoulder, and then it steadies, and then it stops being afraid, which is worse to watch than the crying.

Then he bends and lifts him out. The boy's fist closes on the brown scarf.

"Savdhan rahiye." The television, below. "Satark rahiye."

A young man in spectacles and a red check shirt sits on a patterned sofa in front of it, a cup of tea going cold on the arm. Across the room a woman in a pink salwar suit sits by the lamp with her plait over one shoulder.

"Shalini." He says it to the screen, not to her. "Yeh Parmeet aur Nimrat phone nahin utha rahe. Unke ghar gaya tha main. Wahan taala hai."

The woman looks up.

"Na jaane woh poora Suri parivaar achank se, bina bataye, kahan gayab ho gaya." He shakes his head slowly. "Tu bhi zara dhyan se rahiyo. Sambhal ke."

"Yeh dekhiye na." She points at the screen. "Baar baar kya bata rahe hain news mein."

A knock at the door.

"Ruko." She is up, and the lamp behind her throws her shadow the length of the wall. "Is waqt kaun aaya?"

"Main dekhta hoon—"

"Main dekhti hoon, ruko." She goes to the door and stops short of it. "Kaun hai?"

"Main Tushar Sinha. Main ek scientist hoon. Please darwaza kholiye. Mujhe aapse zaroori baat karni hai."

"Nahin nahin." She puts her hand flat on the door. "Hum nahin darwaza kholenge."

"Dekhiye please, mujhe aapse bahut zaroori kaam hai. Ab aap darwaza kholiye please."

Her husband gets up. "Dekhte hain."

"Nahin, mat kholo." She has her hand flat on the door again. "Yeh pata nahin kaun hai."

"Main dekhta hoon. Ruko. Chhodo." He takes her wrist and moves her hand off the door, and she lets him, and he opens it.

A white car stands in the lane behind the man on the step with its door still open and the engine running.

"O ji, kya kaam hai?"

"Mujhe Ananta se milna hai."

"Kaun Ananta?"

"Anant kul ki Ananta."

"Anant kul?" He looks at his wife, and back. "O ji, aap jaiye. Yahan aisa koi nahin rehta."

"Dekhiye, ek minute. Ek minute." Tushar Sinha lays a hand on his own chest. "Yeh, yeh yahi address hai. Woh, woh yahin rehti hai. Aap use bulaiye. Mujhe baat karni hai usse. Zaroori hai. Uska jaanna, ki is desh ko sirf woh hi bacha sakti hai. Sirf woh."

"Ab kya galat kar raha hai yeh?" The husband turns to her.

"Dekhiye, aap samajhne ki koshish kijiye." The professor's hand comes out and stops before it touches the man's sleeve. "Dushman desh ke ghuspaitiye is desh mein ghus chuke hain. Aur wahi unhein maar sakti hai. Hum sabko bacha sakti hai. Desh ke dushman hamare desh ko tabah karna chahte hain, aur ek wahi hai jo unhein hamesha ke liye khatam kar sakti hai."

"O bhai, maaf karna. Tum jao yahan se. Ab jaiye."

"Please meri baat suniye. Please."

The door shuts. The bolt goes across, and the sound of it is the loudest thing in the scene.

They stand in the middle of the room.

"Kaha tha na maine, mat kholiye." She turns on him. "Kaun hai woh? Kiskee baat kar raha hai? Kaun desh ko bachane waala hai? Yeh kis Ananta ki baat kar raha tha?"

"Ji, menu ki pata."

From the other side of the door: "Dekhiye, meri baat suniye please."

A car stands in the lane with its lights off. The man with the moustache sits in it, looking back over his shoulder, and he does not get out of it the whole time the professor is begging.

## The Woman at the Door

The Aroras' front room at night with the television on and one lamp burning on the cabinet, and the street door standing open onto the lane and the lane's own light coming in.

On the shelf above the divan there is a row of files with a cloth over them, and the lamp makes the cloth yellow.

Another knock.

"Ab wahi hoga." The husband rubs his face. "Woh scientist. Pata nahin kyun phir se aa gaya. Yeh scientists bhi—" He pulls the door open. "Arre bhai, humne kaha na, yahan par koi—"

The sky goes dark. Storm cloud rolls across the night over the lamps, and the wind takes the door out of his hand and bangs it against the wall.

A woman stands on the step with a grey shawl over her head. There is blood on her forehead at the hairline and dust on her feet. Her hands are joined.

She is tall for a woman of that lane, and the ground is hers to stand on. She is hurt: there is a wound under the shawl at the top of her arm, and the red of the sari is wet and dark down one side and it is not water. The shawl is coarse and grey and old, and under it the sari is a deep red gone black in the wet, and it is silk; nothing else about her is silk. Her eyes are gold in the lamplight and stay gold when she steps out of it, which the husband notices and does not have a word for. Her feet are bare and cut in two places, and no woman walks that far and stands that straight.

"Mujhe madad ki zaroorat hai."

"Na ji." He steps into the doorway. "Hum kisi ajnabi ki madad nahin karte."

Shalini comes past his shoulder and looks.

"Yeh toh pet se hai ji." Her voice changes entirely. "Lagta hai iski delivery hone waali hai." She is not frightened of her; she is frightened for her. She takes the woman by the arm. "Aao ji, sheeta aao. Aaiye, andar aaiye."

Inside the door the woman's head goes back and she cries out, and the lamp on the cabinet jumps.

"Bas, bas, bas." Shalini has both arms round her. "Aaram se. Main paani laati hoon. Aaram se, aaram se."

They lay her on the divan under the shelf of files and cover her with a dark blanket. A shadow moves across the ochre wall, thrown by the lamp, and the shadow of Shalini's hands moves with it. The woman's face is wet, her eyes shut, her head pressed back, and she holds the blanket in both fists.

Thunder, close, and the picture on the television goes to snow.

Shalini lifts a child wrapped in white.

"Yeh beti hai."

She lays the child against the woman's side.

"Aap, aap fikr mat kijiye." Shalini's hand is on the woman's hair. "Hum aapko kuch nahin hone denge."

She is in great pain, and she is calm.

"Main jaanti hoon." The woman's eyes are on the child. "Main nahin bachungi. Par aapko isse bachana hai. Aapko isse sambhalna hai."

"Ji, hum…" The husband's hands open and close. "Lekin hum isse kyun?"

"Kyunki woh chahte hain." Her finger comes up. "Yeh unki ichchha hai."

"Woh kaun hai?"

The finger goes toward the roof.

"Woh Vikral. Woh Akal. Woh Mahakal."

The water she said she would fetch stands on the floor beside the divan in a steel glass, full, and nobody has drunk from it.

The child begins to cry.

"Bahut log aayenge isse maarne ke liye." Her hand comes down on the small back. "Par aapko isse bachana hai. Jaise aaj isse maine bachaya hai."

## Sarvashreshth

High ground at night, blue under the moon, and snow standing on the far peaks above the treeline, and the path she is on cut into the rock an arm's width wide. The woman walks alone along a ridge with the grey shawl drawn round her, the wind pulling at it, one hand under her belly.

Her voice goes with her, and the score comes up under it, a woman singing without words.

"Mujhe Sarvashreshth Naagin ke naam se jaana gaya. Maine teen janm liye. Pehle Pratha."

A woman in a mustard kurta stands with a cloth bag on her shoulder. Beside her the hood of a great cobra is spread over moonlit water.

"Phir Prarthana."

A crowned naagin stands in a green forest in warm light. Gold light pours out of her hands and off the leaves.

"Aur phir Pragati. Teenon janmon se insaan. Par janm Sarvashreshth Naagin ka."

She climbs between the rocks, and the shawl pulls, and she stops to breathe.

"Ek naagin kitne bhi roop badal le. Kitni bhi zindagi jee le. Par uske liye woh pal sabse bada hota hai jab uske Bhagwan use chunte hain." She looks up at the moon. "Kisi maksad ke liye mujhe chuna gaya. Tujhe janm dene ke liye."

She has a small bundle tied in a cloth at her waist, and the knot of it works loose, and she ties it again without stopping.

Snow peaks stand behind her.

"Sarvashreshth se bhi sarvashreshth naagin hai tu. Sau saal ki tapasya ke baad ichchhadhari naagin bante hain. Par tu un sau saalon ki tapasya ke bina hi naagin banegi."

She stops and lays her hand flat on the stone and holds herself up.

"Tujhe is duniya mein laane ka kaam shayad meri zindagi ka sabse bada kaam hoga."

The wind goes through the pass and the shawl flies out behind her.

"Tujhe bheja gaya hai Vishnu ke vardaan ke saath, Sheshnaag ke kul se, Shiv ke amrit ke saath. Tujhe bheja gaya hai Bharat desh ko aane waale mahasankat se bachane ke liye. Aayegi jo is mahashaktishali Naagmani ki nayi mahashaktishali uttaradhikari hoke."

The moon goes behind a cloud and comes out again.

"Anant kul ki ho tum. Is kismat, is kayanaat ne chuna hai tumhe. Is duniya ko tabahi se bachane ke liye chuna hai tumhe."

She walks.

"Aaj main in saaton lok ki mahashaktishali Maha Uttaraadhikari ko janm dene ja rahi hoon. Teri teen saal ki behen ko chhodkar ja rahi hoon, uske ghar waalon ke saath. Kyunki yeh Shiv aur Vishnu ka aadesh hai." She lifts her chin. "Chal. Main le chalun tujhe teri kismat ki ore."

She stops.

"Tu nahin maanega."

The head of a wolf fills the dark, the lips drawn off the teeth. Then two more on the slope above her. Then the pack.

She is frightened. She does not run.

She stands on the valley floor, small, and they are all round her. The score goes to drums.

She turns on them. Her hair flies across her face. Her hand is up, the fingers spread.

Cut. She is low among the stones with her hair hanging over her face.

## Ananta

The ochre room at first light, the lamp still burning on the shelf and the window going grey behind it, and the wall still holding the yellow of the lamp up to the height of the flame. She lies on the divan with the child against her. Her eyes are open. There is blood on her face and on her neck, and a bangle shines on the wrist of the hand that holds the child.

"Meri beti." Her hand rests on the small back. "Adbhut. Alaukik. Akhand. Anant."

Shalini's hand comes down to steady the child.

"Ananta."

Shalini's head turns.

"Woh aadmi bhi yahi naam le raha tha." She looks at her husband. "Ananta."

He frowns down through his spectacles and says nothing.

A ruined room, lit blue. Roots hang from the roof. There is one wooden chair, and a window with no glass, and the wind comes through it. Against the far wall, on the floor, a boy sits with a large book across his knees and both arms round it. He is awake, and nobody in the room has any use for him. Tushar Sinha is dragged down between two men and put on the floor. The bald one and the man with the moustache have him by the throat.

"Bol! Hamara naash karne kaun aane waala hai? Bata! Bol, bol!"

Tushar lies on his side in the dust with his glasses still on and blood at his mouth. His mouth opens wide. He is not afraid of them. He is in pain.

"Ek nahin." He gets it out. "Hazaar vaar karo. Kal tumhare ek-ek vaar pe prahaar karne woh aayegi."

"Kaun aayegi?"

"Tum log mujhe maar sakte ho. Mera ant kar sakte ho." He lifts his head an inch. "Uska koi ant nahin. Yeh Bharat hai. Kaal Bhairav, Mahakaal ki bhoomi. Jab jab tum jaise dushman aaye hain, woh aayi hai."

"Kaun hai yeh? Batao na!"

"Na nari, na nar. Na jaanwar, na insaan. Na farishta, na shaitan. Aur na hi bhagwan." The blue light moves on the men's shoulders. "Aur bhagwan ki sabse badi bhakt, sashakt, Shiv vardani. Naaglok ki rani. Anant kul ki Naagrani. Ichchhadhari naagin. Shesh Naagin."

The ochre room.

"Shalini." The husband's voice is small. "Is bacche ka kya karenge?"

"Bachche ka kya karenge?" She has the child up against her shoulder, wrapped in white. "Matlab aise chhod toh nahin sakte na isse."

She turns so that he can see the face.

"Ise dekhiye na. Hum kitni soni lag rahi hai yeh Ananta. Iska chehra dekhiye. Jis pe Vishnu ji ki kripa aparpaar hai. Jo Shiv ji ke gale ka haar hai."

She is crying. She is smiling.

"Hey Mata Rani. Raksha karna."

The ruined room, and the bald one has Tushar Sinha's hair in his fist, pulling his head up.

"And him. There is him with her."

"Kaun?"

Tushar Sinha's lips move.

"Nagaraj of the Anant kul. Sheshnaag. King of Naaglok. Anantveer—"

The blow comes across his mouth. The next word does not come out.

The ochre room, and the lamp stands straight up, and the flame does not move at all.
