## 2025

The ghat at Prayagraj before the sun is over the water, and the sky over it, and the sky the widest thing in the country that morning. *Prayagraj. 2025.*

A boy goes along the edge of the steps with a tray of tea glasses balanced on his head, and the glasses are full and do not spill.

A woman's sari is spread on the top step to dry, held down at its four corners with bricks.

The sangam under a wide bright sky. Pontoon bridges across the water, crowds on them in every direction, tents going back to the horizon. Flags, and a helicopter crossing high above the gathering. The water is brown and wide, and the boats on it are low and crowded, and the whole river is loud.

"San 2025. Poore ek sau chauwalisaal saal baad aaya hai yeh Maha Mahakumbh." The voice is the same voice that spoke over the Kumbh of 2001, older now, and glad. "Poori duniya ke saamne Bharat ka vandan hoga. Abhinandan hoga. Bharat karega duniya ki khaatedari. Atithi devo bhava kehte hain na hamare desh mein? Yeh sirf Mahakumbh hi nahin. Bharat ke sar ka taaj hai. Poori duniya ko bula raha hai Prayagraj."

The helicopter comes across again, low enough this time to read what is painted on the tail boom, and the name on it is the name of the channel that will carry this Kumbh to the rest of the country.

In the OB van behind the ghat a feed sheet hangs from a clip on the door and swings with the door every time somebody goes in or out, and the signature at the foot of the sheet is Vihaan Suri's, put there at the London desk a month earlier. The sheet buys the satellite for four days. Nobody in the van has met the man who signed it.

The score comes up over the water, big, brass and dhol.

Cut. A ceiling fan, turning.

## The Aroras' House

The Aroras' house has brick arches in the hall, a staircase going up out of it, and photographs along the wall in frames that have been wiped every morning for thirty years without anybody ever deciding to. The newspaper lies folded on the arm of the sofa. Tea stands in a glass with a chip on the rim that Ramakant Arora has drunk out of since Amritsar. On the low table a brass tray holds two biscuits and a fly that has been walking round them since eleven.

The panel on the wall is the size of a door and painted by a man in the market: Shiva with a trident worked across him in silver, and the silver gone dull in one corner where the sun gets in.

Ramakant sits under it with his tea — grey now, a white kurta, a mustard waistcoat buttoned over a stomach he stopped apologising for years ago. His wife sits across from him in a dark printed sari, her glasses on, a shawl over one shoulder, her own paper open at the same page it has been open at for twenty minutes.

At the gate a boy in a school shirt stands with his cycle against the wall and a paper packet held up in both hands. He does not ring. He waits the way a boy waits who has been told to put it into a hand and not to leave it on a wall.

The paper on the low table has the Suri family across its front page, on the top step of that ghat with their hands together, and the caption under it gives four names. The fourth is Vihaan, and the second son in the photograph is not Vihaan: the man standing at his father's shoulder in the picture is a cousin, borrowed for the frame because the one whose name is printed there runs the London end and has not come home.

Shalini puts her paper down and goes out and comes back with it, and stands in the hall turning it over.

"Kitne hue?"

"Khaate se, dadi ji." The boy is already on the cycle. "Paisa nahin."

Shalini carries it in, and the packet goes into the drawer under the crockery shelf where the packets go, and she shuts the drawer and keeps her hand on it a second longer than the drawer needs.

"Kya hai?" He says it into his tea.

"Dawa."

"Mahine ki dawa to main laata hoon."

"Toh laate raho." She sits down. "Yeh alag hai."

He looks at the drawer, and at her, and at the panel, and lets it go the way he lets most things go now.

"So much has changed." He says it to the panel. "Hum Amritsar se Delhi aa gaye."

"Hmm."

"Lekin aap nahin badle." He turns his head. "Prayagraj ke tickets abhi bhi nahin hue hain."

"Aapko kya lagta hai?" Her paper comes up between them. "Kumbh se koi aake hamein saath le jaayega?"

"Arre Shalini ji, uski chinta na karo." He sets his cup down with a click. "Maine poora intezaam kar diya hai."

He sits back. The fly goes round the biscuits again.

"Waise"—he looks round the room—"sawere se kuch tootne ki awaaz nahin aayi. Saari gudiyaan hain kahan?"

"Ananta!" the woman calls, without raising her voice, in the direction of the stairs.

"Donon ghar pe nahin hain."

"Ghar pe nahin hain?" He is up, and then he is not, and he sits down again. "Kahan gaye donon?"

"Shopping." She turns a page. "Aapki Ananta ko jab tak woh bazaar nahin jaati na, gale ke neeche khaana nahin utarta."

"Arre, hamari padi hai Ananta."

He laughs into his tea and she says it again under her breath, and somewhere deep in the house the bell goes, twice, and neither of them moves for it.

The clock on the wall ticks. The paper slides off the arm of the sofa and he catches it without looking.

## A Son Comes Home

A gate with a nameplate on the pillar: SURI in English, and the same name under it in Gurmukhi, and the morning sun on the brass of it, thrown back at whoever looks. A car stops outside, and the driver gets out to open the back door.

The man who gets out is twenty-four. He is tall, taller than the driver, in a dark shirt with the sleeves rolled and no jacket over it, and his hair is longer than this city wears it. The face is the sort of face that stops a sentence. The jaw, the line of the cheek, the eyes under the straight brows. And nothing in it asking to be looked at.

He has one bag in his hand and a camera in its case hanging off his other shoulder. His own camera, and a good one; he has carried it since he turned nineteen.

The driver says something about the bags. The tall man shakes his head once, and takes them himself, and stands a moment on the pavement looking at the house.

Then he puts his hand flat on the nameplate as he goes past it. A man does that who has been away a long time.

Inside, the hall is full of flowers and work. The floor is marble and the flowers are in buckets on wet newspaper. Two women are arguing over a list. A caterer's boy carries a crate of glasses through, breathing hard. Somebody has moved every chair in the room. A man stands on a stool with a garland in both hands, and he is not tall enough for the hook.

Two men are carrying a panel out through the other door and it will not go, because they have it the flat way. The tall man puts his bag down, looks at the door, at the panel, at the way they hold it, and says one word: “Ghumao.” They turn it up on its side and it goes through, and he picks his bag up again.

Then the room goes quiet around him, one person at a time.

The two women stop arguing over the list. The caterer's boy walks into the corner of his own crate and catches it with his knee. The man on the stool turns round with the garland still in both hands.

Pinky sees him first and screams. "Viju!"

Then, for the room, at the top of her voice: "Vihaan!"

Nisha drops the list. "Aapne kaha tha kal!"

"Maine kaha tha main aaunga." He puts his bag down. "Kab aaunga, yeh nahin kaha tha."

Both of them are on him at once, and he stands it without moving his feet. Pinky has his face in her two hands.

"Flight early thi. Airport pe koi nahin tha." He says it over her shoulder. "Toh taxi le li."

"Airport pe koi nahin tha?" Pinky's hands go to her cheeks. "Maine Driver ko bola tha! Subah chhe baje bola tha!"

"Woh gaya. Purane terminal pe."

He is looking past her at the hall. At the flowers. At the crate of glasses. At the panel going out through the other door.

"Yeh sab kya hai?"

"Party hai. Aaj raat." Nisha is already pulling at his sleeve. "Aryaman bhai ko Journalist of the Year mil raha hai. Teesri baar. Unhein pata nahin ki hamein pata hai. Humne unka poora newsroom mandap bana diya hai. Woh humein daantenge, aur phir bahar aayenge, kyunki Papa aur Baljeet uncle channel aa rahe hain."

"Unhein pata hai?"

"Unhein pata chal jaayega."

Pinky pulls him by his free arm. "Tu hamare saath chal raha hai. Humein ek aadmi chahiye. Lights latkaani hain aur seedhi pakadne waala koi nahin hai."

She takes his face in both hands again and turns it to the light.

"Look at him. Is chehre ko dekho." She pushes his hair back off his forehead with her thumb. "Arre, London ne hamare ladke ke saath kya kiya hai."

Pinky turns to the room with him still in her hands.

"Aur jaante ho yeh wahan kya kar raha tha? Chala raha tha. London ka office. Baa-is saal ki umar se. Papa ke aadmi se behtar." She holds up one finger. "Aur ghar aaya hai is purane shirt mein. Log kya kahenge, hum apne hi bhai ko khaana nahin khilaate?"

"Shirt saaf hai."

"Saaf hai aur bin mein jaayegi." She lets go of him. "Do minute. Phir tu neeche aayega, aur kuch aur pehen ke aayega."

"Main nau ghante plane mein tha."

"Tu saalon London mein tha, bhai. Nau ghante kuch nahin hai."

He looks at the two of them, and at the hall, and he does not argue.

"Papa kahan hain?"

"Subah se phone pe." Nisha waves at the stairs, where a door stands shut. "Delhi ke sab log Kumbh ke baare mein unhein call kar rahe hain. Tu bag apne room mein rakh. Phir neeche aa, hum chalenge."

On the wall by the stairs there are photographs in frames. Two boys at a cricket match. A family at a wedding. A big house with a lawn.

He goes up, and the house goes on shouting under him.

## The Room on the Fourth Floor

The fourth floor of the Suri house at four in the afternoon, and the sun at the end of the corridor lying in a long shape on the landing floor.

His room is at the end of the corridor. The door has his initials on a brass plate, and the plate has been polished and the door frame around it has not. The bed inside is made up and has never been slept in.

The room at the top of the house has been kept ready. Sheets turned down, the pillow square. A jug of water with a glass upside down over its mouth. The window is open on the street, and the inside of its frame has been wiped and the wall around it not.

The shelf holds a boy's things. A wooden elephant. A cardboard tube with certificates rolled in it. A photograph of a birthday: a baby in a paper cap with one hand flat on the icing of a cake whose single candle stands unlit, and two grown-ups laughing at him from behind the table. In the photograph the cake is never cut.

He puts the bag down at the foot of the bed and does not open it. He opens the camera case instead, takes the body out, and sets it on the desk where the lamp ought to be standing.

The fan turns. The curtain moves. Down in the street a dog barks twice at nothing and stops.

Steps on the stairs, quick, and Nisha in the doorway with a plate of pakoras in one hand.

"Bag neeche rakha hai aur bag khol diya hai." She comes in and sits on the arm of the chair. "Bas itna hi kiya hai tune. Papa poochenge ki kapde almirah mein hain ya nahin, aur mujhe jhoot bolna padega."

"Toh jhoot bol de."

"Main bahut achhi hoon us kaam mein." She holds the plate out. He takes one, and eats it standing. "Aur tu itna lamba ho gaya hai ki ab chhat problem hai. Is ghar mein."

Her eye goes to the desk. She is up off the chair and has the camera in both hands before he can say anything, and holds it out at arm's length the wrong way round, at him.

"Nisha."

"Kuch bol. Tere chehre pe suit karega. Pinky kehti hai teri photograph deewar pe lagwaani chahiye."

He leans over and turns the lens ring, and the screen at the back of it comes on, and he stands still while she makes four seconds of film of him in his own room, holding her breath over it.

"Yeh bahut achha hai," she says. "Main bahut achhi hoon is kaam mein."

"Chaar second banaye hain."

"Apne bhai ke chaar second bahut hote hain." She gives it back. "Ab neeche chal. Gaadi aa rahi hai. Papa ki awaaz oonchi ho gayi hai, aur subah se Delhi ke sab log unhein Kumbh ke baare mein call kar rahe hain."

"Kumbh ka kaam kaisa hai?"

"Jahan woh humein sun nahin sakte?" She pulls at his sleeve the way she pulled at it in the hall, and goes out. "Bahut kharab. Aur teri desk bhi, Monday se. Kursi laga di hai unhone."

Through the floor a man's voice on a telephone, in the English a Delhi man uses on a telephone, then the same voice in Punjabi, and a chair going back.

He puts the camera in its case over his shoulder. He leaves the bag where it lies at the foot of the bed, shut and nothing of it unpacked, and pulls the door of his room shut on a made bed and a jug of water.

## The Study

The first floor, and a door with a brass plate that says OFFICE on it, and inside a room that smells of new carpet and cold coffee and has its tube light on over the desk and its window shut behind the chair.

Parmeet Suri sits behind the desk with his glasses on the bridge of his nose and a folder open under one hand. Baljeet stands at the end of the desk with a pen in his fingers and nothing to write on.

"Baith." Parmeet does not look up.

Vihaan sits in the chair by the window instead of the one in front of the desk, and turns it so that he can see the door.

"Kumbh ka coverage hum karenge." Parmeet turns a page. "Poore desh ka channel. Baljeet ne teen hafte ka roster banaya hai. Sangam pe do OB vans, chaubees ghante. Main chahta hoon ki jab woh ghadi chale, channel pe hum hon."

"Aap chahte hain ki koi Pandit ji bolein, ya aap chahte hain ki koi gine?" Vihaan's hands are on his knees. "Sangam pe teen crore log honge, Papa. Jab giro, tab channel ka naam kisi ko yaad nahin rahega."

"Crowd management hamara kaam nahin hai." Baljeet does not raise his eyes from his pen.

"Hamara kaam khabar hai." Vihaan says it to Baljeet. "Khabar ka pehla farz yeh hai ki jo aapke aadmi pe khada hai, woh gire na."

Parmeet looks up at him over his glasses, and looks at him for a moment longer than the sentence deserves.

"Tu London se aaya hai," he says. "Toh London ki baat kar." He shuts the folder and slides a second one across the desk. "Yeh chaar saal ke accounts hain. Read them, initial every page, and sign the last one."

Vihaan opens it where it lies. He reads the way he reads a script, a finger going down the left-hand column, and the room goes quiet around the sound of the pages.

On the fourth page there is a payment out, twice a year, four years running, to a consultancy with an address in Dubai and no name of a person on it.

"Yeh kaun hai?"

"Woh tujhe jaanne ki zaroorat nahin hai."

"Main sign karunga isko." Vihaan's finger stays on the line. "Aur main iske saamne likhunga *bina vivaran*. Do lafz. Aapke hisaab mein jhooth nahin hoga, aur mere hisaab mein sach hoga."

Baljeet's pen stops.

Parmeet takes his glasses off and puts them down on the desk and does not say anything for a while.

"Kar." He picks his glasses up again. "Aur aaj raat party mein iski baat kisi se nahin hogi. Aryaman ke din pe."

"Haan." Vihaan writes it. Two words, in ink, in the margin, twice. Then his name at the foot of the page, and the date under it.

"Monday se teri desk chalu hai." Parmeet is already reading something else. "Teesri manzil, lift ke saamne. Log tumhein dekhne aayenge. Unhein dikhna bhi padega."

"Main agle hafte Prayagraj ja raha hoon."

The room stops. Baljeet's head comes up.

"Aryaman ke award ke baad?"

"Uske baad. Gaadi se. Do din." Vihaan shuts the folder and stands with it under his arm. "Aapne kaha channel Kumbh karega. Main apni aankh se dekhe bina us camp ka layout nahin likh sakta. Do din ka kaam hai."

"Baljeet se poochh ke—"

"Main Baljeet se poochh ke nahin ja raha." He says it evenly, and it is the first thing in that house that has not been asked as a favour. "Main aapko bata raha hoon."

He goes out. The door shuts on the two of them, and through it, after a moment, Parmeet's voice begins again, low, in Punjabi, and it is not talking about the Kumbh.

## The Bazaar

A Delhi market in the afternoon. Tarpaulin overhead, and the light coming through it orange. Onions in a heap, marigolds in a basket, brass lamps in rows on a cloth. A woman at a fruit cart, flies, a boy with a crate on his head. The noise of a hundred people buying.

The score comes up on a dhol.

A scooter's horn, and the crowd opens down the middle of the lane, and the gap stands there with nobody in it.

Ananta comes through it. A lemon-yellow sari, dark hair loose, sunglasses pushed up on her head as she looks into the stalls, a bag on one arm, her sandals quick on the stones. She is talking before she arrives.

"Purvi! Purvi, sun na—"

Behind her, hurrying, a girl in a salwar kameez with her spectacles slipping down her nose and her dupatta coming off her shoulder. She has a bag in one hand and a shopping list in the other and she is reading the list and walking.

She is wearing the brown cardigan and the green muffler, which go with her in every weather, and the muffler is coming off one shoulder and she has not noticed. Her spectacles are the big dark-blue ones she has had since school and are always sliding. A notepad hangs from a string round her neck, and it is what she calls her calendar, and it is full of other people's errands.

Purvi is twenty-three and does not look it in this lane, where the girls are painted by four and married by twenty. She is a head shorter than her sister and built small and square, with a great weight of hair she pins up in a hurry, a short straight nose, and a mouth that gives her away before the rest of her face does. Her hands are quick and always full of something. She wears her spectacles pushed up on her nose with a finger, thirty times an hour, and it is the one gesture of hers that the whole family can imitate.

The bazaar turns to look. The onion man, the fruit woman, the boy with the crate. Ananta goes past all of it, and the light off the tarpaulin moves over her as she goes.

The snake charmer sits on a square of matting with his been at his lips. He is old, and his eyes are shut, and the crowd stands round him at a distance that has been agreed.

"Nagraj, bahar aa jao."

The note goes out of the been, long and flat, and holds.

"Nagraj."

The basket's lid lifts from inside.

"Naagin ki naagin!" The crowd says it for him, half of them, their hands going up. "Har har har har har!"

The cobra comes out of the basket and crosses the stones with its head up. A woman screams and holds her shopping to her chest. A man pulls a boy back by the shoulder. The crowd leans in and back at the same time.

"Madam, jaldi se is naag ko pitare mein rakh do!"

Ananta has put her bag down. She goes down on her heels in front of the cobra and holds out her hand.

"Arre, Yeh Sheshnaag hain." She says it over her shoulder, to the charmer, without turning her head. "Sheshnaag ne poori duniya ko apne fan pe rakha hai. Aur tum ise is pitare mein band karna chahte ho?"

"Madam—" The charmer's been does not stop. "Agar ise band nahin kiya toh yeh zaroor kisi ko das lega."

"Naag Shiv ji ke favourite hote hain." The snake comes up out of the stones and puts its hood into her open hand. "Yeh bezubaan hote hain. Yeh woh dekh sakte hain jo koi nahin dekh sakta. Agar apne paas rakhna hai inhein, toh inka khyaal rakho."

Ananta carries the cobra to the end of the row, where a small Shivling stands in a niche under a tin hood with a wet bel leaf on it, and sets it down, and it stays there, and its hood goes down.

Behind the shutters a song starts up from a shop's radio, women's voices on it, plain and old, the way a temple sings it: *Karpur Gauram, Karuna Avtaram.*

Her head is down, and she is looking at the snake, and she is smiling.

"Har Har Mahadev! Har Har Mahadev! Har Har Mahadev!"

The crowd takes it up, all of them at once, and the cymbals go, and the score goes with them and stays up. Purvi has both hands on the strap of her bag and her head to one side, watching her sister with the snake in her palm, and she is not breathing.

The cobra goes back into the basket. The lid shuts. The been stops.

"Ay, hay, hay!" The stout woman in the yellow salwar suit pushes through to Ananta's side and takes her hand, and the whole bazaar is looking. "Ananta, tu jitna bhi jaanti hai na, jaadu hi kar diya. Aaj toh tune saanp pe bhi jaadu kar diya."

"Aur tu kadi nahin hai." Ananta says it from behind her, sweetly. "Poori jaadu ki chhadi hai."

"Chhadi!" The woman is pleased with that, and turns to tell the onion man, and the crowd closes.

Purvi has picked up her bag and her list, and she is looking back over her shoulder at the basket, and there is a small line between her brows that will still be there when she is an old woman. Ananta takes her by the wrist and pulls her on.

## The Jhumkas

The lane of the jewellers at eleven in the morning, light coming down it between the awnings and lying in stripes across the counters. Glass cases, and a man sitting cross-legged behind one of them with a cloth over his knees, and a mirror propped at the end of the counter.

"Arre o Purvi." Ananta is at the next case already. "Paise toh de diye. Jhumka nahin leke jaana hai kya?"

"Sorry, sorry, sorry!" Purvi comes back along the counter with her hands up. "Didi, yeh dekho—"

"Teri behen toh Kailash parvat ki thand se bhi zyada thandi hai." Ananta is holding a pair up to her own ears in the mirror. "BMW ke zamaane mein auto rickshaw hai. Woh dekh, woh ja rahi teri auto."

"Arre hato bhaiya!"

The pair Purvi has found is small, gold, with a bell hanging under each flower. She holds them up, and the counter lamp catches them, and the whole case lights up.

"Yahi waale chahiye the na?"

"Yahi toh main dhoond rahi thi tab se." She is already putting one to her ear. "Aaj shaam ki party mein yahi jhumke pehnungi."

The man behind the counter takes down a register with a cloth cover and writes the figure in it and turns it round, and holds out the pen.

"Naam, madam. Address."

Ananta signs it, and under her name she writes the channel's name, because the money came from there this month and not from the house. The man copies the address down out of her mouth and puts the book back under the counter with his thumb in the page.

Her phone lies face up on the glass while that is happening. Four taps, and a sum of money goes out of an account with the channel's name on it to a travel agent two lanes away, for two tickets to Prayagraj in February. Then the phone goes face down on the counter, and it is face down before the man comes back with her change.

"Aaram se dekh ke." Ananta takes her chin and turns her face to the mirror. "Theek hai. Haan. Yahin pe reh."

The mirror. The earrings in her ears, and her own face between them, and behind her shoulder her sister's face watching her in the glass, and the shop, and the street, and the whole bazaar moving behind the two of them.

"I swear, Purvi." Ananta says it to the mirror, not to her. "Ek baar woh ladke mil jaayein na, jinhone aadi daal ke tujhe giraya hai. Main unko chhodungi nahin."

"Woh chhodo." Purvi takes the second earring. "Yeh batao, aapke news channel se mera interview call kyun nahin aaya abhi tak?"

"Purvi, tu kyun itna pareshaan hoti hai?" Ananta turns her by the shoulders. "Maine bola hai na, sab kuch ho jaayega. Mat fikr kar."

"Ho toh jaayega."

The words come out flat. Ananta looks at her, and her face changes, and she is about to say something else, and the phone in her hand rings instead.

"Papa?" She puts it to her ear. "Haan, Papa. Kuch nahin hua hai. Woh thoda sa—" She turns away with her hand up. "Chot lag gayi usse. Market mein gir gayi thi na."

Purvi's eyes come up, and stay on her sister's back.

"Achha, sun, de deti hoon." Ananta covers the phone. "Purvi, tune Mahakumbh ke liye Prayagraj se tickets book kar diye na?"

Purvi's mouth opens. Ananta's eyes go wide and warn her, and Purvi's mouth shuts.

"Kya hua?" The phone is loud. "Kya bol rahi hai?"

"Woh—" Ananta turns back to the phone, and her voice goes up into the sweetness it uses on her father. "Woh, Papa, ek minute—"

"Tune tickets kar diye na?"

Purvi's hand goes slowly into her bag, and comes out with the phone, and the screen of it is lit.

## Home: The Tickets

The Arora hall in the evening. A calendar hangs on the wall by the door, with a picture of a temple on it and the months in small red squares. A storm has come up outside; the curtains move, and the tube light over the door flickers once.

"Toh, beta." The old man is standing in the middle of the room with his hands behind his back, in the voice he uses for evidence. "Tickets book karwaaye na aapne?"

Purvi is still in her sandals, her list in her hand, and behind her Ananta stands with her arms crossed and her mouth pressed shut.

"Papa—"

"Arre beta, jo gale mein yeh calendar leke ghoomti ho, ismein kuch toh likha hoga na?"

"Haan haan haan!" She looks down at it. "Ek minute ruko, abhi batati hoon."

She turns the calendar over, and looks at the back of it, and her face goes through several things.

"Hua kya?"

"Sorry." Her voice is very small. "Likhna bhool gayi."

The old man's hands come out from behind his back.

"Batao zara." He is coming across the room. "Ab kaise ho gaye Mahakumbh ke darshan? Ticket nahin karai, isliye!"

"Arre ho jaayega—"

"Ho jaayega?" He rounds on her, and his voice is loud, and the whole room is listening, and Ananta's hand is over her mouth. "Ticket, ticket, ticket, Papa ji! Ticket aapka pyaara bachcha hoon! Yeh aapki Purvi ki tarah nahin hoon! Ghajini, jo baat pe baat hi bhool jaati hai. Buddhu kahin ki. Yeh banegi journalist!"

"Papa." Purvi's phone is in her hand. "Ek booking rakhi hai. Do din ke liye."

"Kis naam pe?"

"Naam pe nahin." She holds the screen up and her hand is not steady. "Deposit aa chuka hai. Maine nahin diya."

The old man looks at the phone. The house is quiet in a new way.

"Toh kisne diya?"

Nobody answers him.

Behind Purvi her sister's mouth is shut and stays shut, and her eyes are on the biscuit tray and not on her father, and the rain on the window is loud enough that nobody in that room hears how careful her breathing has become.

Ananta's hand comes down off her mouth.

The thunder comes then, close, and the house jumps, and the tube light goes out and comes back.

Purvi's face has gone tight. She turns and goes up the stairs with her hand on the rail, and her shoulders are up around her ears.

Ananta catches her at the top of the landing.

"Mummy, mummy, mummy—" She takes her by the arm. "Kya hua? Tujhe zukaam ho raha hai?"

"Nahin." Purvi wipes her face with the back of her wrist. "Main ro rahi hoon."

Ananta takes her face in both hands, and pulls her in, and holds her, and says nothing at all.

"Tu buddhu nahin hai." She says it into her sister's hair. "Tu na ek number ki stupid hai. Aur main tujhe woh job dilwa ke rahungi."

## Two Women

The Arora house from the street at night. The gate, the small Shivling in its niche by the wall, the window lit above, and the rain going down through the light of it in small straight lines.

The lamp in the niche burns in front of the stone, and its flame stands straight and then bends and then stands again. The storm going over the rooftops, and the dust moving along the road in front of it.

Two women stand on the far side of the road with their heads covered. They do not move in the wind. One of them has her hands folded at her chest.

"How long do we watch?"

The younger one does not look away from the lit window.

"Jitni dawa is ghar mein aati hai, utne mein poora mohalla khada ho jaata." She says it to the window. "Aur is ghar mein koi kamaata nahin."

"Kitne hi mahine ho gaye." The taller one's voice is low. "Hum kitne hi mahinon se apni Maharani par nazar rakh rahe hain. Par aaj unhone hamein dekh hi liya."

In the lit window a girl in a lemon sari comes up to the glass and looks out at the road, pushing the curtain aside with one finger, and the streetlamps catch in her eyes.

The two women go down to the ground where they stand.

"2025 aa chuka hai." The taller one speaks from the ground, with her palms together. "Woh waqt aa chuka hai jab Mahayuddh hoga. Aur unhein toh pata bhi nahin ki woh us Mahayuddh ka kitna bada hissa banegi. Us Mahayuddh ki Mahayodha banegi woh."

"Haan."

"Aur is desh ko bachaayengi."

"Anant kul ki Mahanagin ko"—both of them, together, heads down—"hum ichchhadari naaginon ka sakshat pranam."

When the window is looked at again, the road is empty. The dust goes along it. Somewhere a dog barks twice at nothing and stops.

## The Inhaler

A bedroom with a mirror, a dressing table, a bed with four pillows, and a photograph of the two sisters on the wall in old frames. The lamp is on. The storm is against the window, and there is a towel folded along the sill where the rain comes in, and the towel is wet through.

"Bey!" A fist on the door. "Darwaza kholo! Inhaler chahiye!"

"Bey, darwaza khula hai!" Ananta, from inside, with a sari half over her arm. "Kab se inhaler dhoond rahi ho? Inhaler nahin mil raha. Tum darwaza nahin khol rahi ho—"

On the dressing table the lid of a tin is off, and there are two inhalers standing in it, and one of them is spent.

Two hands come through the doorway, find the handle, find the inhaler on the dressing table, and it is Purvi, in a dressing gown, her hair up in a knot, her breath coming in short and not easing.

"Yeh raha."

She sits down on the end of the bed with the inhaler in her hand and uses it, and watches her sister in the mirror while she does it.

"Aap kahin ja rahe ho?"

"Oh ho, Purvi." Ananta does not turn round from the mirror. "Tu bhi na, sab kuch bhool jaati hai. Maine bataya tha na, meri office mein party hai aaj. Mere boss ko ek minister ke sting operation ke liye journalism ka sabse bada award milne wala hai. Ab tak announce nahin hua hai, lekin hum sab office mein sure hain ki award unko hi milega."

"Teri life kitni pretty hai na." She says it without any bitterness at all. "Tu khud bhi toh kitni pretty hai. Actually tu na genius hai, smart hai, sab hai. Poora ghar sambhaalti hai."

The sari comes off its hanger behind her.

"Ek tu hai jise sab dekhte reh jaate hain." Purvi's eyes are on the mirror. "Aur ek main hoon jise koi nahin dekhta."

Ananta stops.

"Tu na bahut pyaari hai." She comes and sits down behind her, and puts her chin on her sister's shoulder, and looks at the two of them in the mirror. "Aur meri jaan hai. Aur itni zyada pretty hai ki main kya bataun. Sach mein."

The towel has come off the sill and lies in a heap on the floor with the rain in it.

"Jhooth."

"Sach." Ananta gets up and pulls her off the bed by both hands. "Chal, ab jaldi se ready ho ja. Tujhe party mein chalna hai mere saath."

Purvi stops in the middle of the floor. "Main? Party mein? Main kya karungi?"

"Tujhe chahiye na?" Ananta is at the mirror, doing her earring. "Tujhe mere news channel mein job chahiye na? Main tujhe woh job dilwa ke rahungi."

"Tum kaise job dilwaaogi?" Purvi sits down again. "Tum toh fashion designer ho. Anchors ke kapde banati ho. Tumko koi importance kyun?"

"Importance denge." The earring goes in. "Tujhe job chahiye na. Main dilwa ke rahungi. Tu baith aur taiyaar ho ja."

"Tum toh aise bol rahi ho jaise koi jaadu kar dogi."

"Jaadu ka toh pata nahin." Ananta turns round, and the score comes up under her, and she puts her hand on her hip. "Phir kuch super powers hain mere paas."

The two of them look at each other. Purvi's face changes first, and then Ananta's, and then they are both laughing, and the thunder comes again, further off this time, and the lamp does not so much as move.

## Two Houses, One Bulletin

On the television in the next room, the volume goes up, and the light of the screen comes round the doorframe and lies on the floor of the hall in a long blue shape that changes when the picture changes.

"Poora Bharat desh ki nazar aaj hamare channel par hai. Jald hum announce karenge ki 2025 ka Journalist of the Year Award ka winner kaun hai. Lagataar teesri baar—" The screen fills with a face, and applause, and a name in gold under it. "—is Aryaman Suri."

The gold goes off the screen and the studio comes back, wide, with a map behind the anchor, and the bulletin carries on into its next line.

"Aur is saal Maha Mahakumbh ka poora coverage hamare channel se hoga. Sangam par chaubees ghante, do OB vans, aur poora intezaam sambhaalenge—" The anchor reads the last of it off the prompter the way the end of a list is read, without any weight on it at all. "—Vihaan Suri."

In the bedroom the inhaler has stopped halfway to Purvi's mouth.

Her sister is in the doorway with a dupatta over her arm, and neither of them says anything, because neither of them has heard the name before. The storm goes off the roof. On the screen the map fills with water and pontoon bridges and tents going back to the horizon, and the channel's own name goes past on the tail boom of a helicopter.

In the Suri house a woman in the hall stops in the middle of a sentence with a glass in her hand.

"Woh mera beta hai." Pinky says it to the hall and to the Khuranas and to the television. "Poora Mahakumbh uske haath mein hai. Chhote sahab. London se aaya hai aur dekho, do din mein—"

Parmeet comes down the last four stairs with his phone at his ear and stands at the bottom of them.

"Baljeet." He turns his back to the room. "Bulletin mein ladke ka naam gaya hai. Kisne pass kiya? … Achha. Toh sun." His voice does not go up, and men in that house lower theirs when they are being listened to. "Aaj raat ka jo bhi hai, kal subah se us naam pe koi kagaz nahin jaayega. Aur jo aadmi line clear karta hai, uski galti file mein likhi jaayegi. Aaj raat. Apne haath se."

He puts the phone away and goes into the hall, and the party takes him.

Vihaan stands at the foot of the stairs with his jacket over his arm, and he has heard all of it. Behind him the applause for his brother comes out of the room, and he does not clap.

Then he goes up, and on the fourth stair he stops and opens the folder and reads the two lines written in the margin in his own hand, twice, and shuts it again.

A man whose name has just been said on that channel will be asked for his signature before the week is out. So he does not put the folder back in the office almirah.

Vihaan carries it up past the photographs and puts it in the bottom drawer of the desk in his own room, under the camera's spare battery and a cardboard tube of certificates, and turns the key in it.

Downstairs the television is still on, and somebody has turned it up again.
