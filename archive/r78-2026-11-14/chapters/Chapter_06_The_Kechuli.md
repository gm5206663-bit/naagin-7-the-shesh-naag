## The Night of Purnima

The lane outside the Arora house is empty and the lamp at the corner is on. Over the roofs the moon stands with nothing in front of it, and the whole of the street is white with it.

On the first floor a window stands open. The moonlight comes into the room in one block and lies over the floor and the foot of the bed.

A bottle of water stands on the table with the cap off, and her spectacles lie folded on the book beside it. Her calendar hangs from the bedpost by its string, and turns a little, and turns back.

Purvi sleeps with her arm across her face.

Then her arm comes down, and her eyes are open.

"Hum chaah ke bhi unki madad nahin kar sakte." The voice is not in that room. It is in a hall of stone, where the lamps stand straight and a bell has begun to move on its rope. "Unhein is dard se guzarna hi hoga. Yeh dard unhein bahut takleef dega."

Her back comes up off the mattress.

"Anant kul ki naagin ka janm hone waala hai."

Her mouth is open and nothing comes out of it. Her heels drum once on the sheet. Her hand goes out and takes the bedpost and her fingers go into the wood of it, and the calendar swings and knocks and swings again.

In the lane below a dog starts up and does not stop.

"Purvi?"

The house is dark and quiet and the fan goes round.

"Aaj usey uski jadein yaad aayi."

Her feet find the mat. Her knees do not. She goes down on the floor and lies on her side with her cheek on the mat and her eyes open and her breath going in and out.

"Yeh mandir. Yeh gaddi. Usey bula rahi hai."

Her hand goes up over the edge of the bed and finds the strap of her bag and pulls. The bag comes over and everything in it goes across the floor: the diary, the pen, the coins, the small steel box, the inhaler.

She has the inhaler in her hand. Her fingers will not close round it. They close, and they open, and it rolls away under the bed.

"Purvi?"

The moonlight lies on the floor beside her face and stays there.

"Purvi!"

A door below. A light under it. Her mother's slippers on the stairs, and her father's voice behind her mother's, and the dog going on and on.

Purvi gets up on her hands and knees. The pain comes again, and this time it is longer. Her hand finds the leg of the bed and holds it, and her heels go along the mat and stop, and her head goes back, and the veins stand in her throat.

This time it comes out of her. One long sound, and it does not stop, and the dog in the lane goes mad with it.

"Purvi!"

Then it lets go. She lies still on the floor and breathes, and the sound goes on in the room after she has stopped making it.

"Ananta! Purvi!"

She gets up. She holds the bed and pulls and stands, and the room goes over to one side and comes back.

Her spectacles stay where they are, open on the book. She goes to the door and gets it open and goes out into the corridor.

The corridor is dark, and the moonlight lies in it in strips from the windows. The pictures on the wall go past her one after another: a wedding, a school function, a girl in a paper crown.

At the head of the stairs her mother stands in her nightdress with one hand on the banister and her hair loose. She does not come on.

"Purvi. Bey, kya hua? Kya hai ye?"

Purvi goes past her along the wall, and her mother turns where she stands and does not follow her and does not go down.

Her shoulder takes the wall. Then it takes the wall again.

"Didi." Her voice comes out wrong. "Didi!"

The door at the end opens before she reaches it. Ananta stands there with the light behind her and her plait over one shoulder, and her face goes from sleep to something else in one moment.

"Purvi—"

Purvi holds her arms out.

The marks have come up. Four on one forearm and three on the other, and one across the wrist. Bruises, black as ink, and a minute ago there is nothing there at all.

"Didi, mujhe nahin pata kya ho raha hai."

"Andar aa. Andar aa ja." Ananta takes her by the wrists and pulls her in and shuts the door and stands with her back against it. "Ye kya hai? Ye kya hai, Purvi?"

"Nahin pata. Didi, mujhe nahin pata."

Ananta takes her face in both hands and turns it to the lamp and looks at her, and looks at her, and puts her own hand over her mouth.

"Ananta!" Her father's voice, in the corridor. "Ananta, darwaza kholo."

"Ek minute, Papa."

"Nahin." Feet come along the corridor, and stop outside the door. "Ananta. Darwaza kholo. Abhi kholo."

Ananta looks at the door, and then at her sister, and she does not open it.

## The Mirror

Ananta's room. The mirror with the bulbs round it. Creams in a row. A lamp with a beaded shade. Her sari from that evening over the back of a chair where she threw it.

Purvi sits on the edge of the bed with her sleeves pushed up and her arms held out in front of her. The marks have gone darker.

"Koi nahin jaanta." The voice comes from nowhere in that room and from every corner of it at once. "Woh khud bhi nahin jaanti ki woh naagin hai. Koi aur dekhe ya na dekhe, aaina dekhta hai. Woh naagin ka asli chehra dikha deta hai."

Purvi gets up and goes to the dressing table.

"Purvi."

Purvi stands in front of the glass and looks at herself.

The bulbs are off and the only light is the moon through the window. In the glass there is a room, and a bed with the sheet thrown back, and a girl with her plait coming loose, and a face. Her face. The marks on her arms. The wall behind her with the photograph on it.

Purvi looks. The glass holds all of it, and holds it, and holds it.

Her hand goes out and she puts her palm flat on the mirror. The glass is warm under her hand.

"Purvi." Ananta is behind her, low. "Kya hai? Kya dekh rahi ho?"

She does not answer. In the glass the room stays the room: the wall, the photograph, the woman standing behind her with her hand at her mouth.

Then Purvi turns round, and there is nothing behind her but Ananta.

She looks back at the glass. She looks at it for a long time.

"Kya dekha?" Ananta says.

"Kuch nahin." Purvi's hand comes down off the mirror. "Kuch nahin, didi. Mujhe— mujhe laga kuch hai. Kuch nahin hai."

## Ice

Ananta's room at two in the night with the lamp on and the fan turning and the window open on the lane, and its light lying up the wall.

A steel tray stands on the floor with the ice melting in it and a spoon standing in the ice.

Ananta has the towel round the ice now, and she holds it against her sister's forearm, and Purvi sits with her other arm round her knees and her teeth set.

"Kya hua? Kya hua? Kya hua?" Ananta turns the arm over and over. "Kya ho gaya ye? Sab chal raha hai ye. Chal, tu chal, yahan mere saath chal. Main barf lagati hoon. Baith. Baith yahan."

"Itni thandi hai."

"Thandi achhi hai. Thandi matlab kaam kar raha hai." She wraps the towel round and holds it. "Dard ho raha hai?"

"Ab nahin."

Somewhere in the house a tap is running, and nobody turns it off.

"Tu chilla rahi thi, Purvi. Poore ghar ne suna."

"Main nahin chilla rahi thi."

"Tu chilla rahi thi." Ananta wipes her own face with her free hand. "Maine aisi aawaaz tere se kabhi nahin suni."

On the bed between them there is a sheet of paper and a pen, and the paper has a drawing on it. A seat with a high back. The back carved. The arm of it, and the step at the foot of it. It is drawn from the side, quickly, with the pen pressed hard enough to go through in one place.

Ananta picks it up. "Ye kya hai?"

"Maine kuch dekha." Purvi takes the paper back and looks at it. "Haan, maine kuch dekha. Ek nishaan, jo nishaan maine paper pe draw kiya tha. Ek— ek singhasan."

"Ek singhasan?" Ananta looks at the paper, and then at her sister. "Purvi, tu kya bolti rehti hai? Ek singhasan, ek painting. Tu Maharani hai kya koi?"

Purvi does not laugh.

"Pata nahin, didi." Her voice is low and steady and it does not stop. "Mujhe nahin pata ye sab mujhe kya dikh raha hai aur kyun dikh raha hai. Par kuch toh hai. Koi toh hai jo mujhe apni aur bula raha hai. Koi aawaaz hai jo mujhe apni aur kheench rahi hai. Aisa lag raha hai jaise mujhe wahan jaana hai. Jaise meri zindagi, meri kahani, us aawaaz se judi hai. Koi hai jo mujhe baar-baar bula raha hai."

"Kaun bula raha hai?"

"Nahin pata." She looks up. "Yahi toh nahin pata."

The door of the room is open a hand's width. In the corridor their mother stands with one hand on the frame and the other at her throat, and behind her their father in his kurta with his glasses in his hand.

Neither of them comes in.

"Ananta." Their mother's voice is small. "Kya hai isko? Mere bachche ko kya ho raha hai?"

"Kuch nahin, Mummy. Kuch nahin ho raha." Ananta is up and at the door with her hands out. "Neeche jao. Aap dono neeche jao. Main hoon na iske saath. Thodi der mein le aati hoon."

The mother does not move. She looks past Ananta at the girl on the bed with the towel round her arm and the paper in her hand.

Then the father puts his hand on his wife's shoulder and turns her, and they go down the corridor, and their slippers go away on the stairs.

Ananta shuts the door and stands with her back to it.

"Purvi." She comes back to the bed and sits down. "Woh haadse ka asar tera dimaag par ho gaya hai. Bas. Tu gir gayi, sar pe chot lagi, aur ab tujhe cheezein dikh rahi hain."

"Didi—"

"Tu yahan baith." Ananta takes the towel and puts it back in the ice. "Main tere liye dawaai lekar aati hoon."

"Abhi theek ho jayega?"

"Abhi theek ho jayega." Ananta's hand is on her head. "Yahin baith. Kuch mat socho. Main aati hoon."

## London Hours

The Suri house at four in the morning. The terrace is wet with the night, and the water tank in the corner ticks as it cools. Below, the city is dark with little lights in it, and the moon is over all of it.

A plastic chair. A laptop open on a stool. A printout of figures on the floor with a pen lying across it, and a cup with the tea gone cold at the bottom.

Vihaan sits with one headphone in and the other hanging down his shirt.

"June ka figure dobara bolo." He turns the printout round with one finger. "Nahin. Delhi ka figure theek hai. London waala galat hai."

Vihaan takes the pen and circles a number.

"Gyarah lakh chalees. Apni sheet dekho. Ek hi figure do baar hai aur mahina ek nahin hai." He waits. "Toh column dhoondo aur mujhe bhej do. Meeting se pehle main sign kar doonga."

Vihaan writes something at the foot of the sheet and underlines it twice.

"Haan. Good night." A pause. "Yahan subah hai."

Vihaan takes the headphone out and puts it down on the laptop and rubs his eyes with the heel of his hand.

Then he sits back and looks at the moon.

Vihaan has been awake nineteen hours and the moon is the only thing in this house that has asked nothing of him. Downstairs his father is asleep with the Kumbh figures on his chest, and his brother is asleep with an award on a shelf, and the books he has just corrected are wrong, and the wrongness is his to hold until the morning, and there is nobody to tell at four o'clock.

It stands over the roofs with nothing in front of it, and the terrace floor is white with it. He looks at it for a while with his hands behind his head.

"Viju?"

Vihaan turns. Pinky comes up the last steps with a shawl over her dressing gown and her hair loose, and her slippers in her hand so that nobody hears her.

"Hey Bhagwan. Look at you." She stands at the top of the stairs and looks at her youngest brother with her whole face, the way she has looked at him since the day he came home in a shawl. "Chaar baje subah, chhat pe, watchman ki tarah. Aur us chehre ke saath. Agar office ki ladkiyon ne tumhein nau baje is chehre ke saath dekh liya na, toh kal koi kaam nahin karega. Kisi ke haath se kuch gir jaayega."

"Office ne mujhe pehle bhi dekha hai."

"Toh pehle bhi kisi ke haath se kuch gira hoga." She comes over and sits on the parapet with her knees together. "Kyun jaga hua hai? Phir wahi office?"

"London gyarah baje raat ko band hota hai. Unka din khatm hota hai jab meri raat shuru hoti hai."

"Toh unka din khatm ho, aur tu so ja." She points at the sky. "Sooraj aise hi chalta hai, Viju."

He picks the printout up and shows her the column with the circle on it. "Saal ki books. London ka hissa. Ek galti hai aur June se hai."

"Kya galti?"

"Gyarah lakh chalees, hamare haq mein. Kisi ne ek hi figure do mahine mein daal diya." He puts the paper down. "Nau baje correction ke saath jaayega, aur mere naam ke saath."

"Papa tumhein raat ka paisa nahin dete."

"Woh mujhe kisi cheez ka paisa nahin dete. Ghar ka office hai."

Pinky laughs, and then stops laughing and pulls the shawl up round her shoulders. "Papa tumhein ek Sunday us channel pe le gaye the, jab tum chhote the. Poori building khaali, aur tum anchor ki kursi pe." She looks at the side of his face. "Andar unke kya kaha tha tumse?"

He is quiet for a moment. "Kehne the ki woh kursi kama sakta hoon."

"Woh kehne the." She nods slowly. "Toh din mein kama, Viju. Chaar baje subah plastic kursi pe, jahan koi dekh nahin sakta."

He does not answer that. He takes the cold tea and drinks what is left of it.

"Pinky, neeche jao. Tumhein sardi ho jaayegi, aur phir mera dosh hoga."

"Tumhara hi dosh hota hai. Papa ne tay kar liya hai." She gets up, and then leans over and pushes his hair back off his forehead with her thumb, the way she does when he comes in from the airport. "Tum poster jaise lag rahe ho. Ja ke so jao."

Pinky goes down with her slippers in her hand.

Below, at the gate, the guard walks the length of it with his stick and stops where the light falls and looks up. Vihaan lifts a hand. The man lifts his, and goes on to the end of the gate, and turns, and walks back.

The moon goes on over the terrace. The water tank ticks.

Then something moves at the corner of the parapet, and comes over it, and it is thin and it is long, and it crosses the white floor of the terrace without hurrying, and it stops a foot from the leg of his chair.

Vihaan looks down at it. His feet stay where they are, and the chair stays with them.

The snake lifts its head a little and holds it, and the moonlight is on its back, and the little forked thing in the air goes in and out of it, once, twice, and then it puts its head down on the warm floor and stays down.

"Garmi hai." Vihaan says it quietly, to the snake. "Yahan raho."

Vihaan sits down again and opens the laptop, and the light of it comes up on his face, and the snake lies at the foot of the chair the whole night and is gone before the light.

## Morning

Morning in the Arora house. The kitchen is loud with the pressure cooker and the radio, and the sun is on the landing.

The kitchen door stands open with the newspaper folded under the milk bottles on the step, and the bottles have not been taken in yet.

Ananta comes into Purvi's room with her dupatta in her hand.

"Purvi! Oh God, Purvi, tu abhi tak so rahi hai? Pata hai kitna late ho gaya hai?" She sits down on the bed. "Purvi—" She puts the back of her hand on her sister's forehead, and then her palm, and then her wrist. "Tujhe bahut tez bukhaar hai. Tu ek kaam kar, tu rest kar. Theek hai? Aaj tu kahin nahin jaa rahi."

"Nahin, nahin, didi." Purvi pushes the blanket down. "Main jaungi na office."

A glass of water stands on the bedside table with a strip of tablets beside it, and the water has been there since last night.

"Bukhaar hai, Purvi."

"Itni mushkil se job mili hai. Aapko pata hai mujhe kab se job chahiye thi." She sits up, and the room goes still for a moment, and she holds the bedpost until it stops. "Main koi risk nahin le sakti. Aaj nahin."

"Tu khadi bhi nahin ho sakti."

"Main khadi ho sakti hoon." She stands up. "Bas paanch minute."

Ananta looks at her. Then she gets off the bed.

"Paanch minute. Agar us office mein tu gir gayi na, toh main khud tujhe maar doongi."

The door shuts. Purvi stands in the middle of her room and looks at her own arms. She goes to the cupboard and takes down the full-sleeved kurta. She puts it on and buttons it to the wrist and turns the cuffs down. Then she looks at herself in the small mirror over the wash basin, with her hand on the tap.

"Purvi!" Ananta's voice comes up the stairs. "Oh God, you are getting so late!"

## The Record Room

The channel at nine in the morning. The letters on the roof stand unlit over the road and the car park is full. In the lobby the guard writes names in the register with a pen on a string.

"Hello, Ananta. Tum kahan ho yaar?" Ananta is at the rundown board at the end of the newsroom with the phone against her ear and a chalk in her other hand. "Hamari meeting kab ki khatm ho gayi hai. Purvi ko milwana tha main isse. Aur pehle hi din late hai woh."

The newsroom is awake and loud around her. Two women write on the board and rub things out. The ticker runs under the screens with the sound off.

"Main tumhara yahan se hoon." Aryaman's voice comes out of the phone. "Purvi mere saath hi hai. Achha achha, theek hai. Main samajh gaya." A pause, and then, to somebody in the room with him: "Tum Purvi ko B-46 channel file ke saath yahan bhejna."

The chalk stops on the board.

Purvi comes along the corridor with her bag against her chest and her pass in her teeth. She stops at the door of the newsroom and looks at all of it: the board, the screens, the woman with the chalk who is her sister and who does not look round.

Purvi goes on down the stairs, past the canteen where the tea is being made, to the record room. It is a long room with a tube light that flickers. The shelves go from the floor to the ceiling and the boxes of files have the years on their spines in marker.

A fan stands in the corner turning from side to side. At the far end of the room a young man in a blue shirt with the sleeves rolled has three boxes open on the table in front of him. There is a printout beside them.

Purvi stops in the doorway.

"Excuse me. Mujhe B-46 channel file chahiye."

He looks up.

Two women come past the door of the record room with their files, and one of them stops.

"Ye kaun hai?" she says, to the other.

"Woh bhai hai. Aryaman sir ka bhai. Woh jo London mein tha."

"Woh on air nahin hai?"

"Woh kisi cheez pe nahin hai." They go on, and their heels go away down the corridor, and one of them looks back over her shoulder at the door.

Purvi has not moved from the doorway.

"Tum light stand ho," she says.

"Chashma." He puts his pen down. "Chashma tumhare chehre pe nahin hai."

"Banwa rahi hoon. Didi lunch time pe le aayengi." She comes in and puts her bag down on the end of the table. "Purane waale flyover ke neeche road pe hain."

"Toh aaj yeh sab chashme ke bina padhogi."

"Top ki line padh loongi."

"Good."

"Mujhe B-46 channel file chahiye. Aryaman sir ko chahiye."

"Record room hai yeh," he says. "Aur mera nahin. Channel ki files idhar hain — B matlab channels, saath rakhte hain. Aryaman sir ko de do, woh jaante hain."

"Aapko do din mein sab pata chal gaya."

Purvi laughs, and then puts her hand over her mouth, because her head hurts when she laughs.

He looks at her for a moment — the face of somebody who has been told to be somewhere he already is — and goes back to his own printout.

The door of the record room opens and a boy comes in with a voucher in his hand and a pen behind his ear.

"Sahab. Saturday ka caterer ka bill. Parmeet sahab ka office keh raha hai ki aapke paas aata hai."

Vihaan takes the voucher and turns it over. "Chalees crate bottles."

"Chalees, sahab."

"Hamne ikattis liye. Battees ka bill kisi ne kisi ke saath mila ke banaya hai." He puts the voucher down and takes the pen from behind the boy's ear. "Wapas bhej do. Aur kehna mera kehna hai."

The boy goes out with the voucher. Purvi has watched all of it.

"Woh wahan hai." She points at the third shelf from the door. "B matlab channels. Channels saath rakhte hain."

Purvi goes along the shelf with her finger and pulls out a blue box file and puts it on the table and opens it. "B-46. Yeh hai."

"Toh tumhein mil gayi."

"Mil gayi." She starts putting the papers back. Her hands are shaking. One bundle slides out of the file and comes open on the floor, and the pages go across the tiles, and his printout goes out of the middle of them, because the bundles have been stacked on each other all morning. Purvi goes down on one knee and gathers what is hers; he crouches and gets his own sheet from under the shelf and squares it against the floor and puts it back at the top of his stack. Something small and brown comes up out of her page in her own hand.

It is a marigold. Dried, and flat, and the colour of old paper with orange still in it.

"Tumne rakh liya."

"Diary mein gir gaya tha. Us raat se diary mein hai." She holds her hand out. "Do."

"Maine kaha tha doonga."

"Aapne Monday kaha tha."

"Toh late hai." He puts it in her palm and closes her fingers over it with his own, and takes his hand away. "Yeh lo. Ab diya."

She looks down at her fist. Then she puts the marigold into the file between two pages, and shuts the file, and picks it up.

"Vihaan."

Vihaan has turned back to his boxes.

"Ye sab kya hai?" She means the boxes, and the printout, and the figures on it.

"Saal ke ledgers. London ke aur hamare." He puts the lid on one and stacks it. "Delhi waale kisi ne April se balance nahin kiye. London ka hissa ho gaya hai."

"April se?" She looks at the boxes. "Aryaman sir ne mujhe pichhle saal ka monthly ledger balance karne ko kaha hai."

"Toh wohi pata chalega jo mujhe pata chala." He lifts the stack. "Delhi waale sab aakhri box mein hain, aur aakhri box mein ek chuha hai."

"Chuha."

"Chuha maine nahin dekha. Maine dekha hai ki chuha kya kha raha hai." He takes the stack to the door, stops with it there, and puts it down on the shelf at the window, where the fan is blowing straight off it and on to nothing else in the room.

"Garmi hai," Purvi says.

"Bukhaar ko garmi pasand hai." He picks the stack up again. "Ya pasand nahin hai. Main doctor nahin hoon. Main chuhe ka makaan-maalik hoon."

He goes out. Purvi stands in the record room with the blue file against her chest and the marigold inside it. She is smiling. Then the smile goes, and she puts her hand flat on the table and waits for the room to stop moving.

## The B-46 File

The tenth floor of the channel at ten in the morning, and the rundown board on the wall under its own strip of light, and the newsroom's noise coming round the corner of the passage.

Upstairs, Ananta stands at the rundown board with the chalk in her hand and the phone against her ear.

"Hello. Tum kahan ho? Hamari meeting nau baje khatm ho gayi, ek ghanta ho gaya." She rubs out a slot and writes another. "Mujhe Purvi ko tumse milwana tha. Tumne kaha tha dus minute doge."

In the cabin at the end of the floor Aryaman sits behind the desk with his jacket on the back of the chair. The blinds are half down and the morning comes through them in stripes. Arjun is on the sofa with his ankles crossed.

"Woh yahan hai." Aryaman opens the file in front of him. "Ek ghante se yahan hai."

"Toh dus minute aur wait kar legi." Ananta's voice comes out of the phone on the desk. "Woh tumhari assistant hai, Arya. Parcel nahin hai."

"Usse upar bhej do." He picks his pen up. "B-46 channel file ke saath upar bhej do."

The phone goes down. Arjun looks up from the sofa.

"Bhai. Sun." Arjun sits forward. "Aapki personal assistant. Come on bhai. Woh aapke P.A. ki post ke liye aayi hai. File pakadne ke liye nahin."

"Woh file pakad sakti hai."

"Woh file pakad sakti hai." Arjun puts his head back. "Hey Bhagwan."

Purvi comes up the stairs with the blue file against her chest and the pass swinging from her neck. At the door of the newsroom she stops, and takes hold of the frame, and says it to nobody.

"Hey Bhagwan, mujhe job dene ke kehne par mil toh gayi hai. Par bas Aryaman sir ko mera kaam pasand aa jaye."

Purvi goes in.

## The Training of a Dear Heart

The cabin at eleven in the morning with the blinds half down and the morning coming through them in stripes across the desk and the floor.

In the cabin Aryaman has the file open on the desk in front of him and a red pen in his hand. Purvi stands on the other side of the desk.

"Woh aane se pehle hi aapne usko dara diya." Arjun says it from the sofa, and he is enjoying himself.

"Shuru se dara dena zaroori hai." Aryaman does not look up from the page. "Taaki aage kuch bhi ho jaye, dar na lage. Yahi iski training ki shuruat hai."

"Ye kaisi training hai bhai?"

"Pyaare mann ki training hai."

He turns a page, and marks it, and turns it back.

"Sir, kal raat se main yahin par hoon." He puts the pen down. "Mere liye change mangwao."

Purvi stands over him. He looks up at her for the first time.

"Tum. Baitho. Pen lo."

Purvi sits. She takes a pen out of the stand, and her hand is not steady, and she puts it down on the paper and holds it with both hands.

"Uske baad office aake mere nine o'clock show ka rundown ready karo." He turns his hand over. "Uske baad gyarah baje meri dinner pe meeting hai. Uske liye reservation. Hamare last fiscal ke har month ka ledger balance karo. Pichhle paanch saal ki TRP study karo aur mere liye report ready karo. Hamare employees ke payment schedule ko study karo, aur how can you make more efficient. Ye sab karke do."

Purvi writes. The pen goes down the page.

"Ye sab aaj shaam tak," Aryaman says.

"Sir—"

"Aaj shaam tak." He looks at her. "Yeh ek hafte ka kaam hai, ek din ka."

Purvi looks at the page. Then she looks up.

"Sir. Ek baat."

"Poochho."

"Paanch saal ki TRP mujhe kahan milegi?"

"Tumhein mil jaayegi." He picks the pen up again, and then, without looking at her: "Aur haan. One single more thing."

Purvi's pen is ready. "One more thing, sir?"

He writes something at the top of the file and does not look up and does not say what the thing is.

Purvi waits. The pen stays where it is on the page, and the last line on it is blank, and she goes on waiting.

Then he turns the file round and pushes it at her. "Bas. That is all."

Purvi puts her pen down, and under her breath, to the paper, she says: "Main insaan hoon ya naagin?"

Arjun laughs from the sofa, once, and looks out of the window.

## Rajat Is Coming

The elder's room is at the end of the top corridor at noon, and it has a window on two sides with the blinds up, and the sun lies in two long panels of light across the carpet. On the wall there is a photograph of the building at its opening with the ribbon across the door.

Aryaman's sister comes in without knocking, with her phone in her hand and her bag still on her shoulder.

"Tauji. Tauji, suniye."

Parmeet is at the window with his hands behind his back. "Meeting mein hum aise andar nahin aate."

"Naagin ki baat hai."

He turns round.

"Roma ne meri baat Rajat se karwa di hai." She holds the phone up. "Tauji, Rajat keh rahe hain ki he is damn sure, usne naagin ko hi dekha hai. Woh aapse milne ke liye office aa raha hai. Aap ek baar usse mil lijiye. Bas ek baar."

Two men stand by the door. One of them laughs, and it does not sound well in that room.

"Good. Matlab ki naagin hai." Parmeet puts his hand on the back of a chair. "Mere bete pe bharosa mat karo. Woh kuch bhi ancient bolta rehta hai. Dekho, koi naagin nahin hai, bhai sahab. Koi goonda tha, kuch ladkon ne usey peeta, aur ab woh naagin hai."

Nobody answers him.

"Usse aane do," Parmeet says. "Main miloonga."

In the corridor below, the morning's work goes past: a boy with two cups of tea, a woman with a folder against her chest, the lift opening and shutting.

Vihaan comes along the corridor with nothing in his hands; his jacket is over the back of a chair by the lift, where it has been since he came up, and London's day is over, so he is going down. He has been on the phone since seven and these are the last ten steps of it.

The lift opens again. Two men come out with a third man between them. His shirt is not ironed and his hair is flat. He has not slept. He looks at the corridor, and at the doors of it, and at the ends of it.

They take him to the elder's room. The door opens and shuts behind the three of them, and the latch goes over, and it is a good door. Nothing comes through it.

Vihaan stands at the lift with his hands in his pockets.

## The Land of Vishnu and Shiva

The room at the top of the house with the blinds down and one lamp on the desk and the rest of it in shadow.

In the room, Parmeet sits down behind the desk, and the man called Rajat sits on the edge of the chair in front of it with his hands between his knees.

"Maine usey dekha." His voice is not steady. "Bhai sahab, maine usey dekha. Woh naagin thi. Woh ladki thi, aur phir woh naagin thi."

"Tumne andhere mein ek ladki dekhi."

"Maine usey dekha." He leans forward. "Aapko mujh par bharosa karna hoga. Woh wapas aayegi. Usne kaha—" He stops and puts his hand over his mouth.

A jug of water and a glass stand on the tray at the corner of the desk, and nobody has poured from the jug.

Parmeet looks at him for a while. Then he lifts one finger, and the two men take Rajat by the arms and stand him up and take him out, and the door shuts.

Two of the men look at each other. Baljeet stands by the window with his arms folded and says nothing at all.

"Sab chup ho." Parmeet looks round them.

"Achha. Matlab ki naagin hai." The man by the chair says it to the room, and then he turns his head. "Aur us aadmi pe bharosa mat karo jo kuch bhi ancient bolta rehta hai. Dekho, koi naagin nahin hai, bhai sahab."

Parmeet gets up and goes to the window and stands with his back to the room.

"Ye purana zamana hai." His voice is flat and it carries. "Ye desh itna purana hai ki hum soch bhi nahin sakte. Hum dushman desh kabhi is desh ka chhota sa hissa hua karte the. Hum is desh ke saamne khade bhi nahin ho sakte. Ye desh Vishnu aur Shiv ka desh hai, jiski raksha khud Sheshnaag aur Naagraj Vasuki karte hain. Aage bhi is desh par kabhi sankat aayega, toh Naaglok ke in naagon mein itni shakti hai ki ye dushman par bhayanak waar karenge."

Nobody moves in the room.

"Main chance nahin le sakta." He turns round. "Agar ye cheez na giri, aur humein nuksan pahuncha sakti hai—" He puts one hand flat on the desk. "Humein apni aankhein khuli rakhni hongi. Us par nazar rakhni hogi ki kaun hai woh naagin."

## Dragon

The room with the blinds down and the one lamp on the desk, and the light of it on the chair and not on the window.

"Baljeet." Parmeet says the name without turning. "Darwaza band karo."

Baljeet shuts it.

"Mujhe bahut saalon se pata tha." Parmeet comes away from the window and stands behind his chair with both hands on the back of it. "Ki ek na ek din, is desh ke liye, woh ichchhadhari naagin aayegi. Zaroor aayegi. Isiliye maine usey yahan bulaya."

The man by the chair looks up. "Kisko?"

"Kuch logon ka manna hai ki woh insaan the." Parmeet's hands do not move on the chair. "Lekin kuch logon ka manna hai ki woh is dharti par kabhi the hi nahin."

The lamp on the desk hums, and the hum gets into the room and stays in it.

"Kaun?"

Parmeet sits down.

"Angrezi shabd hota hai uska." He says it slowly. "Dragon. Hindustani zaban mein usey ajgar kehte hain, aur Pakistan mein kehte hain ajda. Sadiyon pehle, Akbar ke darbar mein jis dant-katha ke charchay the, woh ye dragon tha. Kehte the ye dragon bhi ek tarah ka naag hi hota hai. Magar iski furti aur shakti naagon se hai kai lakh guna zyada. Naag ko devtaon ka vardaan mila tha. Magar dragons ko kabhi nahin. Padosi desh ki dharti par paida huye the. Ye wahin pale, aur nafrat ki aag mein jalte rahe. Ki aakhir bhagwan ne vardaan naagon ko diya, toh inhein kyun nahin diya."

"Woh ek kahani hai, bhai sahab."

"Woh ek kahani hai." Parmeet nods. "Muh se aag ugalte ye dragon poori dharti pe raaj karna chahte the. Lekin dheere-dheere inka ant ho gaya." He puts one hand flat on the desk. "Lekin hamare mission ke liye humein dragon chahiye thi. Iski vinashakari shaktiyan chahiye thi. Isliye humne iske genes mein ferbadal karke ise phir se paida kiya."

The man by the chair has stopped moving. Baljeet stands at the window with his arms folded and looks at the floor.

"Genes." The man says the word slowly, in pieces. "Bhai sahab—"

"Toh humein chahiye tha." Parmeet turns his hand over on the desk. "Aur humne bana liya."

The room is very quiet. Somewhere below in the building a phone rings and stops.

"Ye dragon, hamare Azha, jo ab poore Bharat desh mein kehar banke tootega," Parmeet says.

## This Is My Weapon

The room at the back of the top floor under its one bulb, and a door in its wall with no handle on this side, and the paint round the door newer than the paint on the wall.

He gets up and goes to the back of the room. A door stands in the wall there with no handle on this side of it. He takes a key out of his pocket and opens it.

Beyond it there is a passage, and the passage goes down, and there is light at the bottom of it.

"Aao," he says. "Tum sab."

They go down. The passage turns twice and goes on down, and the wall of it is warm on one side.

One of the men stops at the last turn and does not go round it. Nobody says anything to him and he does not come on.

At the bottom there is a room with a shutter across it, and the shutter is iron. The iron is discoloured in the middle in a long shape, and the shape is the height of a man and a half. Two men stand at the shutter with their backs to it. They do not turn round. They have been standing there a long time.

Parmeet puts his hand on the shutter.

"Ye kya hai?" one of the men says.

"Ye hai mera hathiyaar." Parmeet says it quietly, into the room, with his hand on the iron. "Ye hathiyaar itna khatarnak hai ki agar maine ise khula chhod diya na, toh poore desh mein kehar macha dega."

Behind the shutter something moves. It is a sound of weight going from one side to the other, and then a scrape, and then nothing.

The iron hums under his hand, and the floor under their feet is warm, and it stays warm.

Parmeet takes his hand off the shutter.

"Yahan koi neeche nahin aata," he says. "Is building mein koi is darwaze ko nahin jaanta."

Upstairs the service lift opens at the top corridor and Parmeet and Baljeet come out of it, and the two men behind them go back to the room.

Vihaan is standing at the lift with his hands in his pockets, waiting for it to come back up.

"Vihaan." Parmeet stops. "Tum yahan kya kar rahe ho?"

"Lift, Papa." He says it without turning round. "Woh aapke saath neeche gayi. Main uska intezaar kar raha hoon."

"Toh intezaar karo."

They go down the corridor. The doors of the elder's room open for them and shut.

The doors open. Vihaan steps in, and the floor of it is warm under his shoes, and he rides it down.
