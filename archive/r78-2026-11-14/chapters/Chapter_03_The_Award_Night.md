## The Newsroom

Nine o'clock at night. The channel's building stands at the edge of the city with the towers still lit behind it, and the logo on its roof is lit too, and the letters stand out over the road.

Inside, the newsroom is all screens and noise. The ticker runs along under the ceiling. A woman at the makeup mirror clips an earring on and reads her script at the same time. Somebody's phone goes off and nobody answers it.

At the back of the room, by the tall screen, a young man sits at a desk that is not his with a laptop open on it and one earbud in his ear. He is not in a suit. His sleeves are rolled and his hair is pushed back off his forehead and coming down again, and he wears a good watch that is loose on his wrist, and he is at work at nine at night in a room where everybody else is talking about a party.

"Verma, sun." He turns the screen a little away from the light. "Jo teen invoices kal ja rahi hain, unme se do par mera signature nahin hoga. Unme se ek par likha hai ki payment ninety days mein hoga. Hum ninety din kisi ko nahin dete. Unhe kaho ki forty-five pe sign karein, warna next quarter se rate badal jaayega."

Verma listens, and turns a page with one finger.

"Aur haan. Jo Kumbh ka file hai—" He stops, and looks up, because the whole floor is looking up.

A woman comes past with a clipboard. "Vihaan, sir, party ka bill—"

"London se."

"Sir, yeh India ka kharacha hai."

"Toh Kumbh ke kharche mein likhwa do." He does not take his eyes off the screens. "Mere paas aaj raat hisaab karne ka waqt nahin hai."

"Of course." She goes, and says something to the woman beside her, and the woman beside her laughs, and neither of them looks back at him.

On the screens, the anchor is talking.

"Poora Bharat desh ki nazar aaj hamare channel par hai. Jald hum announce karenge ki 2025 ka Journalist of the Year Award ka winner kaun hai. Lagataar teesri baar—" The screen fills with a face. The newsroom stops to watch it. "—is Aryaman Suri."

The room claps. Somebody whistles. At the desk at the back, the young man lifts his head and looks at the screen, and the light of it comes off his face, and his earbud is still in.

In the cabin behind the glass, Aryaman Suri sits with his feet on the desk.

Aryaman is in his shirt with the tie pulled loose, a glass at his elbow, and the award already on the shelf behind him where everybody can see it, because he put it there himself.

Aryaman is twenty-nine and looks older in a room and younger in a photograph, which is the whole of his luck. He has a heavy face and a heavy jaw and no softness in it, and he carries no face of that family at all, which nobody in the house has ever said out loud. His hair is cut close at the sides and stands up at the front where he has run his hand through it, and there is grey coming at his temples that he does not cover. He is broad where his brother is tall. He wears no rings and one watch and the shirt is the best shirt in the building. When he drinks, his eyes stay exactly as they are, and it is the eyes that make people careful with him. The newsroom is being decorated on the other side of the glass by his sisters, and he is watching that, and not the screens.

His phone is on the desk, face up, and it keeps lighting.

"Sir." Manoj leans round the cabin door with a tablet held to his chest, and Manoj is thirty-one and has a wife and a middle and no illusions about either. "Home Minister, Chief Minister, State Secretary ke office se call aaya hai. Aur Head of State ne message bheja hai. Aur do businessmen ne."

"Kya kehte hain?"

"Woh—" The young man looks at the tablet. "Woh sir, kuch important hai toh bataayein."

"Kuch important?" Aryaman takes his feet off the desk. "You know something? Life changing."

"Life changing, sir?" Manoj looks at his tablet.

The woman with the clipboard is at the glass. "Sir, life changing baat meri hai." She says it to the room, pleased and nervous. "Meri shaadi ho rahi hai."

Manoj's face does not know what to do.

"That is horrible. Matlab tum khudkushi kar rahi ho." Aryaman puts the glass down. "That is sad. Very sad."

"Nahin sir, main toh bahut khush hoon." She has her hand on her own chest. "Mera bachpan se ek hi sapna tha ki meri shaadi ho."

"Toh Matlab tum bachpan se hi suicide ho." Aryaman thinks about it. "Look, to each their own. But phir bhi, tumhari aatma ki shanti bhi de."

"Sir?"

"Prarthana karunga."

She goes out, and the clipboard is against her chest, and she is laughing at the door.

The woman with the braid comes past his chair, laughing into her hand, and cannot stop. "Ajib paagal aadmi hai Aryaman Suri. Aise khushi ke mauke pe aise kaun bolta hai?"

"Bina filter ke woh kisi ko kuch bhi bol deta hai." Manoj is beside her, and the tablet is under his arm. "Saara din peeta rehta hai. Phir bhi apne kaam mein koi galti nahin karta. Unbelievable, sir."

The young man is back with the tablet. "Sir, main na aapse yeh keh raha tha ki aaj nau baje wali news mein aapke award ke baare mein bata dein."

"Tumhari wife ne kal raat lehsun ki chatni chatayi thi." Aryaman turns his chair. "Yeh bhi news mein bata dein?"

"Arre sir, yeh toh mamooli si baat hai."

"Award jeetna bhi mamooli hi baat hai." He says it to the glass, to the room, to the screens. "Look, hamara kaam hai important cheezein dikhana. Relevant cheez dikhana. Na ki yeh aam cheez hai, ki kaun sa award, ki just have it."

"Sir main diet pe hoon." The young man pats his own middle, which is not the problem. "Aajkal main patla ho raha hoon na, thoda."

"Main diet pe hoon."

"Sir?"

"I can see that." Aryaman looks at his middle. "Lagta hai tumhari diet tumse zyada struggle kar rahi hai."

The score comes up over the newsroom, brass and dhol, and the screens all change at once and the room turns to the door.

## The Mandap

The newsroom floor at nine at night, and his sisters have made it into a mandap.

Marigold. Ropes of it over the screens and along the beams and down the glass of the cabins. A sheet of it on the floor in a circle. Balloons tied to the anchors' desks. A garland hung over the place where the weather girl stands.

On the anchors' desk the autocue still stands, with the last sheet of the evening's script clipped into it, and the marigold has been laid across the top line of it.

A tray of loose garlands goes round the room on one of the debate chairs, and somebody has tied a card to the back of the chair with his name on it.

The whole floor is full of people and the whole floor is loud.

"Mere achhe khaase newsroom ko poora shaadi ka mandap bana diya inhon ne." Aryaman comes out of his cabin with his arms out.

"Pinky aur Nisha—" A cousin with his arms folded, delighted. "Aur karte bhi kya bhai? Aap kahin bahar toh chalte nahin ho, hamare doston se milne."

"Isliye hamare saare dost aapse milne yahin chale aaye, bhai." The other cousin opens both arms. "Bhaichara! Bhaichara!"

"Congratulations!"

"Tumhari kamyabi dekh ke, I am really feeling good." The cousin takes Aryaman's hand and holds it. "Yeh kya happiness hai bhai?"

The sisters are on Aryaman before he can answer, one on each arm, and their bangles are going and the marigold is coming down off one of the screens.

"Arjun aur Karan." Nisha says it for the room. "Tehra saga bhai, yaani hamara cousin."

"Yeh apne jaisa bilkul bhi nahin hai." Pinky is examining Aryaman's collar. "Kam se kam Arjun hamari sunta toh hai. Aur mere kaafi close bhi hai."

"Aur waise sach kahoon bhai—" Arjun leans in, and the score dips under the joke. "Karan se mujhe waise bhi Duryodhan wali vibe aati hai."

Six people laugh at once. Karan does not.

"Aur aapko yeh khaas pasand bhi nahin karta hai," Arjun adds, helpfully.

"Ab main koi pizza toh nahin hoon jo sabko pasand aa jaaun." Karan spreads his hands.

"Nahin nahin bhai. Karan bhai aapko pasand karte hain." Arjun puts an arm round him. "Bas thoda arrogant samajhte hain."

"Arrogant?" Karan takes the arm off. "Main allergic hoon stupidity se. Stupid logon se. Aur especially stupid logon ki bheed se. Isliye main bheed mein nahin."

"That is so mean, bhai." Pinky's hands are on her hips. "Humne kitne pyaar se aapke liye party arrange ki, aur aap aise behave kar rahe ho. Aur bhai, tumhare arrangement ko shaadi ka decoration bole rahe hain. Aur hamein baaraat hi—"

"O please, shut up, guys." Aryaman has his glass up. "Kitni LED decoration ki hum logon ne."

"O bhai, I know." A third cousin pushes in. "Main aapki cousin sister hoon, isliye aapko meri choices buri lag sakti hain. But bechaari Pinky, yeh toh aapki sagi behen hai na. Toh iski pasand aap kaise na-pasand kar sakte ho?"

"I know. I know."

"Aap yeh bolne waale ho ki aapko ladkiyaan generally pasand nahin aati. But we are the sisters. How can you hate us?" Her voice goes up. "I love you, bol!"

"Main—" Aryaman looks round the room he is being hugged in. "Mujhe dikhawa bilkul pasand nahin hai."

"Par tumhare saare ke saare dost dikhawe ke alawa kuch nahin karte."

The music stops. Half the room turns. Aryaman puts the glass down.

"Par ab mujhe bahut kaam hai. Toh yeh toh party karne ka bekaar kaam hai. Tum sab karo. Main bahar nahin aa raha hoon."

"Sir." The young man with the tablet is at his elbow again, and his voice is careful. "Parmeet sir aur Baljeet sir aa gaye."

Aryaman's jaw moves once.

"Ab toh bahar jaana hi padega."

They come in together, the two of them, in white and cream, and the floor of that room reorganises itself around them without anyone being told to. Parmeet Suri is grey-bearded and heavy now, and he walks through his son's newsroom looking at the screens and not at the people. Baljeet is thinner, and he is watching his brother.

"Mubarak ho."

"Thank you, sir."

The two of them stand in front of Aryaman. Nobody in the room speaks. The marigold moves a little on its ropes.

"Koi mujhse bhi baat karega?" Aryaman says it to the ceiling, cheerfully, and then to the room. "Achha."

Parmeet's eyes go past his shoulder, and Aryaman follows them, and the camera goes with him.

The young man from the desk at the back is coming across the floor with his laptop shut under his arm. He has seen the two of them, and he is not hurrying, and he is already reaching for his jacket on the chair.

"Aur tum." Aryaman raises his glass. "Tumhein invitation chahiye?"

"Main London ki line pe hoon."

The room laughs, once, all together, and it is not unkind.

Parmeet does not laugh. He looks at the tall young man with the laptop under his arm for one second longer than a man looks at a guest, and then he looks back at the son who is holding the glass.

## The Two Brothers

The corridor outside the newsroom smells of paint and cold coffee, and the tube light in it is on and nobody has turned it off. The party is a wall of noise behind a shut door.

Aryaman comes out with his glass and shuts the door behind him and stands against the wall.

"I heard the joke," he says, without turning round. "You are funny, they will tell you. You are not funny."

Vihaan sets the laptop down on the floor against the wall and stands with his back to his brother, because his face is easier to hold that way.

"Sunday ko sab log aate hain." Aryaman looks at him along the corridor. "London chhodke, is newsroom mein, is ghar mein. Kya chahiye tujhe yahan se?"

"Ghar."

"Ghar." Aryaman laughs once. "Is ghar se?"

"Yeh mera ghar hai." Vihaan's voice does not change. "Saara jeevan yahi raha hai. Aur koi jagah nahin hai, sir."

Aryaman looks at him for a while, and the noise of the party goes on behind the door, muffled, a song going round and round under it and a hundred people not listening to it.

"Sunday, na. Papa ke saath." He puts his glass down on the floor by the wall. "Woh phone pe sab kuch karte hain. Woh naye office ka baat karenge, aur woh mujhe—" He stops. "Woh mujhe pasand nahin karte, tujhe pata hai?"

Vihaan looks at the floor between his own feet. There is something he could say here and it has been in his mouth for years, and his brother is drunk and hurting and not in a state to hear it, so he says nothing, and the nothing costs him more than saying it.

"Tujhe pata hai." Aryaman picks his glass up again. "Woh sab ko pasand karte hain aur mujhe nahin. Aur main—" He drinks. "Main wahan hoon jahan main hoon."


Vihaan's jaw has gone tight at the corner and he lets it, and does not answer, and the corridor is cold on the back of his neck.
Far down the corridor, an office door opens and closes.

"Corridor mein kyun aa gaya tu?" A woman's voice, behind them, kind and tired. "Arre, yeh laptop bhi le aaye. Andar chalo, dono. Papa baith gaye hain aur ek photo chahiye."

"He is not in the photo," Aryaman says.

"No?"

"I am in the photo." Aryaman pushes himself off the wall. "He is in the photo also. He is just at the edge of it." He goes back in, and the noise takes him, and the door shuts.

The corridor is quiet. The young man picks up his laptop.

## Father and Son

Parmeet's car stands at the channel's gate with the engine running and the driver's door open. He is standing beside it with his phone in one hand and a cigarette in the other, and the towers are lit behind him and the gate light makes a little day of the pavement.

The glass doors give out a crowd and a stripe of wedding light, and Aryaman comes down the steps through it with the jacket over two fingers at his shoulder and the citation in its folder in his hand.

"Mubarak ho." Parmeet puts the phone away and takes his son's head in one hand and kisses the top of it.

"Thank you, Dad."

One more man comes down the steps after them: Vihaan, laptop shut under one arm and his jacket on him. He stands a little way off from the two of them, where a man who has just come out of his family's party can stand without being in it, and waits for the glass.

Nobody says anything. The gate light hums.

"Koi mujhse bhi baat karega?" he says, into it, and when nobody answers he answers it himself: "Achha."

"Vihaan."

"Papa."

"Das baje." Parmeet says it to the towers. "Desk hai teri, kursi hai. Woh news ka kaam nahin. Woh business ka kaam. London ka office jo tu chalata hai, uska hisaab humein dukaan ke bahar nahin jaanna chahiye."

"Samajh gaya."

"Kuch orders aa gaye hain—" Parmeet takes his phone up. "Nahin, baad mein."

Parmeet walks a few steps away, along the pavement, from the gate light into the dark and back into the light.

"Woh dono bhai aaj apne apne haath aage leke aaye." Parmeet's voice changes. "Aur maine baith ke dekha ki meri kya galti hai."

"Papa, unhein nahin pata."

"Unhein nahin pata." Parmeet turns round. "Tujhe pata hai?"

The young man does not answer at once. He looks at his father, and the gate light is on one side of his face.

"Tumhare paas koi nahin hai, bachche." Parmeet takes a pull on the cigarette. "Is ghar mein lonely hoga. Woh mujhe pata hai."

Parmeet gets into the car. The door shuts. The glass comes down an inch.

"Monday, Vihaan."

The car goes out through the gate. The gate light flickers and steadies. The young man stands in it until the tail-lights have gone, and then he goes up the steps into the noise.

## The Road

An old white car on a road at the edge of the city, at night, and the headlights of it going up the tarmac and the dark standing in the fields on both sides.

Inside it, Ananta is driving with her sunglasses pushed up on her head and her sandals off and one foot up on the seat edge. Purvi is in the other seat with her dupatta over her knees and a small bag in her lap, and the two of them have been arguing for ten kilometres.

"Purvi, I know you. Tu east aur west dono saath mein leke chlegi." Ananta takes one hand off the wheel and throws a paper bag across. "Usme sandal hain. Socks utaar. Aur yeh sundar si jacket aur yeh muffler." She leans across and pulls at it. "Ise bhi utaar."

"Tumhein yeh achha nahin laga?"

"Nahin, didi, thand lagegi."

"Didi, please na." Purvi is pulling the jacket back up. "Tum pehno na."

"Jitna bola utna kar."

"Arre—"

The car coughs.

It coughs again, and the lights go dim, and the whole car slows, and Ananta has both hands on the wheel and her chin down.

"Yeh damn—" The engine dies. The car coasts, and stops, and the darkness comes in on them. "Ise bhi abhi band hona tha."

Ananta gets out and slams the door and stands with her hands on her hips on an empty road.

The road runs away both ways between the fields, and the fields run away into the dark, and the lamps have stopped five kilometres back. A dog barks somewhere behind a wall. The wind comes across the road with dust in it and takes at her sari.

"Yahan par toh door door tak koi bhi nahin dikh raha hai." She turns round and looks up at the sky. "Ab kya karun?"

Purvi gets out of the car on her side and stands with her arms folded and looks up as well.

There is no moon over that road, and the clouds are going across in front of everything, and the score comes up under the two of them, low, strings only, and it does not stop.

"Behen, chod na. Thoda door chalte hain." Ananta picks her sandals up off the road. "Kuch toh milega. Mandir mil jaye toh jaas ke paani—"

She stops.

There is a light.

Between the fields, the road climbs and the lamps do not start again, but a small temple stands behind a low wall with a lamp burning, and the light of it lies across the road in a long gold strip.

Purvi looks at it, and her mouth is open, and she has taken her spectacles off to look better and they are in her hand.

"Ananta."

The wind turns and goes up the hill.

## The Naag Mandir

The road at the edge of the city goes up between the fields and stops at the temple, and there is no light on it except the car's and the one lamp in the temple doorway at the top.

It is small from the outside. A low wall, a bell rope in the doorway, a lamp burning in front of the idol, and a floor of stone that is washed, and the washing still shows in the joints.

Inside, it is not small.

The hall goes back into a dark that has height in it. Pillars on either side, and between the pillars lamps in a line, and above each pillar a serpent carved with its hood up. The one at the far end is a throne, and a cloth is across it.

At the low wall in front of the temple, in the last of the light, an old man sits on his heels with a basket beside him and a been at his mouth.

He plays four notes, and then the same four notes again, slower.

Purvi is on the road below with her spectacles in her hand, the frames warm from carrying them. Her right foot comes up onto the first step of the temple and her left foot comes off the road and up after it, and both of them stop on the same step, and she has not decided anything.

Under the skin at the corner of her jaw something moves once and goes still.

The old man takes the been off his mouth and lays it across his knee. He does not look up at her. He looks at the basket.

*"Aapko pata hai?"* He says it to the basket. *"Been bajti hai to jism sunta hai. Jism se poochha nahi jaata."*

*"Main—"*

*"Main achha bajata hoon, beti."* He puts his hand flat on the cloth over the basket, which has gone still. *"Isi liye aap do kadam aur chali aayin. Ek saanp ko apni marzi yaad nahi rehti jab been bajti hai. Uska jism pehle jawab deta hai, aur uske baad wo samajhta hai ki uska jism kya kar gaya."*

Her sister comes up the road behind her and stops, and says her name, and Purvi keeps her back to the road.

*"Ek baat aur."* The old man looks at the basket a moment longer and then at her, and his eyes are not kind and are not cruel. *"Hum marte hain, to hamari aankh aakhri cheez dekhti hai. Aur jo aakhri cheez dekhti hai, use bhoolti nahi. Jisne maara, uska chehra aankh ke andar reh jaata hai. Isi liye humein maarna aasan nahi hai. Hum sirf marte nahi — hum gawaahi chhod jaate hain."*

Uttara is in the temple doorway with the bell rope in her hand.

*"Andar aaiye,"* she says. *"Woh aapke liye nahin bajata. Woh apne saanp ke liye bajata hai."*

The old man's been goes back up to his mouth, and the four notes come again, and Purvi's hand is on the doorframe of the temple before she has thought about it, and she takes it off the doorframe the way a person takes a hand off something hot.

Purvi comes up the steps with her spectacles in her hand.

"Aao." A woman's voice. "aap aa hi gaye jahan aapko aana tha."

Uttara stands by the lamp with her hands folded. She is old, and her hair is white, and there is a gold border round the shawl on her shoulders, and she is not surprised to see either of them.

"Yeh aap hi ka Naag Mandir hai." She comes forward. "Aur aap hamare kul ki Naagrani."

"Main—" Purvi's hand goes flat against her own chest. "Meri koi—"

"Bharosa kijiye." Uttara takes her hand, and turns her, and walks her down the hall with the lamp held up. "Hum beech mein bhi aapse milne aaye the. Aur aapne hamein dekh liya."

The lamps go with them down the hall.

On the wall at the turn of it there is a face painted, and the plaster round it has cracked and gone in places, and the face has not. Older than the temple, by the look of it. A woman's, with the paint still in the eyes.

"Aaiye, Uttara maa—" She catches herself, and starts again, in a different voice, one that is saying a thing she has said a hundred times. "Hum ichchhadhari naaginon ki duniya mein bahut saari ichchhadhari naaginen hain. Jinhein sau saal ka tap kar ke vardaan mila hai. Lekin woh chhe Mahanaginen, chhe Maha ichchhadhari naaginen kaun hain? Jinki wajah se log abhi bhi hamein sunna chahte hain. Batati hoon. In chhe naaginon ki wajah se duniya hamari kahani jaanti hai. Inhon ne Brahmand aur Bharat ko ek saath bachaya hai."

The temple's dark changes as she speaks. On the walls, in the lamplight, the pictures come and go: a naagin at a great height, a naagin in a forest, a naagin in a city street with her hair blown across her face.

"Aur ab baari hai hamari agli naagin ki." Uttara lifts the lamp. "Pehli baar is Naaglok ke dwaar khule."

The score comes up, sung.

"Woh pehli, jiske liye Naaglok ke dwaar khule."

A door cut into a hillside with light coming out of it, and above the door a hood that nobody in the picture explains.

"Shivangi. Ise maa ki shakti mili. Inhon ne apni maa ke kaatilon ka sarnash kiya tha."

The hall gets warmer, and the sung voice goes up, and the six of them pass over the walls one after another, and there is a couple in it, and a beak, and a plague, and mud, and a lamp.

"Bela. Jo badal kar roop aayi, aur apne kaatilon se badla liya."

"Brinda."

The lamp goes along the wall and touches only what is painted on it: a woman with a lamp of her own, water, and at the edge of the wall a man's hand coming in.

Purvi's spectacles come up to her face. She is not blinking.

"Bani. Naaglok ki Aadi Naagin. Jo apne hi dushman cheel ke saath kar baithi pyaar. Apne dushman cheel ke saath. Jisne likhi ajab prem ki yaadgar kahani."

"Mahamari ban ke aaya asur. Kiya desh pe vaar. Dharti ka seena cheer ke, apni mitti ke liye. Dharti pe aayi naaginon ki naagin. Shesh sarvashreshth naagin. Shesh naagin Pratha."

The lamp in Uttara's hand goes up.

"Aur yeh hai sabse badi naagin. Hamari Maharani. Anant kul ki Shesh naagin, Pragati."

The last picture is of a woman on a ridge with a grey shawl and a hand under her belly, and the wind takes the picture and the picture holds.

"Uske aane ka waqt aa gaya hai." Uttara turns, and the lamp comes down, and she is looking at Purvi. "Anant kul ki Naagraani. Ananta."

The cloth comes off the throne at the end of the hall and it does not fall; it lifts, and the hall's lamps all lean at once and then stand straight up, and the flame in Uttara's hand does not move at all.

"Asliye aaiye." Uttara holds the lamp up so that the light goes all the way down the hall and finds the throne and stays on it. "Aaiye, Maharani. Aaiye, apna singhasan sambhaliye. Woh aapka saalon se intezaar kar raha hai."

Purvi stands at the bottom of the hall with her spectacles in her hand.

Behind her, in the doorway, her sister's voice, small, cross, frightened:

"Purvi. Yeh kya ho raha hai, Purvi?"

The lamps burn straight up.

## The Swap

The Aroras' bedroom at eleven at night, with the light off and the lamp on low, the bed turned down on both sides, the calendar with the temple on it hanging where it has hung since they came to this house. The storm has got into the windows and the curtains are moving without any air in the room.

The woman sits on her side of the bed with her hair down and her nightdress round her knees and her hands loose in her lap. The man sits on his with his glasses off and his hands hanging between his knees, and he is looking at the carpet.

"Sunil ji." Her voice is quiet, and it is steady, and it is a voice that has been kept ready for a long time. "Hum Kumbh jaane ki taiyari kar rahe hain. Aur Purvi ka chaubeesvaan janmadin bhi aane waala hai."

The man keeps looking at the carpet.

"Barson se seene mein ek raaz dafan karke baithi hoon."

Purvi looks at the door. It is shut, and the house beyond it has gone quiet, and down the corridor their two daughters are asleep in a room with a mirror in it.

"Aaj bol doongi." She puts her hand flat on the bed between them. "Hamari beti ko Ananta ka naam diya. Aur Ananta ko hamari beti, Purvi, ka naam."

The man's hands come together in front of him and stay together.

"Yahi hai hamari Naag Raani." Her eyes are shining and her chin is up and her voice does not go anywhere near soft. "Naaglok ki Rani."

She brings her palms together and touches her own forehead with them.

"Hum sabhi inhi ko pranam karte hain."

The lamp on the table gutters once, and stands up straight again, and the room is still.

"Ek baat aur hai." She says it to the lamp now, not to him. "Mahine ki dawa jo gate pe aati hai. Woh khaate se aati hai. Woh humara khaata nahin hai."

The man looks up at that, and she is already shaking her head.

"Poochha tha. Wo ladka kuch nahin jaanta. Aur main us chaar rupaye ke peeche nahin padi hoon." Her hand comes off the bed. "Main bas yeh nahin jaanti ki us ghar mein kaun hai jo humein jaanta hai aur humein batata nahin."

He puts his glasses on. It is the only thing he does.

Beyond the window the storm leans on the house and goes on over the roofs, and somewhere past all of it, in a hall with a throne at the end, a girl stands at the bottom of the aisle with her sister's voice behind her and keeps her back to the door.

By eight the storm has gone over and the morning comes in flat and white, and the market is a market again, and the sun is through the tarpaulin, and the lane is loud with buying.

Purvi goes down the lane in her layers — the brown cardigan, the green muffler, the socks, her bag and her list — and the crowd closes round her and closes over her, and she is one small back in a great deal of noise.

A woman stands at the end of the row of stalls with her sari over one shoulder and her hand up, and out of the stones a cobra comes to her palm and puts its hood in it, and the whole lane turns to look at that and forgets the girl entirely.
