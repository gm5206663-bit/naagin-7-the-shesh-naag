The logotype comes up over thundercloud, with something red falling through the frame too slowly to be rain.

A man's voice asks two words in the pitch of a been: *"Kaun hai woh."* A choir of women answers underneath him and leaves the answer unfinished.

The been stops. One low string is struck and left hanging over the black.

A drum, once. Then the story, and no music under it until the story asks.


## Single Malt

The cabin at eight in the morning, with the blinds up and the desk cleared except for one file, and the whole city behind the glass going white at the edges.

The file lies open at the page he stopped on, and a pen lies across it with its cap off. The window behind the desk is shut, and the room is warm, and the coffee on the tray has stopped steaming.

The lift doors open somewhere down the corridor and close again, and nobody comes.

Purvi comes in with the pass round her neck and the bag hanging off one shoulder and a folder held against her chest with both hands.

"Sir. Main aa gayi. Good morning."

"Good morning." Aryaman does not look up from the papers. "Meri reports?"

"Reports? Sir, reports toh abhi start karni hain."

"Toh start karo." He looks up. "Reports start karo. Jaldi. Go. Fast."

She turns to go, and stops, because the desk has three things on it that have nothing to do with the news. A glass with something amber in it. A bottle with no label left on the front. And the day's rundown, printed, with a red line through the middle of it.

"Sir, woh rundown—"

"Reports." He turns a page. "Tumne suna nahin?"

"Suna, sir." She comes back to the desk. "Sir, reports kis type ki? Ratings, ya content, ya—"

"Jo maine kaha." The page turns back. "Reports. Start karo. Jaldi. Go. Fast."

She writes it down and stands there, because there is nowhere yet to sit in that cabin, and he does not tell her where to sit either.

"Sir, woh—" She points at the glass. "Subah ke nau baje?"

"Wait." He holds a finger up. "Haan. Mere liye banao."

"Sir, ye— mujhe nahin aata banana."

"Toh seekh lo."

"Theek hai, sir. Net pe dekh ke banati hoon."

"Stop." He picks the glass up. "Ye single malt hai. Ise neat peete hain. Ismein paani nahin milate."

"Sorry, sir."

He puts the glass down and looks at her over the top of it.

"Now go." He turns a page, and does not look up again. "Aur darwaza band karke jao."

She goes out and pulls the door to behind her, and on the floor outside she lifts her chin at the desks, and her voice comes through the glass once, flat and loud the way a first morning makes it:
"Okay, everyone. Get started."

She goes out and shuts it with both hands.

In the passage outside, on the shelf where the floor keeps its gods, there is a small brass Shiva with a flower on it gone brown at the edges, and the lift's light on his head.

"Shiv ji." Purvi puts her folder down and folds her hands. "Pehla din hai mera aaj. Please, aap sab sambhalo." She picks the folder up. "Self." She says it to the corridor. "Aur bhi bahut kaam karna hai."

## The Prime-Time Face

The make-up room at a quarter to nine, and the light of the bulbs round the mirror on the counter and the wall and the empty chair. A woman in a robe sits sideways in it with her phone at her ear and a tissue in her other hand, taking the red off her mouth.

"Now go." She says it into the phone, and looks at herself in the mirror, and what looks back does not please her. "Tumhari Karan se baat ho gayi hai na? Toh daro mat. Tum jaake Parmeet uncle se milo, aur tumhare saath jo kuch bhi hua hai na, unhein bata do."

A tin of powder stands open on the counter with the puff laid over the top of it.

A glass of water stands beside it with a straw standing in the glass.

She listens. The tissue goes round and round in her fingers.

"I wish." She sits back. "I wish main bhi tumhare saath chal sakti." The phone is against her ear and her eyes are shut. "But mujhe nine pm news ke liye taiyaar hona hai. Lekin tum jao, aur wahan jaake mujhe phone karna. Okay?"

The tissue goes into the bin. The bulbs hum.

"Okay. Bye." She takes the phone away from her ear and looks at it, and then at the door, and then she sits straight and pulls the robe off her shoulders and the day begins.

## Aren't You Coming Home

Outside the channel at half past six in the evening, and the lights of the letters coming on one row at a time. Ananta stands at the gate with her bag on her shoulder and her scooter waiting.

The gate's boom stands up and does not come down, and a car waits under it with its indicator going.

"Aur ghar nahin chalna kya?" She says it over the traffic.

Her scooter stands at the gate with the key in it and a helmet hanging off the mirror.

A sheet of yesterday's paper lies on the steps with a brick on one corner of it to hold it down.

"Didi, bahut kaam hai." Purvi has a stack of files against her chest and her pass in her teeth and she takes it out to speak. "Aap jao. Main bus pakad ke aa jaungi."

"Bus pakad ke." Ananta looks at her. "Tu khadi hai, na? Theek se?"

"Main theek hoon."

"Toh main ja rahi hoon." She puts her helmet on. "Aur phone rakhti hoon."

The scooter goes out into the road and away.

"Mandir ke andar pair rakhne se unke andar tabdiliyan shuru ho gayi hain." The voice comes from the road and the lights and the traffic, and none of it has a mouth. "Shareer ka thakna. Garmi. Aur kechuli ki tarah unki tvacha ka nikalna. Ye pehli nishani hai naagin banne ki."

Purvi stands under the lit letters with the files against her chest and looks up at them.

## The Call From the Newsroom

The newsroom at eleven, and the floor is at its loudest in the glare of the screen wall.

A camera stands by the passage on its tripod with its lens cap off and its tally light on, pointed at the ceiling.

Two men argue over a graphic on one screen. A girl runs a reel backwards and forwards on another. The ticker goes under all of it with the sound off.

Aryaman comes out of the lift with a shirt over his arm and his sleeves rolled, and the whole floor finds something to look at.

"Hi." Ananta is at the board with the chalk in her hand and her phone between her ear and her shoulder. "Good morning. Saara kaam ho gaya hai, ya sapnon mein ho raha hai?"

"No, no, sir." Purvi is up out of her chair with her notepad against her chest. "Sir, matlab haan sir."

"Beta." Aryaman stops in the middle of the floor. "Aaj toh tu kaam ke pehle din hi so gayi."

"Sorry, sir."

"Follow me." He goes on towards his cabin. "Aur haan. Mere liye coffee leke aao. Without sugar. Meetha kuch bhi pasand nahin hai mujhe."

"Okay, sir."

She goes. She comes back with the cup on a saucer with the spoon straight and the steam coming off it, and puts it down on the desk.

"Sir, garam hai."

"Yes, sir." He picks it up without looking at her, and drinks, and then holds it away from himself and looks at it. "Without sugar?"

"Without sugar, sir."

"Hmm." He drinks again. Then he puts it down hard enough that the saucer rings, and the cup goes over on its side and the coffee goes across the papers and off the edge of the desk onto the carpet.

The floor sees it. The whole floor sees it. Two of them stop typing.

"Please—" Purvi is on the floor with her dupatta in her hands. "Sorry, sir. Sorry."

He is already standing. "Okay. Everyone." He comes out of the cabin with his hands up. "Get started."

"Yahan toh saans lene ki bhi jagah nahin hai." Purvi says it to her screen. The floor is loud and the air is not moving. "Us pe jaake taazi hawa khaaoon toh shayad—" She gets up and the bag comes up after her on one strap. "Sir, main ek minute ke liye—"

Nobody is listening to her. She goes.

## Good Morning, Sir

The next morning, and the floor of the newsroom is empty under half its lights.

Chairs pushed back. Screens grey. A keyboard on the floor where somebody has left it. The tube lights over the far half of the newsroom are off, and the ticker runs on the big screen with no sound, and the numbers go round and round.

A cup of tea stands on the nearest desk with the spoon still in it and the surface of it gone still.

The overnight log lies open at the duty desk with its last entry timed at four in the morning, and the entry is in Vihaan's hand, and Vihaan is not in the building.

"Ye kya hua?" Purvi comes in with her bag and stops. "Saare aish-do chale gaye hain?"

"Oh, hello." A woman goes past her with a file. "Sapne dekhne ka time nahin hai. Boss tumhein dhoondh rahe hain. Jaldi se apna assignment complete karo."

"Wahan dekho." The woman points with her chin.

On the big screen a reel is running with the sound off: a woman in a red saree in a doorway, and the camera coming in on her hands.

Purvi looks at it, and then at her own hands, and then at the screen again.

"Ye kya?" She takes her spectacles out of her bag and holds them up, and then puts them on, and then takes them off. "Mujhe bina chashme ke sab kuch saaf dikh raha hai."

"Kya gadbad kar rahe ho?" The woman is at the door. "Jao. Kaam karo."

## The Folder at the Door

The floor outside Parmeet sahab's cabin, ten minutes later, under the corridor's tube light that hums when the lift comes. Two men go past with a trolley of cables. A woman at the far desk is on the phone in a low voice with her hand round the mouthpiece.

Vihaan stands at the shut door. The folder is on the floor against it, squared to the threshold, with a red tag on it and a strip of paper down the side with numbers on it in a small hand. He is in his shirtsleeves in a corridor that is too cold for it, and his weight is on one leg, and it has been on that leg for a while now.

A courier's envelope lies on the floor against the wall where it has been slid under and pushed back out again, and nobody has picked it up.

He has been standing there a while. He looks at his watch, and then at the corridor, and then at the door again.

This is the fourth time this week. The first three times he has come back another day, and every time he comes back another day his father is a little better pleased with him; he knows this, he has decided not to know it, and he stands here anyway, because the papers need signing before six.

Ananta comes down the corridor with a garment bag over her shoulder in its plastic and a cup in her other hand. She slows when she sees him.

"Woh andar nahin hain."

"Desk pe baithe aadmi ne kaha tha ki woh andar hain."

"Desk pe baithe aadmi ne unnis sau athaannave se aaj tak upar nahin dekha." She shifts the garment bag. "Parmeet sahab ek ghante pehle ghar ja chuke hain. Poora khandaan ghar gaya hai. Ghar mein sagai hai."

"Mujhe pata hai." He does not move from the door. "Isiliye papers aaj sign hone chahiye."

"Toh tumhein ghar jaana padega." Ananta looks at the red tag, and then at his face, and then at his face again. "Tum woh ho jo London se aaya hai."

"Haan."

"Pinky kehti thi ki tumhare kamre ki chhat ab ek problem hai." She says it in the same voice, and there is a curl at the corner of her mouth with the cup still at it. "Main Ananta hoon. Main woh banati hoon jo anchors pehente hain. Chalo, main tumhein lift tak chhod deti hoon, kyunki lift tumhare liye nahin rukegi jab tak tumhein pata na ho kaun si hai."

At the end of the corridor Aryaman comes out of his cabin. His jacket is over his arm and the wet papers are held away from him in the other hand.

"Vihaan."

"Bhai."

"Papa ghar ja chuke hain." Aryaman looks at the folder, and then down the corridor at the newsroom. "Tum uske ghar ja rahe ho, us din jab uske bete ki sagai hai, ek kagaz sign karwane."

"Main uske ghar London ke month-end ke saath ja raha hoon. Aaj due hai."

"Toh jao." Aryaman walks on. Two steps later, without turning: "Aur meri yeh assistant. Nayi waali. Us floor pe usse koi ehsaan mat karna. Woh ehsaan maangne lagegi."

Ananta's cup stops halfway to her mouth.

Vihaan looks at the shut cabin door a moment. Then he goes to the lift and presses the button, and the two of them stand in front of it with the garment bag between them.

"Nayi waali." Ananta says it to the lift doors. "Usne kya kiya?"

"Usne usmein paani daal diya."

## The Terrace

A corridor on the fourth floor at ten past six in the evening, and the last of the day coming in flat through the glass behind him, and a man standing with his back to it and his phone at his ear. His jacket is good and his shoes are new and he is holding the phone with both hands.

"Haan, haan, office pahunch gaya hoon." He is smiling at nothing. "Bas aapke Tau ji se milne ja raha hoon."

A woman goes past him with a file and he turns his shoulder to let her by, and into the phone, quickly, with his hand round it: "Ek second— main phone karta hoon."

He puts the phone in his pocket. He looks down the corridor at the shut door at the end of it, and he takes a breath, and he straightens his jacket, and he goes.

The channel's roof at seven in the evening, and the sky going the colour of a struck match over the city. Water tanks, a dish, a washing line with three shirts on it, and the trap door propped open with a brick.

Purvi comes up through it with her bag on her shoulder and her spectacles in her hand. The staples of the trap door catch her dupatta and she pulls it free. There are three chairs up here and a bucket on its side and a cigarette end on the gravel, and the sky is enormous.

Purvi stops, and puts her face up into the air.

Somebody comes up behind her with a crate and puts it down, and she steps out of the way.

"Sorry."

The roof is empty then. The shirts move on the line. She walks to the parapet with her files held against her chest and looks down at the road.

"Waqt aa gaya hai ek naya itihaas rachne ka." The voice comes up out of the city and the roof and the water tank at once. "Ye woh samay hai jab duniya ka ka har niyam, niyati ka har likha badlega. Chhe sarvashreshth Shesh naaginon ke baad, naaginon ke itihaas mein aaj pehli baar ek janmjaat naagin apne ichchhadhari roop mein aayegi. Aur ye sirf uska janm nahin hoga. Ye pralay aur srijan, dono ka aarambh hoga."

The three shirts stand straight out on the line and stay there.

Somebody is at the trap door. A man's shape against the sky, and then a man, breathing hard, with his hand on the frame and his other hand on the brick so it does not fall.

He does not come up. He stands on the ladder with his head and shoulders out of the roof and looks at her, and his face does something that a face does not do in an office.

"Naagin." His voice comes out of him in pieces. "Ye naagin hai."

Purvi turns round. The shirts come off the line behind her all at once and go over the parapet and down into the street.

"Maine usey dekha." He is talking to himself more than to her. "Chhat pe, akeli, aur—" Then he is gone down the ladder, and the brick falls off the door and the door comes down and bangs.

Purvi stands at the parapet with her files against her chest.

"Mujhe abhi uncle ko batana hoga." He is backing away from her. "Is naagin ki talaash poore desh mein hai. Woh naagin yahin hai. Main— main abhi uncle ko batata hoon."

"Uncle." She says the word to the empty line, and it does not mean anything yet.

Down in the street a taxi pulls away from the kerb with a man in the back of it on his phone.

## There Is No Naagin

The drawing room of the Suri house at half past seven, and the lamps are on and the glass door to the garden stands open. The engagement is tomorrow. There are flower boxes stacked by the wall in their paper, and a woman on her knees with a cloth, going over the marble.

Parmeet stands at the side table with a glass in his hand.

"Ye Rajat kahan reh gaya? Ab tak aaya kyun nahin?"

"Usne nahin aana, bhai sahab." The man by the sofa has his hands in his pockets. "Aap bekaar mein pareshaan ho rahe ho. Koi naagin-waagin nahin hai. Aap fikr na karo."

The front door bangs open.

"Naagin hai!" Rajat comes in with his shirt out and his hair in his eyes. "Naagin hai! Woh bahar naagin hai!"

Two of them get hold of him.

"Bhai sahab, koi naagin nahin hai. Aap chinta kyun kar rahe ho?"

"Main sach bol raha hoon!" He is shouting into the room. "Bahar naagin hai! Woh sabko maar degi!"

The door of the passage opens and a woman comes in with a blue file against her chest and her spectacles in her fingers, and stops, because every face in that room has turned to her.

Rajat turns too. Then his arm comes up and his finger comes out.

"Sir, ye file Aryaman sir ki— woh." His voice drops and it is worse for dropping. "Yahi ladki hai. Yahi ichchhadhari naagin hai. Haan, yahi hai. Maine dekha hai. Isne abhi-abhi apni kechuli nikaali hai, aur phir isne apna roop bhi badla hai. Ye hum sabko maar degi. Please, humein bacha lo."

Nobody moves in the room.

"Sir." Purvi's voice is very quiet. "Sir, mujhe lagta hai kuch confusion hai. Hum toh jeete-jaagte insaan hain. Aapke saamne khade hain." Her hand comes up with the file in it. "Hum naagin kaise ho sakte hain?"

"Tum naagin ho." Rajat is not blinking. "Tum naagin ho."

"Nahin." She takes a step back. "Nahin, nahin."

"Pakdo usey!"

Aryaman laughs. It comes out of the middle of the room and it is the worst sound in the house.

"Oh my God." The man by the sofa has his hands on his knees. "Ye kya, aapne naagin-shagin lagaa ke rakha hai? Main toh sochta tha ki aap ameer log thode se crazy hote ho. Par aaj toh maine dekh hi liya. Sach mein crazy hote hain." He wipes his eye with the back of his hand. "Main toh bas yahan pe thode se paise ke liye aaya tha. Lekin aap dono ko dekh ke meri hansi control hi nahin ho rahi hai."

"Uncle." His companion has his hand out. "Aap toh bura hi maan rahe hain."

"Hans-hans ke kahin tera heart attack na ho jaye."

"Uncle." Vihaan says it from the garden door, and he has not been in the room and he is in it now. "Aapka paisa hisaab kar diya gaya hai. Lena hai, ya dena hai?"

The laughter stops in that room the way a fan stops.

The guest's mouth stays open for a moment. Then his companion has him by the arm and is saying something into his ear.

"Nikal yahan se!" Parmeet's voice cuts the room in half. "Security! Security! Andar aao. Jaldi!"

The men come in through the garden door and take Rajat by both arms and walk him backwards across the marble and out.

The glass in the corner is being swept up. The girl on her knees has not stopped.

"Vihaan." Parmeet says it from the side table with his glass still in his hand. "Teri report?"

The room turns. Purvi's head comes up.

"Koi naagin nahin hai." Vihaan says it from the garden door in a voice with nothing in it. "Do din. Jo mila, woh report mein hai. Report aapke table pe hai. Koi naagin nahin hai."

"Aur yeh?" Parmeet's glass moves an inch towards Rajat, who is being held at the door.

"Yeh aadmi dar gaya hai. Darne wale aadmi ko naagin dikhti hai." He looks at Rajat and not at the girl in the middle of the room. "Main ise doctor ke paas bhejta hoon."

"Toh hum khabar band kar dein?"

"Khabar band karne ki zaroorat nahin." He comes in off the step and puts both hands in his pockets. "Khabar yeh hai ki ek aadmi ne apni aankhon se kuch dekha. Woh khabar hum chalayenge. Naam naagin ka nahin hoga."

Parmeet looks at his son for a long moment over the rim of his glass.

"Achha." He sets the glass down. "Toh kal subah mere paas aana. File ke saath. Aur Vihaan—" He picks his phone up. "Tu ek hafte se is ghar mein sabse zyada bol raha hai. Ab tu is ghar mein sabse zyada sunega."

"Ji."

Purvi stands in the middle of the drawing room with the file against her chest and her mouth open, and nothing comes out of it.

And the word comes up on the screen behind them, once, and the score takes the roof off:

**Naagin.**

## The Porch

Outside, the drive is full of white cars with their noses to the wall in the last light, and a man washing one of them with a bucket, and the gate standing open.

The men have Rajat on the gravel between them, and he is not laughing now.

"Main keh raha hoon—" He twists round. "Woh naagin hai. Kisi se bhi poochh lo. Chhat pe baithe ladke se poochh lo—"

"Ghar jao." One of them pushes him. "Ghar jao aur so jao."

The men come back up the drive. They go past a young man in a dark shirt at the turn, and one of them touches his own ear.

"Chhote sahab."

"Woh kaun tha?"

"Rajat. Woh peeta hai." The man shrugs with one shoulder. "Keh raha hai ghar mein naagin hai."

The other one laughs. They go in.

Vihaan stands on the drive and looks at the gate. Rajat is out on the road with his hands on his knees. Then he is up and walking. His car stands under the neem at the corner with its nose out.

Vihaan goes in through the front door. The girl is on her knees with the cloth, and the glass is being swept up in the corner. There is a woman standing in the middle of the hall with a file in her hands and her spectacles in her fingers.

She looks up. He stops.

"Chashma." Vihaan says it to the spectacles.

"Naye hain." Purvi turns them over. "Didi ne banwa diye. Maine lunch time pe pehne, aur poori duniya usmein bohot zyada awaaz kar rahi thi."

"Tumne utar diye."

"Maine utar diye." She puts them in her bag. "Bahut ajeeb din hai, sir."

Behind her, Parmeet's voice comes out of the drawing room, mild and even: "Vihaan. Andar aao. London ke papers."

Vihaan goes past her. At the door of the drawing room he stops and looks back. She is still standing in the middle of the hall with the bag hanging off her hand at her side. Then the door shuts behind him.

Down at the gate a taxi stands with its engine running, and a man gets into it, and another man gets in after him.

"Excuse me, suniye." The first one leans forward. "Parmeet Suri ka cabin kahan hai?"

"Dilli." The driver pulls out. "Chalo."

"Nahin." The man sits back. "Aap galat samjhe. Woh jo doosra cabin hai. Parmeet uncle ka aur cabin kahan hai?"

"Ji."

"Haan. Thank you." The first one settles back against the seat.

The taxi goes off down the lane, and in the back of it one of them puts his hand on the other's knee.

"Ab yahan se hum woh karenge jo humein karna hai."

## The Dicky

The road out past the flyover, and the fields beginning. A black car stands off the shoulder with its rear lamps on and the engine running, and nobody gets out of it.

The driver's door opens. A man in a shirt the colour of wet sand gets out and goes round to the back and looks up and down the road.

Then he opens the dicky.

The lid comes up on its strut, and the boot light comes on, and inside it, folded into the space with his knees against his chest and his mouth taped and his eyes open and wet, is Rajat.

The man looks at him for a while.

"Ab yahan se kahin door chala ja," he says. "Aur wapas kabhi mat aana. Ja."

He peels the tape off, and Rajat's mouth comes open, and nothing comes out of it but air.

"Chala ja." The man puts a bottle of water in beside him. "Paidal. Sadak pakad. Koi na koi gaadi rok lega." He steps back. "Aur agar tu ek baar bhi wapas aaya, agar tu kisi ko bhi yeh baat boli—"

Rajat is looking at him and not at anything else.

"Samjha?"

The lid comes down on him and the boot light goes off.

In the boot the water rolls from one side to the other.

## Death Killed Him

The same road, an hour later, and the light gone. A lorry stands across both lanes with its bonnet up and two men under it with a torch. A load of pipes has come off the back of it and gone along the road in a scatter, and one of them has made a long grey mark on the tarmac.

Two men stand at the edge of the field with a torch between them and look down into it.

"Ye isi laayak tha." One of them puts the torch beam on the shape in the grass. "Humne ise nahin maara. Toh maut ne ise maar diya."

The other one spits and turns round and walks back to the road.

The men stand beside the car with the torch off now, and the night wind comes across the field and turns the grass over.

"Ab Ananta— matlab hamari Purvi ka kya hoga?"

"Ab doosra kadam." The voice comes from behind the torch beam and it is not one of the men. "Doosra kadam tab aayega jab Mahanaagrani ke shareer ka tap badhne lagega. Is avastha mein unki andar ki shaktiyan poori tarah jaagrit hone lagengi. Mann aur tan, dono se. Unki soch aur samajh itni gehri ho jayegi ki kisi bhi insaan se unki tulna karna asambhav ho jayega."

The torch beam comes up off the field and goes across the road to the bus shelter, where a woman sits alone with a bag on her knees.

"Ye unki naagin banne ki yatra ka doosra padaav hoga. Naagin shaktiyon ka poorna prakat roop."

The two men walk back to the lorry and their voices go under the bonnet and stop.

Further along the field, where the crop stands high enough to hold a shadow, two women are standing with a lamp between them and no car anywhere.

The older one has white hair loose down her back and a lamp in her hand and a shawl that is not for this weather.

The younger one wears a man's shirt the colour of wet sand, and her hair is dragged back wet. She watches the bus shelter with her arms folded and her jaw set. An hour ago she sits in the driver's seat of a car on the shoulder of this road, and she opens the dicky, and her voice is a man's voice, and the man in the boot believes every word of it.

## A Week's Work

The newsroom floor at nine in the morning, and it is full and loud and running.

Somebody has put a plant on her desk. Somebody else has stacked four more files on it, and the tray has a note on top that says *before six*, and two men at the far end of the floor are arguing about a headline.

Purvi comes in with the bag in both hands and stops.

The tray is empty. The four files are signed and back in the rack with the dates on their spines, and the top one has Vihaan's initials on the corner of it in the ink he uses for everything, which is the same ink on everything else in that rack.

Vihaan comes along the far side of the floor, stops at the duty desk to look at the sheet pinned there, and he goes on without saying anything to anybody. The reports are gone. The presentation runs on the big screen with her name in the corner of the first slide and the sound off.

A girl goes past with a coffee and looks at her twice.

"Saara kaam ho gaya, sir."

Aryaman is coming through the floor with the jacket hanging off one hand. He stops.

"Aaj ka kaam ho gaya?"

"Aaj ka nahin, sir." She holds up the folder. "Poore hafte ka ho gaya hai."

Aryaman had told the floor at eleven that morning that she would not last the day, and had said it pleasantly, and the floor had agreed with him.

"Hafte ka kaam ek din mein khatm kar diya." He takes the folder and opens it, and turns one page, and another, and the pages keep going. "Chashme mein dum toh hai."

"Sir, chashma nahin hai." She says it before she can stop herself. "Maine chashma utaar diya hai. Mujhe sab dikh raha hai. Saare reports aur presentations maine aapko mail kar diye. Ab main ja rahi hoon. See you."

"Ruko." He closes the folder. "Paanch saal ki TRP tumne kahan se nikali?"

"Storage room ki teesri shelf se, sir. Un boxes ke andar thi jinhein mouse kha raha tha." She says it plainly. "Aadhe numbers pencil mein likhe the. Maine unhein dobara type kiya."

Aryaman looks at her for a moment longer than the floor has time for.

"Jo aapke pehle din itna late ho gaya—" He tucks the folder under his arm. "Maa toh bahut naraaz ho jaayegi."

She stops. "Sir?"

"Kuch nahin." He walks on. "Kal aana. Dus minute pehle."

The floor goes on around her. She stands by her own desk with her bag still on her shoulder and the plant on it, and the ticker goes round under the screens with the sound off.

## The Last Bus

The bus stop at Connaught Circle at half past nine at night, and the last bus standing at the kerb with its door open and its lights on, and a conductor on the step with his arm up.

Purvi comes round the corner at a run with her bag banging on her hip, and the door shuts as she gets there, and the bus pulls out.

"Are, mere Shiv ji, ye kya kar diya!" She stands in the road with her hand out. "Bus chhut gayi. Ye toh aakhri bus thi."

The bus stops forty metres on. The door opens. The conductor leans out.

"Are, bhaiya. Tum Olympic waali ho kya?"

"Nahin." She gets her foot on the step and holds the rail. "Main office waali hoon. Sorry."

"Thoda bhaag ke aana." He laughs. "Thak gayi?"

"Thodi der ruk jaate." She is not laughing. "Aapko maloom hai na ki Connaught Circle se Purani Dilli jaane ki aakhri bus hai? Chhoot jaati toh?"

"Chalaiye, chalaiye. Late ho gaye already." He bangs the rail. "Jai Hanuman gyaan gun saagar, jai kapees tev pooja—"

The bus pulls away with the conductor singing. Purvi sits down at the window with her bag on her knees and her head against the glass.

"Suno." The conductor comes down the aisle and sits on the arm of the seat in front of her. "Kahan hai tumhara office?"

"Yahin paas mein toh. Circle paas mein."

"Paas mein?" He turns round. "Woh toh yahan se aath kilometre door hai. Aur tum aath minute mein pahunch gayi? Bus se bhi tez bhaag ke bus pakad li." He stares at her. "Hai, sachchi?"

Purvi looks at her own hands.

"Ye sab ho kya raha hai?" She says it to the window and the street going by. "Mujhe bina chashme ke dikhne lag gaya. Mera bukhaar bhi utar gaya. Meri skin bhi theek ho gayi hai." Her hands turn over. "Aur ab main aath kilometre, aath minute mein daud ke aa gayi. Kahin main super woman toh nahin ban gayi hoon?"

The bus goes on through the lit street, and the conductor goes back up the aisle singing, and the last stop is hers.

## The First Kechuli

The Naag Mandir at midnight, and every lamp in it burning straight.

Purvi comes up the steps with her bag on her shoulder and her shoes in her hand, and she is out of breath from the last eight steps and does not know why. The hall is warm. The serpents carved over the pillars have their hoods up. The bell at the door moves on its rope with nobody near it.

Her wrist itches. She scratches it and the itch is somewhere else, one inch up the arm, and she scratches that too.

"Ab waqt aa gaya hai ki aap apna asli dharm jaanengi." The voice comes from the far end of the hall, from behind the lamps. "Naagin dharm."

Purvi turns round once, and there is nobody at the door.

"Ye aapki pehli kechuli hai." Uttara comes out between the lamps with her white hair loose. "Jaanti ho, Bharani?"

"Main—" Purvi's hand goes to her own throat. "Mera naam Purvi hai."

"Bharani." The old woman says it again, and it is not a correction. "Anant kul ka bahut ehsaan hai hum par. Anant kul ki naaginon mein Sheshnaag ka ansh hai. Isiliye hum sab naag-naagin Anant kul ki naaginon ko bahut maante hain."

The lamps lean and stand straight. In the corner where the light does not go, something lets out a hiss — dry, unhurried, close to the floor — and both women look at the corner at once, and the corner has nothing in it but a brass bowl and its shadow.

"Toh hum Naagrani Ananta ko bata kyun nahin deti?" The second voice comes from the dark behind the throne, and it is younger. "Ki woh ichchhadhari naagin hamari Maharani hai?"

"Ye itna aasaan nahin hai, Bharani." Uttara's lamp comes up. "Ananta ne chaubees saal tak ek insaan ki tarah zindagi jee hai. Aur ab usey achanak pata chale ki woh naagin hai, Mahanaagin hai— toh kaise sambhalegi woh apne aap ko?" Her voice goes round the pillars. "Isliye humein unke paas unke saaye ki tarah rehna hoga."

"Kaun ho aap log?" Purvi's voice does not carry far. "Mujhe kya ho raha hai? Mere shareer ko kya ho raha hai?"

"Baitthiye." The old woman puts her lamp down on the step of the throne and comes back with it in her hand. "Aur suniye. Rishi Kashyap ki pehli patni Kadru. Woh hazaar naagon ki maa thi. Unhone Rishi Kashyap ki doosri patni Vinasha ko chhal se paanch sau saal tak apni daasi bana ke rakha tha. Is chhal mein Sheshnaag aur unke kuch bhaiyon ko chhod kar saare saanp ne Kadru ka saath diya."

The hall goes quiet enough to hear the lamps. Purvi sits down on the cool stone with her bag against her knees, and under the lamp the skin at the inside of her wrist has lifted a little, a corner of it, the way a label lifts off a bottle. She looks at it and does not touch it.

"Jab Vinasha ka putra Garud ka janm hua, aur unhein apni maa ke saath kiye hue chhal ka pata chala, toh badla lene ke liye unhone saare saanpon ka sanhaar shuru kar diya. Aur tab Anant kul ke Sheshnaag bhagwan Vishnu ke sharan mein gaye." Uttara lifts her hand, and the shadow of her hand goes up the pillar and over the carved hoods. "Aur saanpon ka sanhaar ruk gaya. Aaj hum naag-naagin zinda hain, us Sheshnaag aur Anant kul ke vanshon ki wajah se. Isliye hum logon par bhagwan ka aashirwaad hai."

"Mujhe ye sab nahin jaanna." Purvi's voice is quiet and it does not shake. "Mujhe jaanna hai ki mera shareer kya kar raha hai. Aaj subah main bina chashme ke dekh rahi thi. Kal raat main aath kilometre daud ke aayi. Meri skin—" She turns her forearm over.

The lamps are behind her forearm now, and the light comes through the edge of the lifted skin, and it is thin and dry and it is not her colour.

"Meri skin badal rahi hai. Aap keh rahi hain ki ye pehli kechuli hai. Matlab?"

Uttara looks at her for a long moment.

"Chaar charan hote hain," she says. "Teen poore ho chuke. Chautha aakhri hai, aur chautha aapke ghante ka hai — aapke janamdin ke aakhri ghante ka. Ye kechuli aapki pehli hai, iska matlab ye hai ki aapka shareer ab apna purana roop chhod raha hai."

"Matlab ye ki aap jo hain, woh aapke shareer se nikal raha hai." She puts her hand out and does not touch her. "Dheere dheere. Dard ke saath. Aur jab poora nikal jaayega, tab aap woh hongi jo aap pehle thi."

"Pehle main kaun thi?"

"Anant kul ki beti." The old woman's eyes are wet and she does not wipe them. "Aapke ghar ne aapko chaubees saal ke liye udhaar diya tha, Maharani. Aaj se woh udhaar wapas le raha hai."

Purvi looks at the throne at the end of the hall, with the cloth over it and the brass bowl of red on its step. She looks at it for long enough that the lamps seem to lean towards it, and for a moment the cloth has a shape under it that the cloth does not have when she blinks.

"Isliye," the old woman says, "humein unke paas unke saaye ki tarah rehna hoga."

The lamps go down the hall in a line, one after another, and the shadows of the pillars go with them. The hall is dark and the moon comes in through the high window and lies on the stone in three long pieces.

Purvi stands up and her knees are not there. The cold comes up her arms from the inside, the way a fever goes out of a body and leaves it empty, and her teeth are going, and she cannot remember the eight steps she came up by.

Behind her, low, near the floor, the hiss again.

The bell at the door moves, and this time it rings.
