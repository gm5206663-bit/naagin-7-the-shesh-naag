# Chapter 7 — The First Sign (Part 1)

*Episode reference: Naagin, season 7, Episode 3 (first part).*

*Draft; not yet accepted.*

### TITLES

*The twelve seconds the season plays on every night after its first: the theme, then straight in (`canon/TITLES_SEQUENCE.md` §I). The full forty seconds are written once, at the head of Chapter 1, and are not repeated below it.*

The logotype comes up over thundercloud, with something red falling through the frame too slowly to be rain.

A man's voice asks two words in the pitch of a been: *"Kaun hai woh."* A choir of women answers underneath him and leaves the answer unfinished.

The been stops. One low string is struck and left hanging over the black.

A drum, once. Mahadev's trishul is not shown, but the thunder is his. Then the story, and no music under it until the story asks.

## The Drink

Aryaman's cabin at six in the evening, and the blinds half up, and the last of the day lying across the desk in bars, and the tube-light over the door already on against it.

She knocks once, and does not wait for an answer, because she has learned on her first day that waiting is worse.

"Sir, main — main aa gayi," she says.

"Good," the score sounding under the allowing (m00:06), he says, without looking up.

The desk stands cleared except for one file and a cut-glass tumbler, and behind them a bottle with no label, amber to the shoulder.

"Meri reports. Reports toh abhi start karni hain, sir," the score sounding under the stammering (m00:20, first of two), she says.

"Reports start karo, jaldi. Go, fast. Wait. Haan, mere liye banao," the score sounding under the ordering (m00:20, second of two), he says.

The tumbler means one thing, and her face says she knows it, and hates knowing it.

"Ye hamein nahin aata banana," she says, with shame.

"Toh seekh lo," he says.

She gets the phone out, and the page loads white over her thumb, and she reads it with her lips moving, her sleeves down to the wrist, because the sleeves stay down whatever she does.

"Net pe dekh ke banati hoon," she says.

She pours, and the spirit runs amber, and her other hand goes out for the water jug — and his hand comes up flat.

"Stop. Ye single malt hai. Ise neat peete hain. Ismein paani nahin milate," he says.

The jug goes back. Past the glass, a line of crows goes home over the dish antennas, black on black-blue.

"Sorry, sir," the score sounding under the apologising (m00:51), she says.

"Now go," he says.

She gathers the reports in both arms, and the file.

The week's work owed in a day sits on top of the pile and looks at her, with dread.

* * *
## The Nine O'Clock News

The make-up room at a quarter past six, and the bulbs burning in a row round the mirror, and their light lying warm on the counter and the wall.

Aryaman's sister sits in the chair with a phone at her ear and a girl at her hair, and she talks fast and warm, the way she reads the news, as if the man on the phone can see her smile.

"Tumhari Karan se baat ho gayi hai na, toh daro mat. Tum jaake Parmeet uncle se milo, aur tumhare saath jo kuch bhi hua hai na, unhein bata do. I wish main bhi tumhare saath chal sakti, but mujhe nine pm news ke liye taiyaar hona hai. Lekin tum jao, aur wahan jaake mujhe phone zaroor karna. Okay?" she says.

A tin of powder stands open with the puff laid over it, and beside it a glass with a straw in it, sweating a ring onto the counter.

The night's rundown waits in the file on the counter, unread.

One bulb in the row gives out with a tick, and nobody looks up.

Her desk on the emptying floor, one lamp on in a row of dead ones, and her bag open, and out of it a small brass Shiva, no taller than her thumb.

"Shiv ji, pehla din hai mera aaj, please aap sab sambhalo. Self — aur bhi bahut kaam karna hai," she says to him.

She puts him back, and bends to the reports.

The floor empties around her lamp, with loneliness.

* * *
## The Bus Home

The channel's gate at half past six, and the day gone blue over the road, and the tube over the gate hissing awake.

Ananta stands with her bag packed and her shoes changed, going home, and Purvi has walked her down, and will walk straight back up.

"Aur ghar nahin chalna kya?" the score sounding under the parting (m01:46), Ananta says.

"Didi, bahut kaam hai. Aap jao, main bus pakad ke aa jaungi," Purvi says.

"Chashma banne diya hai. Kal dopahar tak le aaungi," Ananta says.

A scooter stands at the kerb with the key in it and a helmet on the mirror, and nobody near it.

The guard's register lies open on the sill, and the gate stands wide.

The bus comes sighing, and Ananta is up the step, and gone, knowing nothing.

Under the half-shutter a street dog sleeps with one eye open, and it watches her pass.

The corridor is empty, and her footsteps go ahead of her, and then a voice that is not hers and not anybody's fills it from end to end.

"Mandir ke andar pair rakhne se unke andar tabdeeliyan shuru ho gayi hain. Shareer ka thakna, garmi, aur kechuli ki tarah unki tvacha ka nikalna — ye pehli nishaani hai naagin banne ki," the voice says.

She walks on under the tube-lights, hearing nothing, and climbs to her lamp and her reports, with resolve.

* * *
## Good Morning, Sir

The newsroom floor at nine in the morning, and the screen wall already glaring, and the day's first ticker crawling under it with the sound off.

She sleeps at her desk with her face on the reports and the pen still in her hand, and the ink has kissed her cheek, and a cold ring from last night's cup stands on the desk beside her.

His shadow comes over the desk before he does.

"Hi, good morning. Sara kaam ho gaya hai, ya sapnon mein ho raha hai?" he says.

She comes up with the pen in the air and the reports stuck to her cheek, with embarrassment.

"No, no, sir. Sir — matlab haan, sir," she says.

"Beta, aaj toh tu kaam ke pehle din hi so gayi. Follow me. Aur haan, mere liye coffee leke aao — without sugar. Meetha kuch bhi pasand nahin hai mujhe," the score sounding under the needling (m02:29, first of two), he says.

"Okay, sir," the score sounding under the obeying (m02:29, second of two), she says.

The pantry is steel and steam, and the kettle, and a row of paper cups, and she carries his out with both hands and her sleeves to the wrist.

"Sir — garam hai," she says.

"Yes, sir," he says, flat as a slap.

The cup tips off the saucer's edge and goes over on the carpet, and the brown spreads fast between the fibres.

"Please — sorry, sir," she says.

He sends her out with his chin, and the door shuts behind her, and through the glass, flat and too loud for a first morning, she says it to the whole floor.

"Okay, everyone. Get started," the score sounding under the rallying (m03:19), she says.

The floor looks up, and looks down, and on the sill the pigeons go up all at once.

She sits, and squints at the screen, because the broken pair sits folded in her bag with one lens out, and bends to the week's work.

* * *
## No Room to Breathe

The floor at eleven in the morning, and the screen wall glaring, and the air standing dead under the vents, and every desk loud.

Her skin itches under the sleeves, and she scratches through the cloth, and the heat sits under her skin like a second fever, and the noise comes at her from all sides at once, with distress.

The water cooler stands with its queue and its dripping tap, and the wall-fan above it hangs dead.

The files pile at every elbow, and nobody's hands stop.

"Yahan par toh saans lene ki bhi jagah nahin hai. Upar jaake taaza hawa khaaun toh shayad karun. Sorry," she says to nobody.

She takes the stairs, and the stairwell breathes cold on her face all the way up, cold as a hand, with relief.

Her bag knocks the rail at every step.

Above her the trapdoor stands open on daylight.

* * *
## The Shut Door

The corridor outside Parmeet's cabin at one in the afternoon, and the noon light standing in the glass, and the cabin door shut.

Vihaan stands before it the way he stands before everything that wastes his time: still, and with his hands in his pockets, and nothing in them.

The brass plate on the door says Parmeet Suri, and a courier's envelope lies under the door, and nobody picks it up.

A cold breath comes under the shut door and touches his shoes.

"Parmeet sir ghar pe hain. Kal sagai hai na," Ananta says behind him.

She has a small white case in her hands, and she looks at his face, and then at his face again, with curiosity.

"Tum woh ho jo London se aaya hai," she says.

"Ananta ji," Vihaan says.

"Pinky kehti thi ki tumhare kamre ki chhat ab ek problem hai," she says, like a woman repeating the morning's gossip.

"Report table pe hai," he says.

Aryaman comes down the corridor with the jacket hanging off one hand, and does not stop, and the warning drops as he passes.

"Us floor pe usse koi ehsaan mat karna," he says.

Vihaan holds his brother's eye till his brother walks on, with defiance.

Ananta walks him to the lift, because the lift will not come for him otherwise.

The lift arrives with nobody in it.

She goes back to the floor with the white case, and he goes down to the gate.

* * *
## The Terrace

A corridor at two in the afternoon, and the light lying in slabs through the glass, and a man with a phone at his ear, looking for a door that will open.

"Haan, haan, main office mein aa chuka hoon. Bas aapke Tau ji se milne ja raha hoon," Rajat says.

Ahead of him a trapdoor shuts on daylight, and a full-sleeved arm is gone through it before he can call out.

"Ek second — I — I'll call you back," he says, and goes after her, because she is the only person in the building with time to point him to Tauji.

He puts the phone in his pocket and takes the stairs two at a time.

The stairs, and the trapdoor propped with a brick, and then the terrace under a hard noon sun.

On a rope across the terrace the washed shirts stand straight out in the wind.

She stands at the parapet with the wind in her hair, and does not hear him come up, with calm.

The pigeons sit the parapet in a row, grey as ash.

And over the empty terrace a voice comes, out of nowhere and everywhere, with the score sounding under the foretelling (m05:49).

"Waqt aa gaya hai ek naya itihaas rachne ka. Ye woh samay hai jab duniya ka har niyam, niyati ka har likha badlega. Chhe sarvashreshth Shesh naagins ke baad, naagino ke itihaas mein aaj pehli baar ek janmajaat naagin apne ichchhadhari roop mein aayegi. Aur ye sirf uska janm nahin hoga. Ye pralay aur srijan donon ka aarambh hoga," the voice says.

The wind drops dead. The sun goes white, and the shadows wash off the concrete, and a dry tearing sound crosses the terrace with no mouth for it.

"Naagin. Ye naagin hai," Rajat says.

The score comes up under him like a held breath.

"Mujhe abhi uncle ko batana hoga. Is naagin ki talaash pure desh mein hai. Woh naagin yahin hai. Main — main abhi uncle ko batata hoon. Uncle—," he says.

The pigeons burst off the parapet all at once.

She folds where she stands, and the concrete takes her.

He is already running for the trapdoor, with fear.
