## The House in Mourning

The main hall of the Suri house at six in the morning, with the tube lights on and the garden doors shut against the reporters outside.

The television at the far end carries the news without sound.

A white flower lies on its side in the brass bowl beneath it, and the string of marigold over the doorway has begun to dry at the knots.

A tea tray stands on the low table with cups nobody has touched.

The card beside it says *Shok Sabha* in black ink, and the lower corner has curled from the damp.

The house is full of people who come to mourn a woman they barely know.

They sit in their good clothes, their voices low when a camera turns toward the gate, and their hands busy with the cups.

At the far end of the hall, Ravish bends over Swarna's body.

He holds one of her hands in both of his and does not hear the guests behind him.

"Main tumhe kabhi nahin bhool paunga," the score sounding under the vow (m01:21).

His voice breaks once with grief.

"Kabhi kaise bhool paoge?"

The guest beside him touches the edge of her dupatta.

"Apne rishtedaaron ko bhala koi kaise bhool sakta hai."

Parmeet stands by the closed doors. "Hum sab family jaise rehte hain."

He says it to the room, and the room accepts it.

A reporter at the gate looks through the bars.

"Sham ke telecast ke liye koi bite nahin hai."

"Bas ek final picture mil jaaye na, andar se. Maza aa jaayega."

The guard shakes his head.

"Permission nahin hai."

"Peechhe hato."

At the side table, a guest lifts a cup and finds the tea cold.

"Swarna tumhari saggi nahin thi, par family toh thi na. Thoda afsos toh dikhao."

"Unka fashion sense bahut achha tha," says another, the score sounding under the praise (m00:43).

"Unki skin itni glow karti thi. Daily skin-care routine follow karti thi."

"Roma, tumhe yaad hai inka woh parlour?"

"Parlour wali aunty ab bahut yaad karengi inhein."

A laugh escapes one woman and stops at the sight of the body.

At the window, somebody says the minister loved Swarna, and the love killed her.

The sentence passes from one person to the next without finding a place to sit.

A news voice comes through the television.

"Minister Sameer ne apni patni ka qatl kar diya."

Parmeet looks once at the doors.

"Hum sab dost ek family jaise hain."

The reporter outside raises his microphone, but the gate stays shut.

## Music Therapy

The upstairs sitting room at seven, with morning light caught in the glass and a speaker on the floor beneath the window.

The curtains lift and settle in the slow pull of the fan.

On the centre table, the fruit bowl has been pushed to one side to make room for a phone.

Ahana's phone lies beside the speaker, its screen showing the track's progress.

The tea tray from the hall stands by the door, every cup still full.

Ahana dances alone between the sofa and the open doorway.

Her hands keep time; her feet do not touch the flower petals scattered from the hall below.

Dimple stands in the doorway with Pammi.

"Yeh sab kya chal raha hai?" the score sounding under the question (m03:33).

Ahana stops the turn but not the music.

"Main khush nahin hoon. Main dukhi hoon. Aur isiliye main dance kar rahi hoon."

"Dukh mein aise kaun naachta hai?"

"Amir log."

"Lekin amir toh hum bhi hain," says Dimple.

"Aap toh aise nahin naachte."

"Yeh amiron ke bhi amiron ke bhi amir hain," Pammi says.

"Shayad in logon mein dukh ko aise hi handle kiya jaata hai."

"Exactly."

Ahana opens both hands.

"Dil khol ke dance kijiye — aap bhi yeh therapy lijiye. Isse saara dukh, saara dard dheere-dheere khatam ho jaata hai. Pain, stress relieve karne ke liye — main music therapy le rahi hoon."

Pammi looks at Dimple.

"Agar dance nahin kiya toh inhe bura lag jaayega," the score sounding under the warning (m02:31).

"Ahana sad hai, toh woh music therapy le rahi hai," Dimple says, turning toward the stair.

"Aur chahti hai ki hum bhi apna gham door karne ke liye music pe dance karein. Aap hi ne kaha tha na, iski har baat maan-ni padegi."

Parmeet comes upstairs with Baljeet.

"Haan. Agar Ahana bitiya keh rahi hai toh humein bhi therapy try karni chahiye."

"Bilkul," says Baljeet.

Ahana turns the music up, the score sounding under the call (m03:01).

"Kis baat ki deri hai, uncle? Chaliye. Saara dukh, dard, peeda gayab ho jaayega."

A chair shifts under Dimple as she lifts both hands, first awkwardly and then with the beat; Pammi follows, and Parmeet steps into the space between them while Baljeet begins to laugh before making a full turn.

At the bottom of the stairs, the hall keeps its white flowers, its card and its cold tea.

The sound climbs down the stairwell and gets there before anyone does.

## The Feed Sheet

The fourth-floor room at three in the morning, with the desk lamp on and the rest of the house dark.

The open window admits the last traffic sounds from the lane below.

A grey envelope lies on the desk beside the account book.

The courier waits by the door while Vihaan opens it.

His eyes lift to Vihaan's face, then fall to the envelope.

Inside is the office copy of the Kumbh feed sheet, the same form that once hung from a clip on an OB van door.

Vihaan's signature sits at the foot of it.

The new line beneath it carries no sender's name, only an instruction to keep the account open.

Vihaan places the sheet next to the account book.

He does not call his father.

The phone rings once.

"Verma." Vihaan keeps his eyes on the sheet.

"Sir, woh Kumbh account ka paper aaya hai."

"Kahan se?" he asks without looking up.

"Naam nahin hai."

"Line kis desk par daali?"

"Abhi kahin nahin."

Vihaan turns the sheet around.

The lamp catches his signature and the empty space beside the new instruction.

"London file mein rakho."

"Par naam?"

"Mera."

"Sir, aapka sign pehle se hai."

"Is baar routing ka."

He writes his name in the receiving column, draws a line from the account to the London desk, and dates the entry.

The number in the book does not change, but the route does.

Verma reads it back over the phone.

"Kumbh account. London desk. Vihaan Suri."

"Haan."

"Phir agla paper bhi yahin bhej doon?"

"Bhej do."

The call ends.

Vihaan leaves the copy in the folder with his name on its tab.

The sheet no longer hangs from a van door, and the next instruction no longer has to cross his father's desk to reach the work.

## Pinky's Glass

A glass of water stands at the edge of the rug.

## The Four Rules
