## Tina and Roma

The Suri house has its gates open and its lawn lit, and the marigolds are strung in loops from the gateposts to the veranda, and the men at the gate have been told which cars to let through. The mehendi has been on the aunts' hands since four in the afternoon and is still dark at the edges. Sheshnaag's hood is not shown, but the house feels like a temple that has forgotten its god. There is haldi on one of the doorframes, put there that morning by somebody's mother, and a handprint of it on the wall that nobody has wiped off.

A tray of welcome sherbet stands at the top of the steps with a boy behind it in a white coat, and the glasses on it are filled and none of them taken.

Tina's family comes up the drive at a quarter past eight, and Parmeet is at the bottom of the steps to meet them, and Dimple is behind him with her pallu in her hand.

Tina comes up the steps in front of them, because she has been told to. Twenty-two, and tall for it, in a lehenga the colour of the inside of a rose, with the dupatta pinned so that it will not move whatever the evening does. Her hair is half up and the rest of it down her back, and there is a diamond on a fine chain at her throat. She is holding her skirt off the stone with two fingers the way she has been taught.

Her eyes go along the veranda, over the marigolds, over the waiters, and stop on nothing, and she smiles at the right second.

Roma comes up behind her, in green, with her spectacles on. Roma reports for the channel's evening bulletin and is here as Tina's people, and nobody at the door is looking at her, and she settled what she thinks of that a long time ago.

"Tina and Roma, you all are looking so pretty tonight." Someone at the door puts his hands together. "Bhai sahab, dar gaye the hum log to ki kahin aap logon ko late na ho jaye aane mein." "Arey, idhar aao.", the score sounding under the welcoming (m00:04).

"Mera chhota beta." Parmeet has his hand on the shoulder of a young man and is saying it to Tina's father in the doorway.

The son stands a head above his father and does not stand the way a man stands who knows it.

Twenty-four. Short hair pushed back off his forehead where he has run his hand through it once too often tonight. The eyes are long and quick and black and they take in a stranger in one movement and give nothing back; the jaw is square and set. Nothing about him asks the room for anything, and the room has already given it: the two men at the gate have stopped talking to each other, and Tina's aunt has turned round, and neither of them could tell you why they did it. A white shirt with the collar still stiff, because his mother bought it on Thursday; the sleeves turned back twice; dark trousers; black shoes somebody else has polished. His hands are empty and he does not know what to do with them.

"Vihaan. Aaya hai London se — hai iski kal subah ki flight."

"Achha, achha." Tina's father shakes his hand and looks at him for the time it takes to price a shirt. "London mein rehta hai?"

"Kaam ke liye." Vihaan smiles with his mouth. "Rehna zyada, ghar kam."

Tina's father laughs at that, once, and something in the boy's shoulders comes down half an inch, with relief.

"Andar aao, andar."

Parmeet takes his own hand back.

"Photo ke liye sab log khade ho jao."

The two families arrange themselves on the steps for a photograph, and the photographer moves them a step to the left twice, and Tina stands at the edge of the group with her hands folded in front of her and her eyes going once to the gate.

"Vihaan." Parmeet says it over the heads of the guests, and does not lower his voice. "Tumne study kholi?"

"Khol di."

"Mahine ka hisaab aaj raat hi band karo. Subah ki flight hai." Parmeet turns back to Tina's father before the answer comes. "Ladka hai, par hisaab uske haath ka saaf hai."

"Achha." Tina's father looks at the young man for as long as it takes to price a shirt. "London mein kaam karta hai?"

"Ji. Hamare office ka London side."

"Bahut achha."

The photographer puts him at the end of the back row, and Dimple pulls him forward by the sleeve until he is in it properly, and the bulb goes off twice.

One more for Karan and Tina, with her mother's hand on Tina's shoulder in it. Karan stands beside his fiancée with his arms at his sides and does not touch her, and then the whole party moves inside, and Vihaan goes up the back stairs to the study.

## The Gift Book

The study is on the second floor at the back, over the kitchen, with one desk lamp on and its light lying across the open book, and it smells of the food and of old paper, and there are two things open on the desk: the month's London file and the gift book, with a pen laid across it.

Guests have been in and out all evening.

Vihaan sits down and takes the pen and enters what is there: a watch, two sets of bangles, a cheque from Tina's uncle, a chain, and the envelope Dimple has set on the tray with the others and left shut.

Vihaan opens it. It holds a bank slip, and on the slip the amount is written in figures, and beside the slip, in the hand that keeps this book, the amount is written again as a word so that no one can add a nought to it.

Vihaan looks at the figures for a while.

Then he turns the gift book back four pages and looks at the entry for another wedding, and back four more, and the same hand has written the same kind of slip for the same family.

Vihaan puts the pen down and sits back, with suspicion.

"Kitna?" His father is in the doorway with his glass, and nobody in the study has heard him come up the stairs.

"Bees," he says.

"Likh do."

"Likh diya."

Parmeet looks at the book, then at his son, and takes the slip out of it and puts it in his own pocket and goes back down to the party.

The door swings shut on its hinge and does not latch.

Vihaan sits at the desk for a moment with the pen in his hand. Then he writes the entry out again, the same amount in the same figures, on the one page of the book that has been ruled for a second copy, and closes it, and puts the pen across it exactly where he found it.

## Purvi Looking

The hall at nine in the evening with the chandelier on and the canvas up over the garden doors, and the party going on in three rooms at once.

Purvi moves through the rooms with a tray she is not carrying and a phone in her hand she is not using.

Purvi is twenty-three and looks younger in this house, and that is the layers: a cotton salwar in a green that has gone a little grey with washing, a dupatta pinned at the shoulder, her hair in a plait that she has redone twice already tonight and that is coming loose again at the crown. Her spectacles have a mended hinge and the mending shows. Her ears are pierced twice on the left and once on the right.

She has put on the only good sandals she owns and they are one size too narrow, and she is not complaining about them, and it is the sort of thing that shows on a face before it shows in a walk.

Vihaan comes the other way through the same rooms, a message open on the phone in his hand. He is smiling, small and private, at the screen.

She moves out of his road with the tray she is not carrying. "Sorry," he says to the tray and not to her, and goes on. His jaw is steady, and his hand is steady on the phone.

Purvi looks at the door. She looks at the stairs. She looks at the group by the sideboard, and the group by the sofa, and the men on the veranda with their glasses, and Ravish is not in any of them.

She looks at the bookshelf where he stood, and her hand is steady on her own tray, and she smiles at nothing, small and sad.

"Didi ne kaha tha ki Ravish sir function ke baad apni family se baat karenge.", the score sounding twice over the hoping (m00:38 x2 as one). She says it to Roma, who is standing at her elbow with a plate. "I hope aisa hi ho. Par didi hai kahan?"

Her phone goes in her hand, and she looks at it, and her face changes.

"Hello?"

She turns her shoulder to the room.

"Tum aa nahin rahe kya aaj?"

His voice comes thin off the phone, and Roma hears it over the plate. "No, babes, aaj nahin. Magar jaldi aaunga. Then I am all yours."

Purvi puts the phone down with her ears hot, with embarrassment.

Roma watches her over the plate. "O ho, Vikram ka call. Madam blush kar rahi hai."

"Chup kar."

"Lagta hai Tina ke baad tera hi number lagne waala hai."

"Mera number tere baad hai. Isiliye to keh rahi hoon, der mat kar."

Roma puts the plate down on the arm of a chair. "Roma, tu reporter se kuch zyada hi ban gayi hai."

"Arey bhai ki life mein." Roma picks the plate up again. "Main to fully try mode mein hoon. Lekin tera hi—"

## Two Sisters in the Crowd

The hall at the window end, with the curtains open on the lawn and the light of the drive lying flat across it.

A tray of small brass lamps stands on the sill with every one of them lit.

Vihaan comes along under the curtains with two of the aunts behind him, and one of the aunts is telling him about London in a voice that carries into the passage.

Ananta comes in with her dupatta on her shoulder and her eyes going round the room once, and finds Ravish at the far side by the window with his back to the party, and takes one step towards him.

Ananta is the best-dressed woman in that hall tonight and she has done it on purpose: a dark red sari with a gold border, the blouse cut high at the back, her hair down and heavy, a small diamond at each ear. Her lipstick is the red she wears when she intends to be looked at. She has been crying, with grief, and she has fixed it.

It takes knowing her to see it: the skin under her eyes is a shade darker than the rest of her face, and the fixing has been done quickly in a car.

Purvi's hand closes on her wrist.

"Didi."

She pulls her into the passage between the dining room and the stairs, where the music is quieter. "Di, thank god aap aa gaye. Pata hai kab se intezaar kar rahi thi main aapka. Di, kya hua? Aap—"

Her eyes go past Ananta to the window where he stood, and the question dies.

"Wo bhi yahin tha. Pata nahin kahan gaya. Aapne kaha tha ki Ravish sir aaj sab saaf kar denge. Wo kar denge na?", the score sounding under the hoping (m02:52).

"Purvi." Ananta straightens her dupatta. "Sab kuch ho jayega."

"Baad mein baat karungi, sorry — par baat to aaj Ravish sir karenge."

"Purvi, 100% baat ho jayegi." Ananta takes her hand. "Tu 1% to us pe bharosa rakh."

"Haan."

Ananta looks back into the room, at the cake, at the flowers, at her sister's face.

"Chal, shuruaat ho gayi. Ab aage ka raasta seedha hai. Aur wo seedha kya hai, sirf hum log samajhte hain."

The band goes quiet and comes back with a shehnai, and the room turns toward the middle of it, because a shehnai in a hall means somebody is about to be married or engaged and everybody in that room has been trained by a hundred such evenings to look.

The rings are exchanged in the middle of the room. Dimple's hand comes out sideways and finds her son's sleeve while she watches, and holds it there, and he stands still and lets her.

Everyone claps, and Tina's mother cries, and Parmeet puts his hand on Tina's father's shoulder. "Abhi to sirf shuruaat hai."

## The Wrist

The hall at half past nine with the chandelier on and the tables being carried out and the floor opened up.

The rings are on and the hall is working again.

Two of the caterer's boys are taking a long table out through the middle of it, one at each end, both of them looking at their own feet.

Purvi stands at the side of the hall with a tray of empty glasses against her hip and her back to the wall, waiting for a gap.

Vihaan is coming down the last three stairs with his phone at his ear, saying something about a payment that has to be made in London before the banks there close, and he is not looking at the hall at all.

## The Dance

The hall at ten with the chandelier on and the floor cleared back to the pillars.

The band in the hall has gone into something with a dholak and a synth under it, and the aunts have started moving their shoulders, and the children have been cleared off the middle of the floor.

Purvi goes across it to him.

"Sir—" Her voice is quiet and her ears are hot. "Unhein aapke saath dance karna hai. Please."

Aryaman looks at her the way he looks at a bad cut in a package — head down, eyes up — and says something about death, and about who exactly is dying, and takes a drink.

Aryaman turns his shoulder to her and says something to Roma that makes Roma laugh, and it is not unkind, and it is worse than unkind: it is a man finishing with a subject.

Three hundred people have watched her walk out onto that floor. Three hundred people are watching her stand on it.

And then the aunts begin the game, because the aunts begin the game at every sangeet in this country and nobody in that hall is going to stop them tonight.

"Jodi banao! Jodi banao!" Pinky is on her feet with her glass up, and her voice goes over the top of the band. "Jiski jodi nahin bani, uski shaadi der se hogi. Ye riwaz hai — main nahin banaya."

The floor fills up in a rush. The aunts take their husbands. The uncles take their wives. Two of the children are pushed together and scream. Tina's cousin goes to Roma and Roma goes without a word. Pinky drags Arjun off a chair by his wrist and he lets himself be dragged. Even Dimple is standing with her hand out, and Parmeet gets up out of the big chair and takes it, and the room claps that, and the band plays for it.

And there are two people left on the floor.

Purvi is still on the floor, with her hands at her sides. Vihaan is by the chairs with the house book open on the seat beside him and a pen in his hand, and he has not moved, because he came into that room to get a signature and not to be in a photograph or a dance or anything else in it.

The whole hall turns and looks at the two of them at once, and the noise it makes is not cruel. It is worse than cruel. It is delighted.

"Arre!" One of the aunts has both hands on her cheeks. "Ye do bache hue hain! Jodi adhoori hai!"

"Vihaan." Dimple says it from the middle of the floor, over her own dance, without turning round, in the voice of a woman who has run this house for twenty-four years. "Jodi poori kar. Ek gaana."

The band takes the hint and the dholak comes up.

Vihaan puts the book down on the seat of the chair and the pen on top of it.

Then he crosses the floor to her, and he stands in front of her, and he smiles small and private at her, and his jaw is loose, and his breath is slow, and his hand is steady on his thigh, and he holds his hand out.

His hand is steady and his face is open, and the whole of that hall has just decided this for the two of them, and he does not look away, and he does not know why his ears are hot, and he does not know why his chest feels full all at once.

"Aapki marzi hai," he says. "Par agar aap na bolengi, to mazaaq meri bhi udayenge."

"Aapki?"

"Main abhi tak jodi mein nahin tha." He says it plainly. "Aap aa gayin to main bhi aa gaya."

Purvi's hands come off her sides and go behind her back. Her ears are hot and her chin is up and she does the arithmetic of a girl who has been staff in that building for one evening, and who knows what a photograph is worth in a house of this size.

"Sir—"

"Vihaan."

"Vihaan." She says it, and her voice does something on it, and she puts her hand in his.

One string, low, under the band — and one line sung over it in a woman's voice, *Woh do log, wohi raasta* — and then the song the band is playing takes it back.

The hall makes a sound of its own, all round them, the way halls do.

It is not a slow song. It takes them a bar and a half to find the beat, and she is stiff as a new door, and her palm is damp, and the aunties have already started talking behind their hands.

"Aap count kar rahi hain." He says it at her ear, at a volume that belongs to two people and not to a hall.

"Kya?"

"Aap count kar rahi hain. Andar se."

He turns her, and the room goes round them, and her heel comes down on the marble and does not slip.

"Teen pe."

"Main—"

"Doosre pe nahin. Teesre pe." He is not smiling. "Aap chhoti si ek uchhal leti hain teen pe. Aur usse hi aap theek lagti hain."

Her face does nothing for a moment, and then everything at once, with joy, and she laughs, once, on the beat, and turns her face into his shoulder and hides it there.

And then, three turns later, with her hand in his and the dholak going, the hall goes away.

The lamp at the end of the hall gutters — once, and then twice — and comes back. The doors at the far end stand open on the lawn and the wind comes through them and takes the marigold off the pillar and lays it on the marble, and nobody in that hall is looking at the marigold.

Purvi's hand goes cold in his.

And under the dholak, under the singing, at a volume below the band: a hiss.

Dry and short, one second, and there is nothing in that hall with a mouth that could make it, and nobody in that hall hears it but the two of them, and neither of them says anything about it.

It starts at the base of her spine and goes up, the way it went up in the dark of the temple three nights ago. Her skin does the thing her skin has done twice in her life. She has to hold on to a man's shoulder in a room full of three hundred people to keep her feet.

"Aap theek hain?"

"Haan." It is not a yes. Her voice has gone thin and her eyes are wide and she is looking at nothing at all, at the far doors, at the lawn beyond them. "Haan. Main— haan."

"Aankhein band kar lijiye."

She does it, with her chin up, in the middle of the floor.

"Ab dholak sun lijiye."

His hand is flat and steady on her back.

"Bas wohi. Aur kuch nahin."

She listens to the drum. The cold goes down off her arms. Somewhere behind them a child laughs, and the band comes back up, and she opens her eyes, and the gold at the edge of them has gone out.

Four bars of the dholak.

His hand on her back does not move.

Her pulse is under his fingers and it is fast, and it does not slow down.

The lamp over the mandap gutters once and comes back up, and nobody in the hall looks at it except one man.

The photographer is up on a chair at the side with the long lens, and he takes it at that moment, because the light goes and comes back and the two of them are in the frame with the lamp behind them and her hand is white on his shoulder.

He is paid by the picture and not by the hour, and he takes four more of the same pair before the song ends, and the flash goes across the hall each time, once, hard, and the hall squints and goes on dancing.

"Aryaman." Tina's uncle has his glass up and his voice pitched to carry across to the sofa where Aryaman has gone to stand. "Yeh kya ho raha hai? Apni assistant ke saath?"

"Wo humare channel ki reporter hain." Vihaan says it without turning round, still dancing, and he has not raised his voice, and it carries anyway. "Aur unhein dance achha aata hai."

The laugh goes round the room and stops being a laugh at the far end of it.

"Practice mat chhodna.", the score sounding under the teasing (m05:58, second of two).

"Nahin Deep."
