## The Shared Night

The upstairs corridor past midnight holds one burning lamp and a bar of moonlight from the stairwell window, and the wedding's marigold sleeps along the rails.

Vihaan sits his guard outside Pinky's shut door with a blanket over his shoulders and the file turning where sleep should be: the bride driving alone at midnight, the torn brother brought home, the gasped name through open windows, the earring answer told too smoothly.

He files nothing and forgets nothing, and the lamp burns an inch down.

Her door opens without sound, and his wife stands in it in her nightdress with the day's sindoor still in her maang.

"Neend nahin aa rahi," she says.

"Mujhe bhi nahin," he says.

They meet in the moon's bar between their doors, and neither says whose fault the corridor is.

She looks wrecked and held in the way of people who have decided something and hate the deciding.

He looks at her the way he looks at a page that has been tampered with beautifully — for the join, not the tear.

"Andar aa sakta hoon?" he asks.

She steps back from the door and lets him pass.

It is the second night running he has asked it, and the second night she has not said no.

Her room keeps the lamp low and the windows open to the night's end.

On the dresser the milk glass stands where the servant left it in the morning, filmed over and long cold, beside the earring's empty half.

They talk the way the night lets married strangers talk — of nothing, and of everything under it.

The wedding.

The house asleep.

Tomorrow's small errands.

He asks none of the questions he has for her; each one rises and is put back unasked, because the night is thin and she is thinner, and there is no hate anywhere in him to spend on her — only the questions, and the pull that stays his hand.

His head goes back against the chair, once, twice.

The third time it stays.

The day and the night before it take him sitting up, the way exhaustion takes honest men, and his breathing evens out in her chair.

The air by the window thickens — and Ananta steps out of the curtain's shadow, human-plain, and stops dead at the sight of the sleeping man.

For one breath she only looks at him.

Something in her face checks mid-step, the way a foot checks at an edge in the dark, and she does not say what it is.

Then she turns to her sister, and her eyes carry old sorrow the way vessels carry water.

"Bahut khush hoon," she breathes, low enough not to wake him.

"Meri shaadi wale din sab poora lagta tha — phir sab chheen liya gaya. Par tujhe — tujhe tera pyaar mil gaya."

"Nafrat karti hoon usse," Ahana hisses back, fierce and low, with a glance at the chair.

"Vihaan se. Mera parivaar uski wajah se gaya — ye main kaise bhooloon?"

"Ye nafrat nahin hai," Ananta says softly.

"Ye chhupa hua pyaar hai, Ahana."

"Chup," Ahana says.

"Bilkul chup."

Ananta looks once more at the sleeping man — at the shut face, the empty hands, the stillness that is too deep for a house like this — and steps back into the curtain's shadow, and the air thins, and she is gone.

Ahana stands a long moment in the middle of her own room.

Then she lies down on the bed beside the chair where her husband sleeps, and the lamp burns, and the night goes on.

In sleep his body does what it knows.

His arm finds her across the sheet; his breathing slows, and hers slows under his; he shields, and nothing in him wakes to claim it.

The lamp steadies.

The wind stills at the window.

The moon keeps its watch over them both, and nobody explains a thing.

One low string is struck somewhere above the hush and left hanging — no melody, and nobody's to thank.

## The Mines

Aryaman's room keeps its curtains drawn past ten, and the morning light comes in at the edges thin as a blade.

He wakes with his mouth full of ash and his cut hand throbbing under its handkerchief, and the wedding night comes back to him in pieces — the bottles, the road, the woman's hand hauling him clear, Radhika's shoulder with no Radhika in it.

He sits up groaning.

Vikram stands in his doorway already dressed, bruise and all, with yesterday's hall still in his eyes and his keys still in his fist.

"Gaye sab," Vikram says.

"Dhawan ki mines. Heere ke peeche. Teri — "

He stops.

"Ahana bhi gayi hai. Un teenon ke saath."

"Main dekh raha hoon," Vikram says, before Aryaman can answer.

"Wait and watch — mera vaada hai. Par tu — tu ja. Tujhe bechaini ho rahi hai, mujhe dikh raha hai."

Aryaman goes because his friend is right about the restlessness and wrong about its name.

It is not the diamond.

It is the mines — Dhawan's temper, the girl walking into it with those three smiling uncles, something in the telling that smells off to a nose trained on news.

He follows at a distance, and hangs back at the gates.

## Papers and a Pendrive

The highway runs empty under the afternoon glare, and an army officer's car holds the middle lane alone.

The papers ride in a case on the seat beside him, and the pendrive with them — paper and a drive with the code in it, twenty days from a missile, the country's safety in a case he will not let out of his sight.

His cap sits squared on the dashboard, and the mirror holds nothing but road.

He drives with both hands and both eyes, careful, the mirror checked and empty.

The truck comes out of the heat-shimmer where no truck should be, and the accident writes itself across the road in a scream of brakes that nobody hears in time, the score sounding twice over the wreck (m11:53+m12:00 as one).

When the dust settles the car is wreckage and the officer is dead in it, and the sealed case is gone from the seat.

What took it leaves no print on the page — no face, no hand, no engine-sound.

Only the empty road, and the glare, and the dead man guarding nothing.

## The Feed

Vihaan's study keeps the afternoon behind slatted blinds, and the feed runs across his desk in three lit windows.

The accident comes in on the city wire first — an officer dead on the highway, car wrecked, case missing — and then on the channel's own ticker, and then as pictures: the road, the glare, the sheet.

He reads it once for the facts and once for the writing, because that is what his father keeps him for.

The facts are clean.

The writing is not.

An accident report filed too fast and too smooth, every edge sanded before the dust settled — the minister's file carries the same sanding, two cuts under the polish, and for saying so out loud in his father's house they keep him on.

He takes up his diary and enters one line under the date, in his own hand, and shuts it.

The doubt is a page now, not a feeling.

It will wait.

Everything in his file waits, and nothing in it rots.

Through the window the wedding's last marigold string hangs torn across the gate, swaying a finger's width in no wind at all.

## The Ultimatum

"Bam bam Bhole," the temple breathes.

"Shiv Shiv Shambhu. Bam bam Bhole," the score sounding under the chant (m21:01).

No name.

The law needs none.

"Kudrat ke khilaaf hai ye. Naaglok ke bhi."

"Ateet gawah hai."

## The Book and the Plea

Bharani moves before the word is out of Ahana's mouth, and the temple wind rises screaming past the lamps.

"Bharani — bandhan kholo! Abhi ke abhi! Ye aadesh hai mera — kholo ise!"

"Ek minute."

"Mahadev."

"Bholenath — raasta dikhaiye, kar nahin paa rahi main jo karna hai. Raasta dikhaiye. Meri madad kijiye. Raasta dikhaiye."

## Smriti

The temple goes so quiet after the prayer that the lamps can be heard.

Bharani comes and stands over the unconscious man, and teaches, because that is what elders are for.

"Is vidya se," she says, the score sounding under the teaching (m25:43), "Kisi bhi insaan ka mann — is vidya se naagin ke vash mein. Aur mauka milte hi — uski yaaddasht se dhoye ja sakte hain din. Smriti lobh vidya."

"Kya mere paas hai ye?" Ahana asks.

"Smriti lobh vidya — hai mere paas, Bharani?"

"To tum aisa karogi."

Ahana straightens, and the Naagrani comes back into her voice like a crown set down.

"Tum iski aankhon mein dekho. Iske zehen se ye din nikaal do — ke isne mujhe dekha."

"Ye mere bas mein nahin," Bharani says.

"Tum aisa karogi, Bharani."

Steel now.

"Ye mera aadesh hai."

"Smriti lobh vidya," Bharani says slowly, "janm se nahin milti. Prapt karni padti hai. Aur ise paana — samudra manthan se kam nahin. Jis tarah manthan ke waqt shakti nahin — sanyam aur tapasya chahiye thi — theek usi tarah ye vidya bhi kisi tap se kam nahin hai."

"Main har tap ke liye taiyaar hoon."

Ahana does not blink.

"Bas batao — milegi kahan?"

"Wahin jahan hum sabki utpatti hui thi."

Bharani's voice drops.

"Samudra jao. Har jeev ki utpatti wahin. Wahin ugti hai ek anokhi neeli jadibooti, the score sounding under the herb (m26:47) — uske sahaare ye vidya tum tak aayegi. Par use paana aasaan nahin hoga — samudra ke jeev khud pehra dete hain us par. Kisi naagin ke bas ka nahin, use paana."

"Mere bas mein hoga."

Ahana lifts her chin.

"Main haasil karoongi ye vidya. Main khud jaaongi use paane."

"Jab tak main na lautun," she says, "tum ise kuch nahin karogi, Bharani. Ye mera aadesh hai — tumhaari Naagrani ka aadesh."

"Aur agar aapke jaane ke baad isne bhaagne ki koshish ki — "

"Mere lautne tak," Ahana says, "ye naag-rakshak Aryaman ko kahin nahin jaane denge."

The shadows between the pillars thicken and move, though no lamp goes out — coils and eyes and the dry sound of scales on stone, settling round the unconscious man.

A hiss goes up with no snake in frame, and settles, and the temple holds its guard.

She goes out of the temple alone into the night, toward the sea.

The episode ends.
