## Kachcha Chaba Jayenge

The sheds at the river end of the plot at four in the morning, with one bulb over the gate and the water beyond it going from black to black.

The river is low and the bank under the sheds is mud that holds a foot.

Sheshnaag's hood is not shown, but the bank feels like a temple that has forgotten its god.

A cycle stands against the wall with a sack over its seat.

There is a bucket by the gate with the rope still on it, and the rope goes down into the dark and nobody pulls it up.

The crocodile man walks the bank with his hands behind his back and eleven men stand at the gate and watch him walk it.

The men have been brought here from three different places in three different cars and none of them has been told why.

One of the eleven is an old man.

He has been with this work for thirty years.

He carries the torch because his eyes have gone and he knows the animals by their sound, and tonight he holds the torch on the water and says what he has said in this yard for thirty years.

"Bhai sahab, woh bachche nahin hain. Woh—"

"Humein Naagmani laani hogi kuch bhi karke."

The crocodile man does not turn round.

"Warna humein kachcha chaba jayenge ye magarmach."

The gate opens and the old man is walked through it by two of the others, and he does not shout, because he has been in this trade for thirty years and he knows what shouting is worth at the river.

The bucket by the gate goes over on its side.

Two of the eleven came down in the same van as the torch, and the van has a pass in a plastic sleeve on its dashboard, and the pass is good for fifteen days and says nothing about crocodiles.

Nobody at the gate is told what the paper says.

The yard's month runs at six lakh, the cheque for it is signed on the channel's fourth floor where none of the eleven has ever been, and without the paper in that sleeve the eleven are standing on a riverbank at four in the morning with a bucket and a torch.

Behind the sheds, where the light does not reach, the water takes a long time to go quiet.

A man's phone rings at the gate and the man looks at it and does not answer it.

The phone rings a second time and he holds it out to the crocodile man, and the crocodile man puts it to his ear.

"Bhai sahab, woh Arora waala mamla—"

"Baad mein."

Then, into another phone, with his voice changed all the way through, the score sounding twice over the summons (m01:17 x2 as one): "Jaldi aake milo — dad, Aryaman, mujhe tumse bahut zaroori baat karni hai."

The bulb over the gate is still on when the cars go, and the bucket is still on its side, and the mud at the bank has one set of marks going down into the water and none coming back.

## Beta, Ye Kahani Real Hai

The front room of the Suri house at six in the morning, the chandelier off, the light coming in flat and grey from the garden doors.

The wedding is one day gone and the room still smells of it: marigold gone brown in the vases, a sweet crushed into the carpet where somebody walked on it, the wedding silver still on its sheet in the corner with one fold turned back.

Two men are on their knees at the far end with a roll of carpet between them and a bucket of water gone cold beside the bucket that is warm.

A tray on the side table holds four glasses and all four are empty, and the tray leans on one corner where a hand has come down hard on it.

Parmeet sits at the head of the table in his dressing gown with a glass of water in front of him and the morning's paper beside it, and the paper has not been opened.

He is tired and he is frightened, and he is a man who has spent his life not showing either, so his face does nothing at all while his jaw works once under the skin.

Vihaan sits at the table's far end, in the chair Aryaman usually takes, his coat still on, his hands folded on the tabletop.

His jaw is tight, and his thumb is steady on his own hand.

He has been here since five.

Nobody asked him to stay.

He is smiling, small and private, at nothing.

The hiss comes out of the half of the room where the chandelier is off.

It is not long and it is not loud, and it comes from a place with nothing in it but the shut garden doors and the dark behind the sofa, and every head in that room turns to it.

The two men at the far end stop with their hands in the carpet.

The tea boy stands in the doorway with the cup still on the tray and does not come into the room.

Aryaman's shoulder comes away from the doorframe and he looks, and there is nothing to look at, and the room knows it heard a hiss and says nothing about it.

"Apne logon ko dauda, net pe search kar," the score sounding under the order (m01:41).

Parmeet puts the glass down without drinking from it.

"Hamara channel hai itna bada."

"Dad, main aisi kahaniyon mein believe nahin karta."

"Beta, ye kahani real hai." Parmeet's hand comes flat on the table, and the tray of glasses knocks once against the wood, the score sounding under the power (m02:12).

"Woh ab humein dhoondhni hai. Itni powerful hai woh — mara hua insaan bhi zinda ho jata hai. Woh naagmani agar koi beemar chhu le to theek ho jata hai. Aaj bhi woh heera, woh naagmani, is desh ki mitti mein kahin dafan hai."

Outside, all at once, the crows go up out of the garden — a whole tree of them, black into the grey, screaming over the wall and away down the lane.

The two men on the carpet flinch where they kneel, and the tea boy's hand comes up in front of his face, and Parmeet does not turn his head.

He does not turn his head because he has been waiting to hear it.

"Dad—" Aryaman says, and stops.

He is angry, and under the anger he is a boy in his father's drawing room hearing crows at the wrong hour, and this house has always had a story about why.

"Dad, yeh sab—"

The phone rings in Parmeet's pocket.

Parmeet's hand goes flat over the pocket and stays there and he does not take it out.

The ring goes on against his dressing gown, muffled and patient, and the room hears it and the room is careful not to.

Vihaan's hand moves toward his own pocket, stops, and goes back to the tabletop.

His breath is steady.

The phone in Parmeet's pocket is Vihaan's phone — he left it in this house when he went to London, and never took it with him.

Nobody in the room says whose it is.

He looks at Parmeet's pocket, and then at the tabletop, and his hand stays where it is.

"Aur ye baat humein kaun bata raha hai?" Aryaman asks it with his eyes on his father's pocket rather than his father's face, and his own phone is face down in his other hand and has been face down all morning.

"Do log. Ek woh jo bolta hai, ek woh jo darta hai."

The phone stops.

Parmeet picks the paper up at last and opens it and does not read it.

"Tum apna kaam karo. Naam chahiye — kis ke paas hai, kahan hai. Baaki main dekh loonga." — one low string under the assurance.

The phone rings again, and this time it does not stop, and Parmeet lets it ring through the room while he holds the paper open at a page he is not looking at, and the two men at the far end lift the carpet up between them and carry it out past him in silence.

On the drive outside, the last of the wedding's cars is being washed down with a hose and the water goes into the gutter and away.

The washer is a boy of seventeen and he works with his back to the house.

He works with his back to the house because the gate dog will not come up the drive this morning.

It sits in the road with its ears down, looking at the garden doors, and it does not come when it is called, and does not come when it is shouted at.

The page of the paper lies open on the table after Parmeet has gone, and the wind is not moving anything else in that room.

Vihaan's chair is still pulled out, and he has not touched his glass of water.

## Attari

"Milne ka time maang loon?"

"Time nahin." Vihaan picks his jacket up.

## Dost

## Wapas

The gate of the Suri house at six in the evening, with the porch light on and the sun still standing on the wall, and the drive full of cars parked nose to tail with their mirrors folded in.

The marigold is up again on the gateposts and it has been up long enough to be going brown at the ends.

## Ahana Dhawan

The hall at half past seven, with the chandelier still on and the room still standing where it stood, and the doors to the drive open on the blue of the evening outside.

The pandit's tray has been taken away.

The ring box is on the chair where Vikram put it and nobody has picked it up.

The car at the steps is long and dark and the driver gets out and opens the door and stands at it, and the woman who comes up the steps has a trunk behind her that two men carry and set down on the porch, the score sounding five times over the entry (m17:02+m17:09+m17:15+m17:31+m17:39 as one).

The car's door shuts with a sound the drive has not made all evening, and the two men with the trunk wait at the bottom of the steps until somebody tells them.

The score sounds under the stop (m20:23).

Parmeet is standing with his phone in his hand and his face changed entirely, and the phone in his hand has the grey square on it, with the white place in it that could be a light on a wall, the score sounding again over the square (m20:23).

"Ye to Ahana Dhawan," the score sounding under the naming (m20:38).

He says it to the room without taking his eyes off her.

"Kisi bhi haalat mein rokna hoga humein is ladki ko."

He has been away six months.

The woman in the doorway is a name off a property file, with a trunk on the porch and a watch on her wrist, and there is no reason at all for the back of his neck to know her before his face does.

But it does.

The beat under his ribs has gone quick and cold, and he does not know why.

**He has not slept more than four hours in a night for six months, and every one of those nights has a different reason.**

The house does not know that.

What the house knows is that the boy who went away has come back with the London end doubled and his case still unpacked, and that he works at three in the morning and answers the phone before the second ring, and that he has not once, in six months, asked anybody in this family for anything.

He comes down the stairs into this hall an hour before her and the first thing he does is count the cars at the gate.

Four.

He has no reason to count them and he counts them anyway, and he has counted them before he has seen the trunk.

Vikram takes her hand and turns with her to the room, and Vihaan's hand comes down flat on the back of a chair, once, the way a man uses a chair when his hand has nowhere else to be.

"Chair bech ke nahi laane hain," he says to the boy beside him, and his voice is a size too big for the hall.

"Number ke hisaab se lagao, side mein do line chhodo. Aurat aayengi, unhe uthna baithna hai."

Two men stand by the trunk at the bottom of the steps waiting to be told.

He goes down to them and tells them himself, which is four steps and a job that is not his, and he points at the stairs the servants use and then takes his hand back as if the pointing had cost him something.

A low string is struck once under him and left hanging, and the boy with the tray says "sahab" to him, and it stops.

He walks back into the hall and decides, on the way, that it is the heat and the crowd and the fact that Vikram has combed his hair with water and is standing in the middle of the floor holding a stranger's hand like a man who has found something.

All of that is a reason.

It is not the reason, and he does not take it out and look at it.

But when Vikram's hand closes on Ahana's, Vihaan's jaw works once under the skin, and he looks away.
