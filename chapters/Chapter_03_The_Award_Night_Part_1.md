# Chapter 3 — The Award Night (Part 1)

*Episode reference: Naagin, season 7, Episode 1, "Naagin Legacy Unleashed" (last part).*

*Draft; not yet accepted.*

### TITLES

The logotype comes up over thundercloud, with something red falling through the frame too slowly to be rain.

A man's voice asks two words in the pitch of a been: *"Kaun hai woh."* A choir of women answers underneath him and leaves the answer unfinished.

The been stops. One low string is struck and left hanging over the black.

A drum, once. Mahadev's trishul is not shown, but the thunder is his. Then the story, and no music under it until the story asks.


## The Newsroom

Nine o'clock at night. The channel's building stands at the edge of the city with the towers still lit behind it, and the logo on its roof is lit too, and it throws its light over the road.

Inside, the newsroom is all screens and noise. The ticker runs along under the ceiling. A woman at the makeup mirror clips an earring on and reads her script at the same time. Somebody's phone goes off and nobody answers it.

At the back of the room, by the tall screen, a young man sits at a desk that is not his with a laptop open on it and one earbud in his ear. He is not in a suit. His sleeves are rolled and his hair is pushed back off his forehead and coming down again, and he wears a good watch that is loose on his wrist, and he is at work at nine at night in a room where everybody else is talking about a party.

"Verma, sun." He turns the screen a little away from the light. "Jo teen invoices kal ja rahi hain, unme se do par mera signature nahin hoga. Unme se ek par likha hai ki payment ninety days mein hoga. Hum ninety din kisi ko nahin dete. Unhe kaho ki forty-five pe sign karein, warna next quarter se rate badal jaayega."

Verma listens, and turns a page with one finger, a small, tired smile touching his mouth that he does not let reach his eyes.

"Aur haan. Jo Kumbh ka file hai—" He stops, and looks up, because the whole floor is looking up.

A woman comes past with a clipboard. "Vihaan, sir, party ka bill—"

"London se."

"Sir, yeh India ka kharacha hai."

"Toh Kumbh ke kharche mein likhwa do," he says without taking his eyes off the screens. "Mere paas aaj raat hisaab karne ka waqt nahin hai."

"Of course." She goes, and says something to the woman beside her, and the woman beside her laughs, and neither of them looks back at him.

On the screens, the anchor is talking.

"Poore Bharat desh ki nazar aaj hamare channel par hai. Jald hum announce karenge ki 2025 ka Journalist of the Year Award ka winner kaun hai. Lagataar teesri baar—" The screen fills with a face. The newsroom stops to watch it. "—is Aryaman Suri!"

The room claps. Somebody whistles. At the desk at the back, the young man lifts his head and looks at the screen, and the light of it comes off his face, and his earbud is still in.

In the cabin behind the glass, Aryaman Suri sits with his feet on the desk.

Aryaman is in his shirt with the tie pulled loose, a glass at his elbow, and the shelves behind him carry his trophies, and this year's award is already among them where everybody can see it, because he put it there himself.

Aryaman is twenty-nine and looks older in a room and younger in a photograph, which is the whole of his luck. He has a heavy face and a heavy jaw and no softness in it, and he carries no face of that family at all, which nobody in the house has ever said out loud. His hair is cut close at the sides and stands up at the front where he has run his hand through it, and there is grey coming at his temples that he does not cover. He is broad where his brother is tall. He wears no rings and one watch and the shirt is the best shirt in the building. When he drinks, his eyes stay exactly as they are, and it is the eyes that make people careful with him. The newsroom is being decorated on the other side of the glass by his sisters, and he is watching that, and not the screens.

His phone is on the desk, face up, and it keeps lighting.

"Sir." Manoj leans round the cabin door with a tablet held to his chest, and Manoj is thirty-one and has a wife and a middle and no illusions about either, and his suspenders are over his shirt and his lanyard is crooked. "Home Minister, Chief Minister, State Secretary ke office se call aaya hai. Aur Head of State ne message bheja hai. Aur do businessmen ne."

"Kya kehte hain?"

"Woh—" The young man looks at the tablet. "Woh sir, kuch important hai toh bataayein."

"Kuch important?" Aryaman takes his feet off the desk. "You know something? Life changing."

"Life changing, sir?" Manoj looks at his tablet.

The woman with the clipboard is at the glass. "Sir, life changing baat meri hai." She says it to the room, pleased and nervous. "Meri shaadi ho rahi hai."

Manoj's face does not know what to do.

"That is horrible. Matlab tum khudkushi kar rahi ho." Aryaman puts the glass down. "That is sad. Very sad."

"Nahin sir, main toh bahut khush hoon." She has her hand on her own chest. "Mera bachpan se ek hi sapna tha ki meri shaadi ho."

"Toh Matlab tum bachpan se hi suicide ho." Aryaman thinks about it. "Look, to each their own. But phir bhi — tumhari aatma ko shanti bhi de."

"Sir?"

"Prarthana karunga."

She goes out, and the clipboard is against her chest, and she is laughing at the door.

The woman with the braid comes past his chair, laughing into her hand, and cannot stop. "Ajib paagal aadmi hai Aryaman Suri. Khushi ke aise mauke pe kaun aise bolta hai?"

"Bina filter ke woh kisi ko kuch bhi bol deta hai." Manoj is beside her, and the tablet is under his arm. "Saara din peeta rehta hai. Phir bhi apne kaam mein koi galti nahin karta. Unbelievable, sir."

"Sir, aapki jitni zyada taarif ki jaaye na, utni kam hai." Manoj looks up from the tablet.

"Aur tumhari jitni kam, utni zyada hai." Aryaman says it to the glass.

The young man is back with the tablet. "Sir, main na aapse yeh keh raha tha ki aaj nau baje wali news mein aapke award ke baare mein bata dein."

"Tumhari wife ne kal raat lehsun ki chatni chatayi thi." Aryaman turns his chair. "Yeh bhi news mein bata dein?"

"Arre sir, yeh toh mamooli si baat hai."

"Award jeetna bhi mamooli hi baat hai." He says it to the glass, to the room, to the screens. "Look, hamara kaam hai important cheezein dikhana. Relevant cheez dikhana. Na ki yeh aam cheez hai, ki kaun sa award, ki just have it."

"Main diet pe hoon." The young man pats his own middle, which is not the problem.

"Lagta hai tumhari diet tumse zyada struggle kar rahi hai." Aryaman looks at his middle.

"Aajkal, sir, main patla ho raha hoon na, thoda."

"I can see that."

The score comes up over the newsroom, brass and dhol, and the screens all change at once and the room turns to the door.

The lamp on Parmeet's desk flickers once, twice, then stands straight. Sheshnaag's hood is not shown, but the weight of it is on the glass.

## The Mandap

The newsroom floor at nine at night, and his sisters have made it into a mandap.

Marigold. Ropes of it over the screens and along the beams and down the glass of the cabins. A sheet of it on the floor in a circle. Balloons tied to the anchors' desks. A garland hung over the place where the weather girl stands.

On the anchors' desk the autocue still stands, with the last sheet of the evening's script clipped into it, and the marigold has been laid across the top line of it.

A tray of loose garlands goes round the room on one of the debate chairs, and somebody has tied a card to the back of the chair with his name on it.

At the door of the hall, garlands hang on either side.

The whole floor is full of people and the whole floor is loud.

"Mere achhe khaase newsroom ko poora shaadi ka mandap bana diya inhon ne." Aryaman comes out of his cabin with his arms out.

"Pinky aur Nisha—" A cousin with his arms folded, delighted. "Aur karte bhi kya bhai? Aap kahin bahar toh chalte nahin ho, hamare doston se milne."

The other cousin straightens his jacket and opens both arms. "Isliye hamare saare dost aapse milne yahin chale aaye, bhai." "Bhaichara! Bhai, bhaichara!"

"Congratulations!" The photographers' flashes go off along the wall.

"Tumhari kamyabi dekh ke, I am really feeling good." The cousin takes Aryaman's hand and holds it.

The brass comes again under the noise.

"Yeh kya happiness hai bhai?"

The sisters are on Aryaman before he can answer, one on each arm, and their bangles are going and the marigold is coming down off one of the screens.

"Arjun aur Karan." Nisha says it for the room, and she has them by the shoulders, one on each side. "Tera saga bhai, yaani hamara cousin."

"Yeh apne jaisa bilkul bhi nahin hai. Kam se kam Arjun hamari sunta toh hai, aur mere kaafi close bhi hai."

"Aur waise sach kahoon, bhai — Karan se mujhe waise bhi Duryodhan wali vibe aati hai, aur aapko yeh khaas pasand bhi nahin karta hai."

Six people laugh at once, Pinky's full and loud over the rest, and Nisha grins beside her. Karan does not.

"Ab main koi pizza toh nahin hoon jo sabko pasand aa jaaun." Aryaman spreads his hands.

"Nahin nahin bhai. Karan bhai aapko pasand karte hain." Arjun puts an arm round him. "Bas thoda arrogant samajhte hain."

"Arrogant?" Aryaman takes the arm off. "Main allergic hoon stupidity se. Stupid logon se. And specially stupid logon ki bheed se. Aur isiliye main bheed mein nahin."

"That is so mean, bhai." Pinky's hands are on her hips. "Humne kitne pyaar se aapke liye party arrange ki, aur aap aise behave kar rahe ho." Aryaman's mouth twitches in a small, reluctant smile he tries to kill before it shows.

"Aur bhai tumhare arrangement ko shaadi ka decoration bol rahe hain." Arjun looks from the sisters to Aryaman. "Aur humein baraat hi—"

"O please, shut up, guys." Aryaman has his glass up. "Kitni LED decoration ki hum logon ne."

"O bhai, aap—" Nisha pushes in. "I know main aapki cousin sister hoon, isliye aapko meri choices buri lag sakti hain. But bechaari Pinky — yeh toh aapki sagi behen hai na. Toh iski pasand aap kaise naapasand kar sakte ho?"

"I know. I know."

"Aap yeh bolne waale ho ki aapko ladkiyaan generally pasand nahin aati." Her voice goes up. "But we are the sisters. How can you hate us? I love you, bol!"

"Lekin mujhe dikhawa bilkul pasand nahin hai."

"Par tumhare saare ke saare dost dikhawe ke alawa kuch nahin karte hain."

The music stops. Half the room turns. Aryaman puts the glass down.

"Par ab mujhe bahut kaam hai. Toh yeh toh party karne ka bekaar kaam — tum sab karo. Main bahar nahin aa raha hoon."

"Sir." The young man with the tablet is at his elbow again, and his voice is careful. "Parmeet sir aur Baljeet sir aa gaye."

Aryaman's jaw moves once.

"Ab toh bahar jaana hi padega."

They come through the marble lobby together, the two of them, in dark suits, and the floor of that room reorganises itself around them without anyone being told to. Parmeet Suri is grey-bearded and heavy now, his glasses catching the light, and he walks through his son's newsroom looking at the screens and not at the people. Baljeet is thinner, and he is watching his brother.

"Mubarak ho." Parmeet takes his son in his arms, there in front of the room, and Aryaman's glass goes up over his father's shoulder. "Thank you, Dad."

The two of them stand in front of Aryaman. Nobody in the room speaks. The marigold moves a little on its ropes.

Parmeet's eyes go past his shoulder, and Aryaman follows them, and the camera goes with him.

The young man from the desk at the back is coming across the floor with his laptop shut under his arm. He has seen the two of them, and he is not hurrying, and he is already reaching for his jacket on the chair.

"Aur tum." Aryaman raises his glass. "Tumhein invitation chahiye?"

"Main London ki line pe hoon."

The room laughs, once, all together, and it is not unkind.

Parmeet does not laugh. He looks at the tall young man with the laptop under his arm for one second longer than a man looks at a guest, and then he looks back at the son who is holding the glass.

## The Two Brothers

The corridor outside the newsroom smells of paint and cold coffee, and the tube light in it is on and nobody has turned it off. The party is a wall of noise behind a shut door.

Aryaman comes out with his glass and shuts the door behind him and stands against the wall.

"I heard the joke," he says, without turning round. "You are funny, they will tell you. You are not funny."

Vihaan sets the laptop down on the floor against the wall and stands with his back to his brother, because his face is easier to hold that way. His jaw is tight, and his fingers curl against his palm, and a smile touches his mouth and dies, and his breath comes shallow and does not go all the way down, and his ears burn at the brother who is drunk and hurting and not in a state to hear anything he could say.

"Sunday ko sab log aate hain." Aryaman looks at him along the corridor. "London chhodke, is newsroom mein, is ghar mein. Kya chahiye tujhe yahan se?"

"Ghar."

"Ghar." Aryaman laughs once. "Is ghar se?"

"Yeh mera ghar hai." Vihaan's voice does not change. "Saara jeevan yahi raha hai. Aur koi jagah nahin hai, sir."

Aryaman looks at him for a while, and the noise of the party goes on behind the door, muffled, a hundred people not listening to it.

"Sunday, na. Papa ke saath." He puts his glass down on the floor by the wall. "Woh phone pe sab kuch karte hain. Woh naye office ka baat karenge, aur woh mujhe—" He stops. "Woh mujhe pasand nahin karte, tujhe pata hai?"

Vihaan looks at the floor between his own feet. There is something he could say here and it has been in his mouth for years, and his brother is drunk and hurting and not in a state to hear it, so he says nothing, and the nothing costs him more than saying it.

"Tujhe pata hai." Aryaman picks his glass up again. "Woh sab ko pasand karte hain aur mujhe nahin. Aur main—" He drinks. "Main wahan hoon jahan main hoon."

Vihaan's jaw has gone tight at the corner and he lets it, and does not answer, and the corridor is cold on the back of his neck.

Far down the corridor, an office door opens and closes.

"Corridor mein kyun aa gaya tu?" A woman's voice, behind them, kind and tired. "Arre, yeh laptop bhi le aaye. Andar chalo, dono. Papa baith gaye hain aur ek photo chahiye."

"He is not in the photo," Aryaman says.

"No?"

"I am in the photo." Aryaman pushes himself off the wall. "He is in the photo also. He is just at the edge of it." He goes back in, and the noise takes him, and the door shuts.

The corridor is quiet. The young man picks up his laptop.

## Father and Son

Parmeet's car stands at the channel's gate with the engine running and the driver's door open. He is standing beside it with his phone in one hand and a cigarette in the other, and the towers are lit behind him and the gate light makes a little day of the pavement.

The glass doors give out a crowd and a stripe of wedding light, and Aryaman comes down the steps through it with the jacket over two fingers at his shoulder and the citation in its folder in his hand.

One more man comes down the steps after them: Vihaan, laptop shut under one arm and his jacket on him. He stands a little way off from the two of them, where a man who has just come out of his family's party can stand without being in it, and waits for the glass.

Nobody says anything. The gate light hums.

"Koi mujhse bhi baat karega?" he says, into it, and when nobody answers he answers it himself: "Achha."

"Vihaan."

"Papa."

"Das baje." Parmeet says it to the towers. "Desk hai teri, kursi hai. Woh news ka kaam nahin. Woh business ka kaam. London ka office jo tu chalata hai, uska hisaab humein dukaan ke bahar nahin jaanna chahiye."

"Samajh gaya."

"Kuch orders aa gaye hain—" Parmeet takes his phone up. "Nahin, baad mein."

Parmeet walks a few steps away, along the pavement, from the gate light into the dark and back into the light.

"Woh dono bhai aaj apne apne haath aage leke aaye." Parmeet's voice changes. "Aur maine baith ke dekha ki meri kya galti hai."

"Papa, unhein nahin pata."

"Unhein nahin pata." Parmeet turns round. "Tujhe pata hai?"

The young man does not answer at once. He looks at his father, and the gate light is on one side of his face, and his hand clenches on the laptop strap, and his jaw is tight, and his ears are hot.

"Tumhare paas koi nahin hai, bachche." Parmeet takes a pull on the cigarette. "Is ghar mein lonely hoga. Woh mujhe pata hai."

Parmeet gets into the car. The door shuts. The glass comes down an inch.

"Monday, Vihaan."

The car goes out through the gate. The gate light flickers and steadies. The young man stands in it until the tail-lights have gone, and then he goes up the steps into the noise. His jaw is tight, and his hand is light on the doorframe, and his breath is shallow. He does not look back at the dark road. He does not know why his chest feels empty all at once, and he does not know what he is waiting for, and he does not know who he is waiting for.
