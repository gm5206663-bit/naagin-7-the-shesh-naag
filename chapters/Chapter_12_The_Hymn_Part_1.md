# Chapter 12 — The Hymn (Part 1)

*Episode reference: Naagin, season 7, Episode 4, "Naagin Legacy Unleashed" (last part).*

*Draft; not yet accepted.*

### TITLES

*The twelve seconds the season plays on every night after its first: the theme, then straight in (`canon/TITLES_SEQUENCE.md` §I). The full forty seconds are written once, at the head of Chapter 1, and are not repeated below it.*

The logotype comes up over thundercloud, with something red falling through the frame too slowly to be rain.

A man's voice asks two words in the pitch of a been: *"Kaun hai woh."* A choir of women answers underneath him and leaves the answer unfinished.

The been stops. One low string is struck and left hanging over the black.

A drum, once. Then the story, and no music under it until the story asks.


## The Leftovers

The lawn at twenty minutes to eleven, with the light of the house standing in its windows, and the party over in the way a party is over: the guests are still out there, and the work has already started behind them.

The service gate at the side of the Suri house stands open on the lane, and there is a wet patch of light on the road where the street lamp leans, and the rest of the lane has gone dark and smells of the drain and of somebody's jasmine.

Two steel trays come out of the gate. Vihaan carries them, one across each forearm, and they are heavier than they need to be because the cook has put the good bones in with the rice.

His sleeves are turned back twice and his collar is open at the throat and his watch has been pushed up his arm because his wrists are wet.

He has been at this since the last guest came up the drive, and before that at the gift book, and before that in the photograph, and there is nothing on his face about any of it. He smiles small and private at the empty trays, hand steady on the steel, the smile of a man doing a thing no one will thank him for.


The man at the end of the lane takes the trays.

He is old and thin, in a checked lungi, and there is a birthmark the size of a thumbprint over one eyebrow, and he comes out of the dark to take the food and does not thank anybody and has been doing this for eleven years.

"Sukhriya," Vihaan says.

The old man looks at him for a second, because nobody in that house says it.

He sets the trays down on the step without a word.

"Kaise ho?" Vihaan asks.

The old man does not look up.

He is already turning back to the dark.

"Khana theek tha?" Vihaan says.

"Bacha hai."

The old man's voice is dry, and he does not stop.

"Kal bhi de dena."

"Main de dunga."

The old man lifts a hand, not a wave, and steps back into the lane's shadow.

The party's music comes round the corner of the house: an old song about a wedding, the singer's voice going thin under the generator.

The air has turned cool, and it has turned in the last hour. The street lamp buzzes.

And across the lane, at the edge of a step, something is sitting.

Vihaan's hands stop on the empty trays.

The animal is low and long and sits the way nothing in this lane sits. Its coat is wrong for the season, thick at the neck and matted at the shoulder, and the tail is down and still, and it is looking at the light and not at any of the people.

No dog in Delhi sits the way this sits. No dog in Delhi is that size when it is sitting.

At the neighbour's door two of them come out.

The elder is Nandini, twenty-one, in a kurti and a towel round her wet hair, and she has a steel plate in her hand with the last of the evening's rotis on it. The younger is her brother Rohan, nineteen, in a college t-shirt with a printed name across it, in his pyjama, and his slippers are inside the house and he does not go back for them.

"Kya hai?" Nandini stops on the step.

"Didi, vo neeche ek dog baitha hai."

Rohan is already off the step.

"Cute sa. Usko roti aur doodh de do please."

"Tujhe jo karna hai kar."

"Arre ja yaar."

"Ye kya kar raha hai?" She says it to his back.

"Kuch nahin."

He is already halfway across.

"Roti. Doodh. Uske liye."

"Teri akal ne kutta kha liya hai kya?"

She follows him, plate still in her hand.

"Usse mat de na. Humara hai."

"Mera bhai hai wo. Mera farz hai."

"Farz kahan se aaya abhi?"

"Arre yaar." He takes the plate out of her hand and starts across, and their mother's voice comes from inside, and she does not come to the door.

"Rohan! Nange paanv mat nikal!"

"Chappal kahan hain?" Rohan calls back without turning.

"Kahan rakhi hoti hain!"

"Arre ja."

Vihaan takes one step into the lane.

And then another.

Nothing about the animal has changed. That is the thing he will remember afterwards: nothing about it changes.

The ears do not lift and the tail does not move. It sits and it looks at the light, and then its head comes round to the boy with the biscuits, slowly, the way a thing comes round when it has decided.

"Rohan."

Rohan does not hear him.

He is crooning at it, holding a biscuit out, and taking one more step.

"Rohan!" Vihaan is already across the middle of the lane, and his voice comes out of him at a size no part of him chose.

"Woh kutta nahi hai! Andar jao!", the score sounding under the calling (m24:26).

There is a half second in which the lane is very quiet. The mother's face appears at the door.

Nandini stops kicking the bricks. Rohan turns his head, and the biscuit is still in his hand, and the animal comes off the step.

The animal is not fast. It is worse than fast.

It goes across the lane low and gets the boy's forearm in its mouth and takes him down onto the step, and the plate goes and the biscuits go, and Rohan's scream is not a boy's scream, it is a sound going through a wall.

Vihaan moves. He does not fumble.

He gets a hand on Nandini's shoulder and puts her back through her own door, and he does not have anything in his hands except a steel tray, so he takes the tray.

Vihaan puts it between the animal and the boy and he goes in low with it, the flat edge first, and it clangs and the animal's weight comes into his shoulder and he goes back two steps and stays standing.

"Andar! Andar le jao!"

Nandini has her brother by both arms and is dragging him up the step, with fear. Their mother screams from inside the door.

The animal lets go and does not run. It stands there and looks at all of them and its shoulder is working under the coat.

Then it turns its head down the lane.

Vihaan looks where it looks.

Purvi and Ananta are coming round the corner of the Suri house, and Purvi has her dupatta in her hand, and Ananta is on her heels with a clutch bag under her arm, and neither of them has seen it yet.

"Ruko!"

Vihaan shouts it down the lane at the two of them.

"Ruko — peeche!"

"Kya hua?" Purvi calls back, already slowing.

"Peeche! Peeth pheirkar mat dekhna!"

Vihaan says it loud enough to carry.

"Ruko wahan!"

Ananta is pointing.

"Bhaago, Purvi! Khadi ke paas!" Her voice goes up over the lamp.

"Mat bhaago!"

Vihaan shouts.

"Dobara mat bhaago, Purvi! Wahan rukna!"

Purvi stops. She does not turn round.

She looks at him, and her mouth opens, and the animal goes.

## Run

The lane at the back of the Suri house at ten to eleven, with the moon down towards the ridge and the lamp swinging on its bracket and the generator going on behind the wall.

Ananta screams before her sister does.

The animal comes down the lane and Purvi gets in front of her, and the two of them turn to run the wrong way, into the dark part, past the lamp, and Ananta's heel goes at the first step and she goes down hard on one knee with her clutch under her.

"Ananta!" Purvi's voice breaks on the name.

"Chalo!"

Ananta is on one knee, clutch still in her hand, and she does not try to get up.

"Purvi, chalo!"

"Nahin. Tum baitho."

Purvi pulls her up by the arm.

"Chal, didi. Abhi chal."

"Purvi—"

"Chal!"

The animal is between them and the road.

Vihaan runs.

There is no plan in it and there is nothing in his hands, and the wind takes the lamp and the shadow moves under it, and he runs at the shadow.

The animal is between Purvi and the road. Purvi does not go back for her sister, and she does not go to the road.

She goes deeper into the dark, past the wall, past the rubbish, towards the plot where the buildings stop, and she is shouting as she goes, and the shouting is the thing that works: the animal turns.

Behind her the lane takes it up, the neighbours screaming the one word at her back, and the word is run, the score sounding twice over the screaming (m25:16 x2 as one).

"Didi!"

Purvi shouts it behind her.

"Didi, wahan mat jao! Wahan mat jao!"

"Shikaari ko apne peeche lagaana hoga — Didi ko bachaana hai to."

She says it to herself, at a run.

"Door. Door le jaana hoga.", the score sounding twice over the drawing-away (m25:54 x2 as one).

"Purvi!"

Ananta is on the road on one knee with her hand up, and her voice cracks in the middle.

"Purvi, ruk na! Wapas a!"

"Nahin!"

Purvi does not stop.

"Tum wapas jao! Ghar jao!"

The animal turns towards her voice.


Vihaan gets to her. He goes down on one knee and has her by the arm, thumb steady on her forearm, jaw tight, breath fast, and her hand closes round his wrist with fear, so hard it will bruise tomorrow.

She looks at him. Blood on her knee, dust in her hair, face wet, mouth open on nothing.

"Woh uske peeche gayi."

She says it to his face.

"Woh uske peeche gayi, Vihaan."

"Ananta?"

Purvi says it, and her voice breaks.

"Ananta kahan hai?"

"Ghar jao." He gets her up.

"Andar jao, darwaza band karo, aur phone—" He hears himself and stops and takes a breath and starts again, low, because his voice is doing something he does not want it to do in front of her.

"Andar jao. Main dekhta hoon."

"Ananta—"

"Purvi."

His thumb is steady on her forearm.

"Ghar jao."

Then he runs into the dark.

The lane has no proper light past the second turn.

There is a plot with an empty plinth and a watchman's fire not yet lit, and beyond that the ground falls away to the road that will be the bypass in ten years, and there is scrub and broken brick and the smell of the drain.

Vihaan goes through all of it at a full run and finds: a broken tea stall, two frightened watchmen with a torch, a dog that barks at him properly and hides.

"Purvi Arora."

Vihaan says it to the watchmen.

"Ek ladki. Green sari. Dekhi tumne?"

The older watchman shakes his head.

"Nahin, sahab. Hum toh yahin hain."

"Koi aaya? Ladki daud rahi thi, ye lane se."

"Nahin, sahab. Koi nahin aaya."

Vihaan looks past them, into the dark.

"Thik hai."

Vihaan does not find her.

Vihaan comes back to the second turn and the street lamp is buzzing again and the road is lit and there is blood on the asphalt in a line of little separate drops, and it is Rohan's, from the boy being carried inside, not hers.

There is a piece of cloth on the road.

It is a green dupatta with a gold edge, torn at one end, and it is wet with something, and it is the only thing in that lane that came off her.

Vihaan stands in the middle of the road with it in his hands.

"Purvi," he says it to the cloth, and the word is quiet enough that only the dark hears it.

He smiles small and broken at it, the smile of a man who has been given the only thing left of someone he is too late to keep whole.


Vihaan is not shaking. His hands are quite steady and the cloth hangs between them and he is not shaking.

Nothing in twenty-four years has done this to him, and he has no name for it, and the name he would reach for is not a name for his own sake.

In the Suri house, at the gate, Ananta is standing in the light with her clutch under one arm and one shoe in her hand.

Ananta looks at him for a long time as he comes up the drive, and then she looks at the cloth in his hands, and she says nothing, and neither does he.

"Purvi?" Ananta says it at last, and her voice is small enough to be lost in the gate light.

Vihaan holds the dupatta in both hands, and he does not look at her.

"Kahan hai wo?"

Ananta takes a step towards him.

"Vihaan, kahan hai Purvi?"

Vihaan looks at her then. His eyes are flat.

"Wo wapas nahi aayegi, Ananta."

Behind them, the animal is somewhere in the dark beyond the plot, and it is not making any sound at all.

## The Jacket

Half past one, and the Suri house has one light on in it.

The generator is off. The lawn is dark and the chairs are still out on it, and the marigold along the veranda has begun to smell of the end of a party.

The back hall smells of wet shoes and of the blue disinfectant the sweeper uses at the end of a night.

Vihaan comes in through the service door with his jacket over his arm and his shirt wet through at the shoulder, and there is mud on both his shoes up to the laces.

The study door is open and the light in it is the one light.

Parmeet is at the desk with his glasses off and a glass in front of him that he is not drinking.

"Kahan tha?"

"Lane mein."

Vihaan does not stop walking.

"Ek ladke ko kutta kaat liya. Nandini ke bhai ko."

"Kutta?"

Vihaan stops at the foot of the stairs.

"Haan." He says it to the newel post.

"Kutta."

The clock in the study does its two notes and stops.

Parmeet comes to the doorway with his glass in his hand and stands in it, and he looks at his son from the shoes to the shoulder, and the look takes its time.

"Lane mein khoon tha?"

"Tha."

"Aur tum wahan kya kar rahe the?"

"Khaana de raha tha."

Vihaan turns round.

"Do thaliyan. Sukhriya kehne ke liye ruk gaya."

Parmeet's mouth does something at one corner of it that is not a smile.

"Jacket de de."

"Nahi."

The word is in the hall before either of them has decided about it.

Parmeet's glass comes down on the desk behind him without his looking away from his son.

"Kya kaha?"

"Maine kaha nahi."

Vihaan's voice is the voice he uses in the office.

"Jacket meri hai."

Parmeet laughs. It is one note and it does not go anywhere, and it is worse than a shout in that hall, with contempt.

"Bada aaya." He picks his glass back up. "Theek hai. Rakhiye apni jacket." He drinks.

"Karan ki shaadi ki date tay ho gayi hai. Pandrah tareekh. Tum wahan khade rahoge, apne bhai ke saath, aur tumhara chehra dekh kar log kahenge ki is ghar mein sab theek hai." He sets the glass down again.

"Aur us ladki ko ghar mein mat laana. Na naukri ke bahane, na rishtedaari ke bahane."

"Kaunsi ladki?"

"Tumhe pata hai kaunsi ladki."

Parmeet goes back to his chair.

"So jaao. Subah chhe baje gaadi ja rahi hai."

"Kaun hai wo, Papa? Tumhari ladki? Ya meri?"

Parmeet's glass stops on its way back to his lips. He puts it down.

"So jaao, Vihaan."

"Main jaanunga kaun hai wo."

"Tumhe koi matlab nahin hai usse."

"Mera matlab hai."

Vihaan's voice is flat.

"Is ghar mein bhi."

Vihaan goes up the stairs with the jacket over one shoulder.

At the third step he stops, and comes back down two of them, and says, into the study door, at a volume that is almost polite: "Wo ladki abhi bhi wahan hai. Us lane mein. Aur kisi ko uski khabar nahi hai."

"Toh?"

Parmeet does not turn round.

"Uske ghar waale hain na. Unko fikr karne do."

"Uska naam Purvi hai."

The glass stops halfway to the desk.

"Aap use 'wo ladki' kehte hain."

Vihaan's voice is flat and quiet and it does not go up at the end.

"Main use naam se bulaunga. Is ghar mein bhi."

Parmeet puts the glass down and looks at his son for a long moment, and then he laughs again, and this time the laugh has something else in it, and it is not pleasant.

"So jaao." He sits down. "Subah chhe baje gaadi ja rahi hai."

Vihaan goes up, and the light in the study stays on under the door, and the stairs take his weight one at a time and stop.

In his room he does not turn on the big light.

He puts his jacket on the bed and takes out of it a green dupatta with a gold edge, torn at one end and stiff with something that has dried in the wind on the way home, and he holds it for a moment at arm's length.

There are four more cartons of the same thing downstairs in the dining room, stacked against the wall where the sideboard used to be, and nobody in this house has asked him a single question about them.

It is warm.

Vihaan turns it over once and the sleeve of his shirt comes out of his cuff.

The cloth is warm in a cold room, and it stays warm, and the second time he turns it, it is still warm.

The lamp in the passage outside the door dims and comes back up.

Vihaan takes down from the shelf the fat house book with the brown cover and the gold on it and opens it in the middle, and folds the cloth once, and lays it in the fold of the pages, and shuts the book on it.

His hand stays flat on the cover after the book is shut.

The score comes up under it.

One string, and out.

Vihaan puts the book back between the two others.

Vihaan puts his shoes by the door and his shirt over the chair and he sits down on the edge of the bed with his elbows on his knees and does not move.

Then he gets up, and puts the shoes back on, and takes the torch out of the drawer, and goes down the back stairs, and out through the service door, and pulls it to behind him.
