## Teen Baje, Teen Manzil

Three in the morning, and every light in the Suri house is on, and the house has the look a house has when nobody in it has been to bed.

The tube in the porch buzzes.

The gate stands open on the drive, and the gravel has been walked over from the road to the steps and back again.

Behind the house the generator runs at its one note, and in the hall the marigold under the chairs has begun to brown at the edges.

The hall has been washed twice.

The chairs still stand in their two rows with the marigold under them, and the carpet at the third step has a dark place in the pile that has dried brown, and Vihaan is on his knees beside it with the camera in both hands, jaw tight, hands steady on the camera, voice flat.


The flash goes up the stairwell and puts the banister on the wall for a moment and takes it away.

"Shyam." Vihaan does not look up from the small screen at the back of the camera.

"Tum yahan the."

"Ji, sahab."

Shyam stands at the end of the passage with his cap in his hands and his eyes on the floor.

"Aaj dopahar, teen baje." Vihaan turns the camera over and looks at the step again, smiling small and broken at nothing.

"Gate pe kaun aaya tha?"

"Koi nahin, sahab."

"Koi nahin." Vihaan stands up.

"Aur bhabhi? Aur Purvi?"

"Bhai sahab ne kaha tha—"

"Main bhai sahab se poochh raha hoon ya tum se?"

Shyam's cap goes round in his hands once.

It is blue-black wool with a badge on the front, and the badge has been polished so often that the lion on it has no face left.

"Kitaab lao." Vihaan puts the camera down on the step and holds out his hand.

"Aaj ka panna."

Shyam goes down the passage and comes back with the cloth-spined register and puts it in Vihaan's hand and does not let go of it at once.

The page for the day has four cars on it, all four crossed out.

The white police car is the third line.

Under the fourth line the page is empty to the foot of it, and empty on the page after it, and empty on the page after that.

"Poora din khaali hai." Vihaan turns the book round in his hand, thumb on the page edge.

"Chaar gaadiyan, aur poora din khaali."

"Hukum tha, sahab."

"Kiska?"

Shyam does not say it.

Vihaan closes the book on the step and goes up the stairs past the photographs, and the passage light stays on behind him.

The photographs go up the wall in their frames, three sizes of them, and the glass on one near the top has been cracked across and left cracked.

At the top of the stairs the door of the room at the back is open, and a man is sitting on the edge of the bed in the dark with his shoes on, and his shirt is half out of his trousers, and there is a smell of the cloth that someone held over his mouth and nose.

"Aryaman."

Aryaman lifts his head.

His eyes go to the camera in Vihaan's hand and stay there.

"Kya hua?"

His voice is not right yet.

"Main— mujhe kuch yaad nahin hai. Main utha aur—"

"Bhai." Vihaan sits down on the chair by the door, and does not come closer.

"Aaj raat, gyarah baje ke baad, tumne kisi ko dekha?"

"Kisi ko?"

Aryaman looks at him.

"Tum kya keh rahe ho?"

"Purvi kahan hai?"

The clock in the passage goes on ticking.

Aryaman's hands come up to his own face and stay there.

"Woh chali gayi."

Aryaman's voice comes out through his fingers.

"Papa ne kaha— woh dono chali gayin. Bhabhi aur—"

He stops there.

"Purvi ka phone do baje se band hai." Vihaan says it plainly. "Gate ki kitaab mein aaj chaar gaadiyan hain, aur chaar ke baad panna khaali hai. Agle teen pannu bhi. Ghar mein chaudah log hain, aur tumhare gate se aaj kuch nikalta kisi ne nahin dekha."

Aryaman's hands come down off his face.

"Bhabhi kahan hai?"

Vihaan does not answer that.

He sits with the register on his knee and lets the question stand in the room, and it is Aryaman who looks away first.

"Kal subah aath baje inspector mere desk pe hoga." Vihaan stands up.

"Tum mere saath chaloge. Jo yaad hai, jo yaad nahin hai— dono batana. Main tumhara naam kisi kagaz pe nahin likh raha. Abhi nahin. Aur ek baat—"

He stops in the door.

"Parmeet se akela baat mat karna. Jab baat karni ho, mere saamne karna."

He goes out and pulls the door to behind him, and on the landing he stops and holds the sill and puts his head down for a moment, and then takes out the phone and looks at it, and does not make the call here.

## Teen Baje, Teesra Kamra

Three in the morning, and the third room on the fourth floor has never been anybody's: a table under a cloth, two chairs, a tube that flickers twice before it holds, and a window with the paint gone at the bottom of the frame where somebody has had it open in every weather for twenty years.

The house is loud below him — the hall being washed, a chair moving, a woman's voice in the kitchen saying the same sentence twice — and this room is above the noise and has none of it in it.

Vihaan puts his phone on the table with the register beside it, face up, and a steel glass of water at the corner, and sits down and opens the register at the day before.

He reads the last two pages of it twice.

Then he takes the pen out from behind his ear and turns the book round and writes, in the box where the day is written:

**18.**

The date is the first thing on the page and he writes it in the smallest hand in that house, because a page in that register is not a record of what happened.

It is a record of what the day has spent, and the day is not over.

He goes down the column — the tent, the chairs, the boys, the flowers, the light — and then the two accounts that are not the wedding's: the keeper's feed for eleven men, and the printing of two thousand cards.

He writes them in the same column as the flowers, because they came out of the same month and the same pocket, and the pocket is his to keep.

The tube flickers.

He waits it out, and then goes on.

He puts the pen down and sits back, and the room does what it does, which is nothing.

At four he picks up the phone and puts it face down on the register and sits with his hand on it.

Then he turns it over.

"Verma. Teen baje ka phone hai. Sun." His voice is level and it is the voice he uses at a desk.

"Missing person. Purvi Arora, teis saal, channel mein kaam karti hai. Aaj dopahar se ghar pe nahin hai. Inspector ko bol do ki file subah aath baje mere desk pe ho. Aur ek baat—"

He looks at the door of the room.

The door of the room is shut.

"Us file mein ghar ka naam nahin aayega. Sirf mera naam."

"Aur ek baat, Verma."

He stops him before the line goes.

"Kal subah jo file aaye, uske pehle page pe likhna — yeh Vihaan Suri ki taraf se hai. Ghar ki taraf se nahin."

He hears his own name said back to him and puts the phone down.

He writes the time against it.

Then he takes a fresh page and writes at the top of it, in the same small hand: **18. Missing person. P.A.**

He underlines the date once, folds the page twice, and puts it in the inside pocket of his jacket where the day's papers live, and closes the register on the wedding and its account.

In the drawer under the register there is a second book, and it does not match the first.

It is smaller, and its cover has no printing on it at all, and its first page begins — not with a date, not with a heading — with a name written once at the top and a hundred pages of nothing after it.

He does not open it.

He puts his hand flat on the cover, and then he puts the register on top of it and shuts the drawer.

Downstairs somebody is still washing a floor at four in the morning, and the water goes out of the house through the drain at the side of the drive, and the man in the room at the top has a page in his pocket with a name on it that the house has decided not to say.

## Mitti

Six in the morning, and the grey light that comes before the sun is properly up. The sheds stand in a row with their shutters down, and the last one has its shutter half up with a truck inside it. Behind the sheds the ground is broken open, and the tyre tracks go round and round the same circle in it, and at the end of the tracks there is a pit with the earth going back into it on top of a sheet.

Two crows sit on the wire over the yard and have not moved since the men came.

"Oye iska haath hila yaar," the score sounding under the hand (m00:09).

The tall man in the black shirt is up out of the pit and back a step.

"Oye!"

"Abey paagal hai kya tu?"

The second man has the shovel in both hands.

"Mar gayi hai woh — aisa hota hai kya?"

The shovel goes in and comes out and the earth lands and slides down the side.

Behind the sheds a pump is running, and the sound of it goes on under everything they say.

"Bani thi woh har baar ko sehne ke liye."

Nobody in the pit says this.

It comes down over the sheds and the gravel and the grey sky, and it is a woman's voice and it is old and it is not for them.

"Kahan hai woh saari shaktiyan, aapka vardaan, aapki kripa jo aapne hum naaginon ko di thi? Phir unki jaan kyun chali gayi? Yeh baat woh achhe se jaanti thi — amrit ko paane ke liye vish peena padta hai."

The two men in the pit look up at the sky and then at each other.

"Tu ne zyada chadha li hai kya, abey?"

The second man puts the shovel down.

"Isne aankh kholi hai, maa kasam."

The sheet is the colour of the inside of a hospital, and where it has been dragged it has taken the red dust of that ground and kept it.

The sheet has come away from a face at the end of the pit and the eyes in it are open.

"Chal mitti daal," the score sounding twice over the earth (m00:48 x2 as one), the tall man's voice gone thin with fear.

"Daal, daal, daal—"

"Ruk."

The second man goes down on one knee at the edge and looks, and then he is very still.

"Abey iski aankhon mein—"

"Kya?"

"Chehre," the score sounding three times over the faces (m01:07 x3 as one).

He says it without lifting his head.

"Iske dono aankhon mein chehre hain. Ek, do, teen— maine tera gina. Saat, aath—"

"Kisi naagin ki aankhon mein—"

"Naagin ki aankhon mein sirf."

The second man's hand comes up.

"Yeh sabko pata hai! Yeh sabko pata hai" His hand stays up.

"aur jo bhi un par hamla karta hai koi" He looks at the tall man.

"uski tasveer naagin ki aankhon mein chhap jaati hai, na."

"Ye naagin thodi na hai, abey."

The tall man kicks the earth back in with the side of his boot.

"Bhabhi thi. Humare saath chaar din roti khaayi hai."

The second man goes down on his other knee in the dirt at the edge, and the dirt goes into his trousers, and he counts under his breath, and the count goes past ten and past eleven and stops at thirteen, and then he is on his feet and walking backwards from the pit.

"Terah."

His mouth is dry.

"Terah chehre. Tera chehra. Mera chehra. Sabke chehre."

"Mitti daal."

The tall man has the shovel up.

"Zameen ke neeche se—"

"MITTI DAAL."

The earth goes in over the sheet.

It goes in fast and it goes in wrong, and one corner of the sheet stays out of it, and the second man puts his boot on the corner and pushes it down.

Behind them the last shed has its shutter up and a truck standing in it with the back doors open, and the truck is not going anywhere.

Its bonnet is dull with the dust of that ground, and on the shelf under its windscreen there is a garland gone brown and a paper god with one corner curled.

The pit's edge has the marks of two boots on it, and the shovel stands in the earth where it has been left, and the wire over the yard is empty now.

"Mahadev, aapka vardaan vyarth nahin ja sakta."

The old voice is not finished.

"Aapko unhein lautana hi hoga — humare liye, is desh ke liye, is desh ko bachane ke liye. Aapko humari Maha Naagrani ko wapas laana hi hoga. Yeh anarth nahin ho sakta."

The invocation goes up over the sheds at six in the morning, thin and sure of itself, and the two men stand beside a filled-in pit with the shovels in their hands and do not say one word to each other.

"Jagiye Mahadev," the score sounding under the invocation's tail (m03:17).

The voice is not in a hurry.

"Jagiye."

## Aadha Ghanta

Seven in the morning, and the sun is over the sheds now, and the yard behind them is full of it, and the dust the forty men have walked up stands in it in a cloud to their knees.

The truck is in the middle with its tarpaulin off and its back doors open, and three more stand against the wall, and the men are round the first one the way men stand round a fire.

"Wahan mere aadmi pehle pahunchenge," the score sounding twice over the half hour (m06:07 x2 as one).

The man with the phone is walking along the line of them.

"Bas aadha ghanta aur ye poori jagah dhamake se ud jayegi — wahan bomb aur visphot ka plan ban raha hai hamara."

"Har har har Mahadev."

One of the forty says it, and two of them laugh, and the laugh goes down the line and stops.

By the truck's back wheel there is a coil of wire, a roll of tape, and a pair of rubber gloves that have been pulled off and left where they fell.

The phone rings in the walking man's hand and he takes it and his face changes.

"Kya matlab hai gaadi paani mein?"

He stops walking.

"Hello? RDX laga diye, aur phir—"

He listens, and the forty watch him listen.

"Nashay mein tha?"

His voice has gone quiet.

He puts the phone down against his leg and stands there.

## Naam Kya Hai Tera

Eight in the morning, and the Kumbh ground with the ghat below it and the tents going away in their rows, and the mist still on the water and the smoke of a hundred cooking fires standing up straight in it.

Down by the river the last of the forty stand round a fire with a bottle going round, and one of them is telling the others about last night the way a man tells a story he has not been in.

"Arre kya sahi ladki hai," the score sounding under the measuring (m06:55).

He has his hands out, measuring a woman nobody in that circle has seen.

"Utha le kya isko? Yeh ladki chalegi kya?"

The girl comes out of the mist, the score sounding twice over her coming (m07:24+m07:31 as one), and stands at the edge of the firelight with her arms at her sides.

"Paani."

She says it once, and gets no answer, and does not ask again.

The bottle stops going round.

They look at her the way men look at a thing that has walked out of the river.

Her kurta is dark down the back with what is on it and her hair is down and there is river weed in it, and her spectacles are not on her face and not in the mud behind her, because she is not the girl those men put in the ground.

"Naam kya hai tera?"

"Ananta."

"Ananta."

The man by the fire looks at his friends and grins.

"Ananta."

"Kaun hai tu?"

"Naagin hoon."

"Ladkiyan kya bakwaas kar rahi hai?"

He laughs, the score sounding under the laugh (m07:44), and the laugh is honest, and it is the worst thing on that ground.

"Naagin. Arre, aaj kal sab naagin hi hain. Sun—"

"Anant kul ki Naagrani," the score sounding under the declaration (m08:35).

She says it to all of them, and her voice is a girl's voice in the first word and is not a girl's voice by the last.

"Ichchhadhari naagin. Shiv vardani. Naaglok ki rani."

The fire does not move, the score sounding under the stillness (m08:02).

There is no wind on that ground at all.

"Na nar na naari."

She takes one step in.

"Aur na hi bhagwan — na shaitan na farishta. Na insaan na jaanwar."

"Bhai—"

The man behind the fire is on his feet.

"Bhai, yeh—"

"Magar bhagwan ki sabse badi sashakti."

Her eyes come up, and they are not doing what eyes do.

"Vishnu ki bhakti. Shiv ki vardan."

The first man puts his bottle down very carefully, the way a man puts down a thing he does not want to break.

"Aaj ki raat tumhara desh nahin khatam hota."

Her mouth is open and the teeth in it are not a girl's teeth.

"Aaj ki raat tum jaise gaddaron ki aakhri raat hai."

The man with the gun is the one who had the bottle.

He gets it out of his waistband and fires, twice, and the sound goes across the water and comes back off the far bank.

The bullets stop in the air a hand's width from her and fall on the stones like coins.

He fires again.

The same.

Then the gun is empty and clicking and he is still pulling at it with terror.

She kills them one by one.

It is quick, and it is quiet, and the page does not count the men round that fire.

By the end there is one of them running along the water with his own breath loud in his ears, and she is in front of him without having crossed the ground, and then there is nobody running either.

On the stones there is a bottle with nothing spilled out of it, and a gun with its barrel still warm, and a shoe come off and lying on its side in the ash.

The mist goes on over the water.

A boat knocks against a pontoon somewhere and nobody on that ground corrects it.

## Gyarah Aadmi

Half past nine, and the light is up over the ground and the mist has gone off the water, and the ash of the fire has been walked through by a hundred people who have come down since eight.

Two men of the crew come down from the tents with a torch and a tarp and a sheet of brown paper, and they stand at the edge of what is on the stones and they do not go any further.

"Gin le."

The older man's torch does not move.

"Ek, do, teen—"

The torch beam goes along the stones and stops and comes back, and where it stops there is a dark place on the ghat step that the river has already touched and left a line round.

He counts out loud to the end and then he counts again, and the second count comes out the same as the first and he does not like it either, and the thing he does not say is that this crew came down eleven strong.

"Gyarah."

"Gyarah aadmi."

The younger one has the tarp across his arms and has not unfolded it.

"Sir ko kya bolein?"

"Sir ko woh bolein jo hai."

The older man crouches and puts the paper down and does not look at the faces while he does it.

"Aur ek baat— goliyan chali hain. Gun tani hui hai, aur barood gayi hai, aur goli kahin nahin lagi."

"Toh lagi kis ko?"

"Yeh nahin likhna."

He stands up.

"Yeh kabhi nahin likhna."

The tarp goes down over what is on the stones, and the brown paper sits on the tarp with a stone on its corner to hold it.

A cow comes down the ghat steps to the water and stops, and one of the men throws a stone at it and the cow goes up again without hurrying.

The two men go back up the ground to find the man with the phone.

He is at the truck with a page in his hand and his finger on the top line of it, and he is not listening to them.

"Kya hua?"

"Sir, ghat pe—"

"Baad mein."

He folds the page and puts it in his inside pocket.

"Tel laga hua hai. Gaadi ke andar. Aur mera aadmi phone nahin utha raha."

## Mahayudh

Half past twelve, and the ghat is stone from the water to the last step. The fire orange light is on the water in one long road that shakes.

The diyas stand in rows on the steps.

Their flames go straight up in the still air.

The boats are tied in a line.

Their ropes go down into the water.

The crowd is a wall from one end of the ghat to the other.

There is nowhere to go.

At the edge of the crowd, by the generator van, Vihaan stands with his earpiece in.

His camera bag is on his shoulder.

The cameraman beside him has the camera up.

The red light is on.

The diya on the third step has oil to the brim.

The wick is black at the tip.

The diyas gutter all at once.

Then they stand straight again.

The wind comes down the water.

It moves the flames sideways.

A dog barks once from the lane behind the ghat.

It does not bark again.

"Camera on rakho." Vihaan does not look at the cameraman.

"Jo bhi ho, camera on rakho."

"Sir, yeh—"

The cameraman's hands are shaking on the camera.

"Sir, aag—"

"Camera on rakho."

The shadow comes over the ghat before the sound does.

The shadow is too big for any cloud.

The boats pull at their ropes.

The crowd goes down on its knees in one movement.

The children are pulled in under the arms of the women.

The rope on the second boat is frayed where it rubs the stone.

The dragon comes over the water low.

Its wings go down and up.

Its mouth is open.

The fire comes out of its mouth in a stream.

It hits the stone and runs.

The diyas go out where the fire touches them.

The smoke goes up in a black column.

The wind takes it sideways.

"Bhaago!"

A man shouts it.

Another man shouts it.

A woman is screaming a name over and over.

Her hand is over her own mouth.

The cameraman turns to run.

Vihaan takes the camera out of his hands.

He puts his own hands on it and holds it up.

The red light stays on.

"Idhar." Vihaan says it to the cameraman.

His voice is down and not up.

"Tu idhar khada rahega. Camera mere paas rahega."

The woman comes forward through the crowd.

The crowd opens for her.

Nobody decides to open it.

Her long dark hair is moving in the wind off the water.

The gold maang tikka with the red stone is on her forehead.

The nose ring with its chain is catching the fire light.

The gold dangling earrings are swinging.

The gold fringed outfit is dark with water at the hem.

The gold scaled skirt is shining where the fire hits it.

Behind the ghat the temple stands with its door open on its own dark.

A voice goes up inside it.

It is a woman's and it is old and it is not for the ghat.

"Jagiye Mahadev."

The diyas on the steps lean.

All of them, one way, towards the water.

At the top of the steps there is a trishul standing in the stone.

Its three points are black against the sky.

Nobody has put it there.

The light comes up out of the temple's dark and goes into it and stays in it, and the stone under it goes pale.

The crowd does not turn round.

The crowd is looking at the water.

The woman climbs the steps.
