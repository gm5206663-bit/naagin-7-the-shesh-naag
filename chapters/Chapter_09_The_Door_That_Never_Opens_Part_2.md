# Chapter 9 — The Door That Never Opens (Part 2)

*Episode reference: Naagin, season 7, Episode 3 (third part, and the end of it).*

*Draft; not yet accepted.*

### TITLES

*The twelve seconds the season plays on every night after its first: the theme, then straight in (`canon/TITLES_SEQUENCE.md` §I). The full forty seconds are written once, at the head of Chapter 1, and are not repeated below it.*

The logotype comes up over thundercloud, with something red falling through the frame too slowly to be rain.

A man's voice asks two words in the pitch of a been: *"Kaun hai woh."* A choir of women answers underneath him and leaves the answer unfinished.

The been stops. One low string is struck and left hanging over the black.

A drum, once. Mahadev's trishul is not shown, but the thunder is his. Then the story, and no music under it until the story asks.

## Water

The hall with the chandelier down and the lamps burning on the pillars, and the score up, and the engagement a thing that has happened and is now being celebrated by three hundred people who have not stopped talking.

Purvi stands at the edge of the floor with her card in her hand and her bag strap wound twice round her wrist.

A man goes past her with a glass in his own hand and his jacket open and his tie gone, and without her spectacles he is a shape and a white sleeve, and she catches the sleeve.

"Waiter bhaiya, zara paani dena please."

He does not move. He shakes his head.

"Mundi kya hila rahe ho?" She lets go. "De do na. Paani toh maanga hai. Itna kya ho gaya hai mere chashma ko?"

Aryaman holds his own glass out to her.

"Aa, thank you." She takes it and drinks half of it standing up.

"Saath mein thoda zeher bhi doon?" He lifts his second glass.

She stops, and her hand comes up, and she looks at him.

"Sir— sorry. Woh, woh aap mujhe dikhe kaise?"

"Sorry sir. Mera matlab—" She takes her spectacles out of her bag and holds them up, and looks at them, and looks back at him. "Sir, aap mujhe dikh rahe ho. Poore ke poore. Bina chashme ke. Aapki aankhen theek theek ho gayi hai."

She puts the spectacles back in her bag because her hands have nothing else to do with them.

"Main theek hoon. Main bilkul theek hoon."

Across the floor, at one of the pillars with a tray of glasses going past them, the two women stand with their heads together and their lips moving, and only the nearest of the three hundred would hear it.

"Dekha, yaani ab woh naagin ban rahi hai. Ye toh shuruaat hai, kyunki humne unki kechuli ko sinhaasan par virajit kar diya hai. Toh jab tak woh apne poorn swaroop mein nahin aa jaati, shaktiyan aati jaati rahegi."

The score comes up under the hall and takes the words away.

"Sir—" Purvi puts the water glass on the tray of a boy going past. "Sorry. Matlab main— nashe mein nahin hoon, sir."

Aryaman looks at his own glass.

"Papa ne kal ek tonic diya tha."

"Phir toh zaroor woh Papa ka shot raha hoga."

"Nahin sir, sorry." She is walking beside him. "Main sachchi mein nashe mein nahin hoon. By God. Agar main nashe mein hoti, toh kya main aapko yeh baat batane aati, ki mujhe aapse kitni urgent baat karni hai?" She gets in front of him. "Aap baat chhodo. Main aapko dikhati hoon sir. Ye dekho."

The phone comes out. The screen comes up. The photograph is there: a body, and the mark on it, and the light of the hall on the glass of the phone.

Aryaman looks at it. He looks for two seconds.

"Kya dikhana hai?"

"Sir, yeh—" She holds it closer. "Yeh nishaan. Ye—"

"Done." He gives the phone back. "Pakki nashedi ho tum." And then he sees her face, and he does something with his own that is not quite a smile and is not quite anything. "Par actually ye buri baat nahin hai. Achhi baat hai. Tumhare liye bhi, aur tumhare aas-paas ke logon ke liye bhi."

"Sir, main sach mein nashe nahin karti hoon." Her hands go out. "Bahut sorry."

There is a crash at her feet. The tray, the bottle, the glass, and the dark going out along the marble between three hundred pairs of shoes.

At the edge of the crowd a man in a dark shirt has come out three steps and stopped, and a woman's hand comes out of the crowd behind him and closes on his elbow and holds it, and the score does not stop at all.

At the table by the door Pinky looks up at him and her pen stops on the list.

"Aapki bottle toot gayi." She goes down on her knees with her dupatta in her hands. "Sorry, sir. Sorry. Sorry."

The shoes move away from the sound on both sides. Nobody helps. A boy runs with a cloth and goes down on his knees beside her and starts on the marble, and the score does not stop at all, and the photographer's flash goes off at the front of the hall for the couple still standing under the mandap in the same place, in the same pose, with their garlands straight.

"Sorry." Purvi is picking glass out of the wet with her fingers and putting it in her own dupatta. "Sorry. Sorry, sir."

Aryaman stands over her with his glass and watches her do it, and does not put his hand out.

"Utho." He says it quietly. "Yeh unka kaam hai."

At the edge of the crowd Vihaan takes Pinky's hand off his elbow and puts it back in her own hand.

"Ruk." He says it to her without turning. "Main aaya."

He goes across the marble through three hundred people, and the floor gives him to her the way a floor gives way to a man who is not going to stop, and he comes up beside his brother and looks down at the girl on her knees in the broken glass.

"Aryaman." He says it quietly. "Yeh ghar ka kaam hai. Tum andar chalo."

Then he goes down on one knee himself — not on both, and not in front of the room, and it is not a courtesy, it is a man getting a thing off the floor — and he puts one hand under her elbow and the other flat on the wet marble beside her hand.

"Haath do." Vihaan looks at her hand in the wet. "Glass hai. Utho."

"Sir, main—"

"Haath do, Purvi." Vihaan holds her wrist a moment longer than the lift needs, and notices that he does.

Her hand comes up and closes round his wrist, and he lifts her off the marble and puts her on her feet, and her dupatta comes up with the glass in it and he takes the corners of it out of her fingers and gives it to the boy with the cloth.

Neither of them says anything about it. The score comes back up at the far end of the hall and goes on.

Purvi stands there with her wrist in his hand and her breath going, and something goes up her arm from where he is holding it — warmth, and then heat, and then a turn in her stomach that has nothing to do with the glass — and she looks up at him and her mouth opens and nothing comes out.

"Bahut garmi hai andar." She says it at last, and it is not what she means, and she knows it is not what she means.

"Haan." Vihaan lets go of her wrist and stands back. "Garmi hai."

Vihaan turns to Pinky at the table by the door. "Iski chot dekho. Aur glass ka hisaab subah mere naam pe."

Then he stands up out of the glass, and he does not take the cloth out of the boy's hands, and he looks at his brother across the hall.

"Glass saf karo. Aur isse aaj koi cheez uthane ke liye nahin kaha jayega. Kisi ke liye bhi." His voice carries to the front of the hall and no further and every head in the middle of it turns.

Aryaman's glass comes down and his arm goes behind his back.

Vihaan is already walking out, and the photographer's flash goes off at the couple under the mandap, and the score does not stop at all.

## Who Wants to Live Longer

The side of the hall afterwards, where the music is quieter and the kitchen door gives out its heat, and the two of them stand at the wall with the crowd behind them going on with its evening.

"Waise aapko bhi itni nahin peeni chahiye, sir." Purvi says it to the floor.

"Kyun?" Aryaman has one shoulder against the wall and his glass in his hand. "Kyun nahin peeni chahiye? Zyada jeene ke liye?" He lifts the glass to the room. "Kaun idiot zyada jeena chahta hai?"

"Main sir, main nahin marna chahti jaldi." She says it without looking up.

A tray of used glasses passes at the height of her elbow and goes on past.

Across the hall, at the side of the bar, Vihaan stands with a glass he is not drinking and his shoulder against the pillar. He does not come over. His jaw is tight, and his hand is light on the glass, and his ears are hot. The two of them look at each other for the length of one sentence and then not at all. He does not know why his chest feels full all at once, and he does not look away first.

Aryaman turns his head and looks at her for a while.

"Mera toh clear hai." He drinks. "Healthy khao piyo, khoob jiyo. Bachche toh chhodo, parvaton ko bhi marte hue dekho. Nahin." He waits, and she does not answer. "Humm. Vriddhashram mein pade-pade hain. Hai na? Kyun? Yahi karte hain na bachche? Jab maa-baap buddhe ho jaate hain, toh unhein old age home mein daal dete hain."

A boy goes past with a crate of bottles on his shoulder and says something to the man behind him and laughs.

"Aur kya kya dekhna hai budhape mein?" Aryaman's voice does not go up. "Akelapan, beemariyan, chhurriyan, doston ki arthiyan, Ganga mein asthiyan." He turns the glass round. "Ye jo zindagi hai na, ye jawani mein ji jaati hai. Budhape mein bas saansein chalti hai. Insaan nahin."

"Sir—"

"Tum meri baat maano." He is not looking at her. "Jo bhi nasha kar rahi ho, karti raho. Chhodo mat. Tabhi is duniya ko jald se jald chhod paogi."

The hall goes on behind them. Somebody's child goes past under the tables.

"Duniya toh maine kab ki chhod di, sir." Purvi lifts her head. "Ye toh meri aatma bhatak rahi hai. Jo aapke liye duty kar rahi hai."

Aryaman looks at her for a long moment, and drinks.

Purvi goes, and the kitchen door takes her, and Aryaman stands at the wall with his glass.

At the top of the hall, on the landing where the stairs turn, Parmeet has come out of the passage with two men and stands with his hand on the rail. He looks down at the room, and at the girl going in through the kitchen door with her dupatta over her arm, and he lifts one finger and says something to the man on his left.

The man goes down the stairs and out through the crowd towards the gate.

## The Song

The hall with the chandelier off and only the work lights on, and the hall being taken apart.

The chairs go down in stacks. The marigold is swept off the marble into a heap by the door, and the heap still smells of the morning it comes from. Somebody has turned the band's speakers off but nobody has moved them, and the biggest one stands in the middle of the floor on its stand, dead, with a garland over one corner of it.

The love theme comes up for the first time in this story.

It is one instrument at first — a single string, low, and under it the drone the titles use, which does not resolve. It is not a song yet. It is the thing a song is going to be.

She carries the tablecloths out to the store room in armfuls. The heavy crate, the one with the brass in it, went out of the hall a while ago on the caterers' trolley, and nobody in this house has asked Vihaan to move a thing tonight, and he is out on the marble with his hands empty for a reason the household would not believe. They pass each other four times on the same bit of marble. On the third pass she says, "Raat bahut ho chuki hai," and he answers, "Abhi nahin jaana," and does not slow down. And on the fourth they do not speak, because there is nothing that needs saying, and because in this house the two of them speaking in an empty hall is the most dangerous thing either of them could do tonight.

On the third pass the top cloth slips off her armful, and he puts his hand flat on it to keep it where it is and lets go, and she does not slow down for it, and neither of them says a word.

Somewhere behind the hall a door goes and the work lights at the far end are switched off one row at a time.

The rain comes on the lawn at twenty past one. It comes all at once and it makes a noise on the tent.

She is out under it with her arms full, going the wrong way, towards the out-house. He comes off the verandah with a sheet of plastic the size of a door and holds it over her head and walks her in under it.

"Aap theek hain?" she says.

"Theek hain." Vihaan holds the plastic steady over both of them. "Aap theek hain?"

"Theek hain."

Vihaan smiles small at her under the plastic, the smile of a man who has done something he does not need to explain. His jaw is loose, and his breath is slow, and his hand is steady on the plastic. He does not move the sheet off himself to fix it. He does not know why his chest feels full, and he does not know why he stays under it with her, and he does not know why the rain sounds different all at once.

The plastic drums on itself with the weight of the water, and neither of them says anything about the rain.

Her spectacles go. She takes them off with her wrist and holds them in her fist and her hair is flat to her head, and he is wet through one shoulder and does not move the sheet off himself to fix it.

They come into the store room. One bulb on a wire, and shelves to the roof.

The shelf she needs is above her head. She puts a stool under it. He puts his hand flat on the stool.

She climbs, and takes hold of his shoulder to do it, and the love theme finds her hand and the vocal comes in — a woman's voice, one line, in Hindi, and the words are not for the audience — and it goes out again the moment both her feet are back on the floor.

There is a plate on the shelf with two of the cook's cutlets on it, gone cold, meant for whoever is still working. They eat it standing up, one each, and he takes the smaller one.

"Accha hai." She wipes her fingers on her dupatta. "Aapka haath theek hai."

"Nahin." He takes the plate from her. "Mera haath theek nahin hai. Bas aaj theek hai."

The house is asleep. She is asleep sitting up in the verandah chair with her back against the pillar and her chair tipped against the wall. Her mouth is open and her spectacles are folded in her hand.

He stands there with a cup of tea gone cold in his hand and looks at her for four seconds longer than a man looks at a colleague, and then she moves, and he is looking at the lawn.

The rain has stopped. The garden lamp is doing what it does.

Then the hall takes her — a voice from the main door, her name, two boxes still to go — and she is up and gone, and he is left in the verandah with the cup, and the love theme goes out on the single string it came in on, and does not resolve.

## The Owner's Lift

Under the hall, and under the channel, and under the street between them, under the same bare bulb, the same warm air, the same passage, the same iron with the long discolouration in it.

At the bottom of the service stairs the lift door stands open, and the light inside is orange, and there is nobody to press anything.

The doors close on nobody. The floor of the cabin knocks once, and the light holds, and the lift goes down. It does not go down the way a lift goes down. It goes down the way a bucket goes down a well, and it goes a long way.

The passage empties of its light. The warm air stays.

Then the building does what a building should not do: the lights on the tenth floor go out in one row, and come on, and go out in the next, and the hum in the walls goes up until the glass answers in its frame.

The sound that comes up the shaft does not stop where a scream stops.

## The Service Contract

The channel's accounts office at half past one in the morning, one desk lamp on, and the corridor outside it dark.

Vihaan sits with the building's service file open and a printout beside the keyboard that he has been through twice.

"Verma, sun," he says with the phone against his shoulder. "Is building ka lift contract kiska hai? Meridian Elevators? Nahin. Woh doosri building ka hai." He turns a page. "Toh yeh batao ki chaubees saal se is building mein ek hi company service kar rahi hai, aur woh company kis ke naam pe registered hai."

He listens, and underlines something, twice.

"Nahin. Main isliye nahin poochh raha ki paisa zyada ja raha hai." He turns the page back. "Main isliye poochh raha hoon ki contract mein chaar stop likhe hain. Chaar. Ground, one, do, teen. Aur jo bill aaya hai usmein service charge paanch stop ka hai. Paanchwan kis floor pe hai, Verma? Building mein paanchwan floor hai nahin. Plan mein nahin hai. Building ki naksha mein yeh building chaar manzil ki hai."

He listens for a long time, and the fan in the accounts room turns.

"Toh kal subah nau baje tum unka engineer mere paas bhejna. Aur usse pehle mujhe chaubees saal ki poori service history email pe bhejna. Har visit, har signature, har naam." Vihaan shuts the file. "Aur Verma — kisi ko mat batana ki maine yeh file maangi hai. Is ghar mein bhi nahin."

He puts the phone down and sits for a moment in the light of the one lamp.

Then he opens the file again and writes, on the inside of the cover, in ink: **chaar stop. paanch ka bill. poochho kyun.**

### Continuity and source note

This chapter is the last part of Episode 3, in the episode's own order: the corridor and the lift that never opens; the night of the engagement at the Suri house and the two women whose feet she touches; the question in the big house; the water and the tonic; the exchange about living longer; and the close, which the caption track does not carry. **The readings this half declares**

1. **Who she asks for water.** The track carries *waiter bhaiya, zara paani dena please* with no speaker attached to the answer, and the recap says she mistakes **Aryaman** for a servant and his is the sarcastic answer about the poison (`TellyReviews` L36). There is no separate waiter boy: the man who does not move and shakes his head is Aryaman, the *Aa, thank you* is hers to him, and the poison line is his. The draft's boy, and the draft's *Water.*, are struck.
2. **The photograph.** Canon has her show Aryaman something — *ye dekho* / *kya dikhana hai?* / *done, pakki nashedi ho tum* — and the track never says what he sees. The page gives him two seconds and a question, and does not tell the reader whether the mark is on the screen or not. That silence is the beat: the first person she chooses to tell cannot see it.
3. **Four repairs the track forces.** *Main preeti jeeti nahin hoon* is read *main nashe mein nahin hoon* — the syllables are the track's garble for the phrase the scene says twice more, and the ledger reads it so. *Shor shor* is read *zaroor*. *Kechuri* is read *kechuli*, the word the track says everywhere else in this episode. *Tanga mein asthiyan* is read *Ganga mein asthiyan* — ashes in the Ganga, the catalogue's own ending; the ledger's *tin* is not followed. All four are declared here and nowhere else.
4. **One tail struck.** The 38:53 block ends *jo aapke liye duty kar rahi hai. jars sir* — two syllables that are no Hindi word and no English one the scene can carry, in no ledger and no recap. It is struck, and the scene ends on her line.
5. **Parmeet on the landing.** Authored, and nowhere near a canon line: he stands above a wedding he is paying for and sends a man out to the gate. **He does not know who the young man at the pillar is** (`O032`: his name is Anantveer and the ruling's own words are *Parmeet does not know*), and nothing on this page says otherwise — the landing is the house being a house, and the man goes out on the house's ordinary business.

**Where the episode is not certain, and this half does not invent**

The hall being taken apart, no plot in it and nobody saying anything that matters. **The season's love theme** comes up in it — one string and the titles' drone, and the vocal arriving for exactly one line when her hand finds his shoulder on the stool and going out again when both her feet are on the floor. The theme is named `Woh Do Log` at §9.3, and it is *named* in Chapter 11; that naming is the whole of Chapter 11's share, and this page is where the book first plays it. The four seconds too long, the shared plate and the chair in the verandah are `canon/SITUATIONS.md` §8 and §5 devices and the held look is `canon/STYLE_CANON.md` §1e.6. **And this chapter spends two of the situations inside one scene, which `SITUATIONS.md` forbids.** The rain comes on at twenty past one *inside* the song, because the love theme is written for rain (`canon/SOUND_AND_SCREEN.md` §5).
