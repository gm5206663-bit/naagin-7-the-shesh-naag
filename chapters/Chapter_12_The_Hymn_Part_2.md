# Chapter 12 — The Hymn (Part 2)

*Episode reference: Naagin, season 7, Episode 4, "Naagin Legacy Unleashed" (last part).*

*Draft; not yet accepted.*

### TITLES

*The twelve seconds the season plays on every night after its first: the theme, then straight in (`canon/TITLES_SEQUENCE.md` §I). The full forty seconds are written once, at the head of Chapter 1, and are not repeated below it.*

The logotype comes up over thundercloud, with something red falling through the frame too slowly to be rain.

A man's voice asks two words in the pitch of a been: *"Kaun hai woh."* A choir of women answers underneath him and leaves the answer unfinished.

The been stops. One low string is struck and left hanging over the black.

A drum, once. Then the story, and no music under it until the story asks.


## Above the Lane

The lane below the Suri house at two o'clock, between the out-house wall and the wall of the next plot, with one light on in the house and the rest of it in the dark.

The sky over that part of the city has been clear all night. It is not clear now.

Cloud comes up over the water tank of the Suri house and over the two towers behind it, going black at the edge, and the wind turns twice and comes in hard and takes the marigold off the gateposts and lays it in the road.

Somewhere two lanes away somebody's tin sheet goes over. The street lamp swings. And there is lightning under the cloud, deep in, no thunder with it yet, three flashes that light the inside of the sky.

On the flat roof of the house behind the Suri plot, an old woman stands with both hands on the parapet.

She is very old and it is not a thing a doctor could account for. Her hair is white and thin and pulled back, and she wears a white sari with the pallu over her head and a woollen shawl over her shoulders, and there is a brass stick hooked over her arm which she has not leaned on in sixty years.

Her forearms are so thin that the bangles stand away from the bone. Her eyes in the lightning are not an old woman's eyes.

Behind her, a girl of about twenty is holding her own pallu at her throat against the wind.

Bharani, in green, with a red bindi and quick nervous hands, and bare feet on the wet concrete.

"Ye achanak mausam kaise badal gaya?"

Bharani says.

"Aur ye bijli—", the score sounding under the turning (m26:12, Part 1's window, staged here).

"Abhi chaubees ghante bhi nahin hue hain ise." Uttara does not turn round. Her voice is dry and carries without being raised, the way old voices do in a temple.

"Maharani ne pehli baar kechuli badli hai. Aur ab se aisa lag raha hai — unki pariksha shuru ho chuki hai."

"Pariksha?"

Bharani says it to the lightning.

"Pariksha kaisi? Wo ladki hai, Maa. Woh koi—"

Uttara's hand comes out and touches her wrist.

Light, but it stops her.

"Jaan kar kya karegi?"

Uttara says it quietly.

"Tumhari jaan bhi unki jaan hai. Unke kaanton se."

"Main unhein bachana chahti hoon."

Bharani pulls her hand back.

"Agar wo sach mein naagin hai, toh wo apne aap ko nahin bacha sakti. Toh main bachaungi."

"Na."

Uttara turns her head at last, and her eyes in the lightning are not an old woman's eyes.

"Tum bachana nahi. Tum dekhna. Sirf dekhna."

The storm rolls and does not break.

A rain begins somewhere over the next colony and stops again.

"Kya wo ghar se akele bahar nikli hain?"

Bharani's voice goes up.

"Hamein unhein is haalat mein aise akela nahin chhodna chahiye tha. Kahin wo kisi bade sankat mein hongi to?"

A bucket stands on the roof by the stairhead with a cloth over it and a stone on the cloth.

Uttara's hands stay flat on the parapet.

Her bangles knock once against the stone, and the wind lays the marigold it has been carrying down on the parapet between them.

"Pehla charan nikal gaya." Uttara says it to the lane, not to the girl. "Doosra bhi." The lightning goes inside the cloud again.

"Aaj raat teesra shuru ho gaya hai."

Bharani's hands come together at her throat, with fear.

"Shiv vardani."

Uttara begins it the way the old ones begin it, flat and without hurry, and the wind takes each word and puts it down.

"Naaglok ki Rani. Anant kul ki Naagrani. Ichchhadari naagin."

"Maa—"

"Shesh Naagin."

The old woman turns her head at last, and her eyes in the lightning are not an old woman's eyes.

"Aur woh apne chauthe charan se ek raat pehle ghar se akeli bahar nikli hai. Aur use khabar nahin — wo hai kya."

Then, from the dark behind them, a sound that has no place on a Delhi rooftop: a hiss, dry and short, with nothing in the frame that has a mouth for it.

Bharani crosses herself with her thumb.

"Kya tha woh?"

Bharani whispers.

"Maa, kya tha woh?"

Uttara does not answer.

Her eyes are on the dark behind the wall.

Down in the lane the street lamp goes round on its bracket, once, and steadies.

"Kuch nahi."

Uttara says it at last, but her voice is not steady.

"Kuch nahi tha. Chalo, andar chalo."

And far off, on the black ground beyond the plot where the lane stops, there is a small sound: bones, and gravel, and then nothing.

## Two Men and a Bus

The depot yard at half past two in the morning smells of diesel and wet cardboard, and the tank of the bus nearest the gate is ticking as it cools.

A register lies open on the counter in the office window with a pen chained to it, and the pen has not been used since the last bus in.

A tin of tea stands on the step between the two men.

The glass beside the tin has a chip out of the rim, and nobody has offered the other man one.

Two buses stand in a row with their lights off and the yard light above them with a moth going round it. One of the drivers is on the step of the first with a glass of tea and his cap pushed back, and the other is under the second with a torch and his vest over his shoulder.

"Ae bhai, kal tu chhutti mein tha — warna dekhta."

The one on the step drinks.

"Ek ladki kal humari bus ke saath-saath daud rahi thi. Aise — aise."

He does it with two fingers along the rim of the step: a run that no woman runs.

"Arey kaise?"

"Jaise wo…"

He tries it again and cannot do it and stops and looks at his fingers.

"Arey. Chhod."

The man under the bus shines the torch up at the axle and says nothing for a while, with unease.

"Arey kaun, bhai? Koi bhi to nahin hai." He comes out from under it, and there is nobody on the lane behind the depot, and no sound but the diesel humming in the tank.

"O bhai."

The driver puts his tea down on the step.

"Nasha karna band kar de, please.", the score sounding under the telling (m26:49, Part 1's window, staged here).

"Nasha kahan se karta hoon main."

The man under the bus is standing now, cap in his hand.

"Main dekhta hoon. Ladki thi. Green sari. Daud rahi thi jaise koi uske peeche ho."

"Green sari?"

The driver on the step finishes his tea.

"Kahan thi wo?"

"Seedhi baithi thi bus ke peeche. Darwaze ke paas. Main dekhta hoon, phir wo gayab."

"Kahan gayab?"

"Samajh nahin aaya."

The man looks down the lane.

"Wo wahan thi, aur phir nahin thi. Jaise koi ne use utha liya ho."

"Ya tumne dekha galat."

"Main galat nahin dekhta."

The driver pushes himself up off the step.

"Chal, bas. Subah ki bus taiyaar karni hai."

"Chal raha hoon."

## Sampark

The Naag Mandir on the hill at four o'clock, and the lamp in the outer hall burning with the wind coming through the door.

Bharani has both hands round the flame.

She has put a shawl over her head now and she is kneeling, and the wind keeps taking the flame sideways and it keeps standing up again.

"Ab tak ye kabhi bhi is tarah se pehle…" Uttara says, and stops, and the word does not come.

She stands at the door with the rain on the steps behind her and her stick in her hand.

"Kya aisa koi tareeka hai?"

Bharani says.

"Jisse hum jaan saken ki wo kaisi hai — aur kahan hai?"

The lamp steadies.

The flame stands straight up and stays.

"Sampark," Uttara says.

The word sits in the hall.

Bharani looks up at her.

"Sampark," Uttara says again, and puts her hand out, and the flame leans into that hand and does not go out, the score sounding twice over the contacting (m29:18 x2 as one).

"Kaise karenge?"

Bharani says.

"Hum dono? Ya sirf tum?"

Uttara's hand stays out, the flame leaning into it.

"Tum bhi. Lekin tum pehle jaan lo ki wo kya hai. Toh tum usse darogi nahin."

"Darr?"

Bharani laughs, and it is not a laugh.

"Maa, main use darr ke liye nahin jaa rahi. Main use jaanna chahti hoon."

"Jaanna khatarnak hai."

Uttara pulls her hand back, and the flame falls back to the centre of the lamp.

"Jaanna maangta hai ek keemat. Tum wo keemat dogi?"

Bharani looks at her for a long moment.

"Main jo bhi hai, dunga."

Down the hill, a watchman on a scooter with his helmet undone passes a girl in a green sari walking up the road with a shawl over her head, and he raises a hand to her and she lifts hers.

Half a mile on, at the bottom of the hill, where the road turns, the same girl in the same green sari comes out of the dark and crosses in front of his headlight and does not look at him.

It is the same face. There is no second girl on that hill and there is no road round it, and it is the same face.

He brakes. He looks in his mirror.

There is nobody on the hill behind him and nobody on the road in front of him, and the light of his own headlight is the only thing moving.

He rides the rest of the way home without turning his head once.

## The Third Step

Half past four. The study on the second floor at the back of the Suri house, over the kitchen, and one lamp on the desk and the rest of the room in the dark.

Parmeet sits at the desk with his back to the door and his shoulders out of the shape a man's shoulders are in at that hour. His shirt is off and a shawl is round him and his glasses are off and lying on the page.

The gift book is open in front of him, and the London file under it, and the pen where his son laid it.

The phone is on the desk and it is on speaker, and the voice out of it is not a voice from this country.

"Ab samay aa gaya hai."

Parmeet says it slowly, and looks at the pages.

"Woh dono ghar mein hain. Bahar nahi."

"Wo teesra kadam." The voice from the phone is patient and old and there is a distance in it.

"Jo aakhiri kadam se — " A pause opens on the line, dry as dust.

"Bas ek kadam peeche hoga." Another breath.

"Magar kathinai badhane — " The line hisses faintly on the speaker.

"Aur kahar dhaane mein sabse aage." A longer silence.

"Ab asli pralay aayega — " the distance thins to a thread, "jab Mahadev ka teesra netra khul jaayega."

Parmeet's thumb goes along the edge of the gift book.

"Aur tab tak?"

"Tab tak sab kuch waise hi chalta rahega." A pause, and the sound of something being set down on a table made of wood, a long way away.

"Jaise ab tak chala hai. Chhupa hua. Sadak pe yahan tak ki koi shak na kare."

"Kaun hai wo?"

Parmeet says it low.

"Wo teesra kadam. Kaun hai wo?"

"Jisne tumhe yahan tak le aaya hai."

The voice is patient, and old, and there is a distance in it.

"Jisne tumhe bataya tha ki teesra kadam aayega. Ab wo kadam hai."

"Main use pehchan sakta hoon?"

"Kabhi nahin."

The voice goes softer, and farther.

"Lekin use tumhe pehchan par hai. Aur jab wo khulegi, tumhari bari hogi."

The lamp on the desk gutters once, and stands up.

Parmeet reaches out and turns the phone over and puts his palm flat on it and leaves it there for a while, looking at nothing, with dread.

Then he gets up and goes to the window and stands at the edge of it with the shawl round him, looking down at the lawn, at the gate, at the dark past the gate.

Down there, at the end of the drive, somebody has left the garden lamp on all night.

## The Hymn

The open ground where the lane stops, at ten past five in the morning, with the sky going grey above it and the buildings stopping behind.

The rain comes at ten past five, and it comes down in one piece, across the whole plot at once, and then across the whole city.

It comes down on the plot where the lane stops and the buildings do not, on the broken brick and the scrub and the plinth nobody ever built on.

The ground turns to water and the water turns to mud and the mud runs.

She is on the ground in it.

A sheet of tin lies flat in the mud a little way off, and the rain is drumming on it.

Purvi lies on her side with her arm under her and her salwar torn at the knee and one sandal gone, and her spectacles are somewhere in the dark and she cannot see, and she is not moving.

And the ground around her is moving.

Snakes come out of the scrub and the brick into the rain — not two, not a dozen: the ground is thick with them, black and grey and one that is pale as ash. They come out of the wet earth and they come out of the wall and they come over the plinth.

They do not come for her. They come to her. They arrive and they settle and they lie against her arms and her throat and her open palm.

One of them lifts its head and strikes her on the inside of the wrist. Another on the ankle. Another under the ear.

She does not wake up for any of it and her body takes each strike with a small jolt that does not reach her face.

The venom goes in where the wrist is thinnest, and it goes in cold, and the cold goes up the arm and into the shoulder and stops at the jaw, and waits.

The rain goes on. The lamp that no watchman has lit stands in the middle of that ground with no flame in it.

Somewhere behind the wall the animal is standing in the wet with its head down and its shoulder working, and it does not come on.

The score comes up at the bottom of the sky.

*Om.*

A woman's voice begins to scream, and it is not a scream of a woman in pain; it goes up and up and does not stop and does not break, and it goes out over the plot and the road and the rain.

Under it, the choir comes in.

*Om.*

The snakes lift off her all at once.

All of them, the whole ground of them, and they go back into the scrub and the wall and the wet earth, and the ground is empty.

The moon is behind the cloud and the field is the colour of a wet slate, and the rain comes down on it straight.

Purvi's fingers move in the mud.

"Didi." The word is barely breath, and it does not reach the rain.

The skin at her wrist lifts in one corner and comes up off the flesh under it in a strip, and under the strip the new skin has the sheen of a wet stone and a pattern on it that the rain fills and does not stay in.

Her jaw sets first, and two fangs come down past her lip — the first teeth in that head that belong to nothing human — and the rain on her face turns pink where it runs off them.

Her hand closes.

Her spine arches off the ground and stays arched, and her head goes back, and the rain falls into her open eyes and her eyes do not blink and are not her eyes.

The chant comes up under the choir, half words: *ki shakti*, and then something that is nearly *Krishna ki aachar*, and the words are not for the audience to keep.

*Om hawa om.*

Her eyes open gold.

The cost is on the ground under her and nobody is looking at it: two of her nails gone, the inside of her wrist bloody where the skin has come off it in a strip the length of a finger, and the cold in her so deep that the rain is warmer than her.

She will not remember any of it. She will remember waking up in a field with a torn salwar and no spectacles, and the whole of that day she will believe she fell.

And the last frame of it is not her face and not the animal and not the rain.

It is the ground she is standing on, wet and black and lit from under her, and the print of one bare foot in it that is not a woman's print any more, and the whole of that plot, and the city beyond it, and above the city the cloud coming apart.

Then the title, and the score over it, and the frame goes.
