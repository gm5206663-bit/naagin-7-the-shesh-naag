## The Hall Turns

The crowd is on its feet and not a chair in the hall holds anyone now, the wedding lights beating white-hot down on them all.

Ahana rises from the mandap with sindoor in her maang and fury in her face.

"Vikram kahan hai?" she demands.

"Vikram kahan hai? Vikram — Vikram!"

She turns on Parmeet.

"Uncle — meri shaadi Vikram se honi thi."

The fury shakes her voice.

"Is se nahin. Is se kabhi nahin."

She looks round the hall.

"Aapne dekha na? Dekha — is ladke ne kya kiya?"

Her hand shakes at him.

"Ye bijli-ka-khamba — dhokhe se mujhse shaadi racha li isne."

She draws herself up.

"Main ye shaadi nahin maanti. Hum abhi yahaan se nikalenge. Chaliye."

Under the fury, nothing in her is surprised.

The mandap gives her exactly the husband the plan needs — the rest is noise for the hall.

"Nahin, beta," Parmeet says.

"Lekin hamaare yahaan is shaadi ko bahut maante hain. Agni ko sakshi maankar — "

"Saaf-saaf kya naam hai?" an elder intones.

"Saat pheron ka bandhan."

He raises a hand over the fire.

"Saath rehta hai — saat janmon tak."

The hall quiets.

"Beta — ye sindoor."

He touches his own forehead.

"Ye mangalsutra."

His voice steadies.

"Ye sirf nishaani nahin."

A pause for the words to land.

"Ye janm-janmantar ka rishta hai."

"Haan, beta," Pammi says, beaming through the smoke.

"Vihaan beta, pata nahin kab tumhein hamaari pyaari bahu itni pasand aa gayi, aur tumne itna bada decision le liya."

"Dhokha!" Rishabh shouts.

"Dhokha kiya hai tum logon ne. Ye meri bahu banne waali thi. Tumhaari nahin — mere bete Vikram ki patni. Ye shaadi ek dhokha — "

The mike is dead, so he shouts it raw, the score striking under the fraud (m17:52).

"Ek fraud hai. Mister Dhawan, aap — aap mere samdhi banne waale the."

"Ye mere samdhi hain," Parmeet says, "aur mere samdhi rahenge."

"Main is shaadi ko nahin maanta," Dhawan says.

"Tum logon ne mazaak bana rakha hai."

His hand tightens on her wrist.

"Meri beti ki life ka — mazaak."

He pulls her toward the door, the score striking under the refusal (m18:27).

"Chalo, beta. Hum ghar jaayenge."

His voice hardens.

"Jab shaadi valid hi nahin — toh tohfa kaisa?"

He looks round the hall.

"Woh naayaab heera."

A silence.

"Shaadi ka tohfa — kisi ko nahin doonga."

"Kaise nahin doge?" Baljeet asks.

"Ye shaadi — kaise tod sakte hain aap?"

She steps forward.

"Aap bhi tod nahin sakte."

Her voice rises.

"Ahana hamaare ghar ki bahu hai."

She looks round.

"Vihaan ki biwi."

A beat.

"Ab Ahana bitiya — hamaare ghar hi mein rahegi."

"Bhai sahab sahi keh rahe hain," Dimple says.

"Hamaare ghar ki bahu ko — "

She lifts her chin.

"Aise nahin le ja sakte aap."

Rishabh catches Parmeet's sleeve and hisses low, for his ears only: "Agar woh money humein nahin mili na, toh Erul hum teenon ko magarmachch ko khila dega. Money tabhi milegi jab Vikram aur Ahana ki shaadi hogi. Shaadi karwaao."

"Humaara Changistaan nahin. Yahaan — "

He stops.

"Naagmani par dhyaan do. Warna hum mein se koi nahin bachega."

Two chairs went over by the mandap steps, and a brass tray of rice spilled beside them, and nobody has righted either.

The crowd presses back from the mandap steps by a pace, all at once, the way water draws back before it comes on.

The dead mike guards the hall's edge with its cable beside it, and a paper plate landed face-down in the cable's loop.

## The Aside

Cooler here, and dimmer — the corridor behind the hall gets the wedding lights only as spill through the door.

The sharp question rises in him — *kis kaam ke liye, kaun bhejta hai* — and does not leave his mouth.

Something in the corridor's dimness stays his hand, and he lets it.

She studies him for one long breath.

She has heard this smoothness before — on a screen, looking into a camera, saying *koi naagin nahin hai*; a man who can bury a thing that deep can bury her staying too.

And behind her eyes the plan she came with closes its fist around the seat: the murderer's house, the murderer's name, hers to unmake from the inside.

His name is the one the plan has always worn.

Then she turns to her father.

"Dad, sorry," she says, the score sounding twice over the staying (m20:23 x2 as one).

"Par ye log sahi keh rahe hain. Shaadi ho chuki hai meri. Meri maang mein Vihaan ke naam ka sindoor hai, dad. Toh ab yahi mera ghar hai. Aur main nahin ja sakti aapke saath."

"Teek hai," Dhawan says.

"Phir tum yahin baitho. Khelo yahaan ghar-ghar. Main ja raha hoon."

"Arre sahab — Dhawan sahab, Dhawan sahab!"

The elders flutter after him down the corridor, and he goes through them like weather.

"Arre bhai sahab, ye toh chala gaya. Aur Naagmani bhi nahin dekhe kya?"

"Ladki ka baap hai," Parmeet says, watching him go.

"Thoda wait karo. Thoda nakhra dikhaayega, phir maan jaayega."

On the corridor table a tray of untouched sweets softens in the heat, one fly walking the rim.

Dhawan's car starts below with a roar, and the sound hangs a moment and is gone; an envelope dropped in the elders' rush flags the corridor door.

## Vikram's Vow

The marigold has given up pretending — browning at the edges, half the hall empty around it, the wedding lights showing everything.

Vikram comes through the door with a bruise rising on his temple and his sherwani half-torn at the shoulder.

The remaining crowd goes quiet the way a field goes quiet before hail.

Aryaman is nowhere — walked out with the pheras done — and no friend stands at Vikram's shoulder.

"Vihaan!"

he shouts.

"Teri himmat kaise hui ye sab karne ki? Tu toh mera dost hai na! Aur ye — ye meri mangetar thi!"

"Ab ye meri biwi hai," Vihaan says.

Something turns under his ribs at the claim in Vikram's mouth — *meri mangetar* — jealousy, though he has no name he would give it and no cause he can put his hand on.

What he gives it instead is the insult: a shouting man spending the house's shame as coin in his own hall.

His hand finds the mandap pillar behind him and stays there, flat and hard, till Vikram is gone.

"Lekin chaahti toh main tumhein hoon, Vikram," Ahana says quickly.

"Isne mujhse dhokhe se shaadi ki. Tumhaari aadh mein."

Her Vikram-line he files with the day too — said too quickly, for Vikram's ears and the hall's, and not for his.

Vikram does not look at her.

"Maine kaha tha na."

His voice shakes.

"Kabhi chaahta hi nahin tha ye — hamaari shaadi ho."

He points.

"Aur isne wahi kiya."

His voice rises till the walls throw it back.

"Tujhe kya lagta hai? Tu ye sab karega aur main Pinky se shaadi karunga? YOU ARE WRONG. Ab tu suffer karega. Just wait and watch, Vihaan. JUST wait and watch."

At Pinky's name, in Vikram's mouth, like a thrown thing — Vihaan does not move.

The whole hall hears a taunt.

He hears his sister's name spent as currency, and files it with the day: unspent, unanswered, his.

"Vikram," Ahana says.

He walks out without looking back, and the door bangs, and the hall breathes again.

"Jo hona tha, woh ho chuka," Parmeet says heavily.

"Ahana Vihaan ki biwi hai."

He looks at them both.

"Is ghar ki bahu hai."

His voice drops.

"Bilkul sahi."

He waves a hand.

"Tum dono khadi dekh kya rahi ho?"

He points upstairs.

"Bahu ko apne kamre mein le jaao."

"Arre wah! Itni jaldi kamra taiyaar bhi ho gaya," somebody says, too brightly.

The torn shoulder of Vikram's sherwani left three gold buttons on the floor by the door, scattered where no foot has touched them.

The hall's big clock ticks on above the empty chairs, past five, into the wedding's wrecked evening.


## Taken Up

The lamps come on one by one down the length of the upstairs corridor, and the wedding's tired evening light thins between them.

Dimple and Pammi bring the bride up with a small tired procession, and the house's noise stays below.

Vihaan comes up behind them — the groom, kept three steps back by Dimple's shoulder — and stops at Pinky's shut door as they pass it.

Ahana walks between the elders with her eyes down and her shoulders shaking — the picture of the wronged bride, played to the last finger.

"Sorry, Ahana," Dimple says.

"I know you are upset."

She pats her hand.

"Lekin beta — jo hota hai, achche ke liye."

"Haan," Pammi says.

"Abhi tum aaraam karo."

They reach her door — his wife's door, his own across the corridor.

Vihaan opens it for them, his hand on the wood, and holds it while they bring her through.

"Hum — ya. Chalo. Nice," Pammi says vaguely, settling her on the bed.

For one breath, as the door swings, the bride's eyes lift — and meet his.

The distress in them is perfect.

Too perfect: it sits on her face the way jewellery sits, put on for the occasion, with the true accounts kept underneath.

He has read tampered pages for two years, and a performed total has a shine no true one carries.

He files it shut, with the earring answer from the morning, and lets the door close.

"Bhai sahab ke bete ne darwaza khola," Pammi murmurs to Dimple, pleased, as they turn away.

"Kitna tameezdaar bachcha hai."

"Chhote sahab," the servant says, coming up with the bride's milk.

"Dheere se," Vihaan says, and takes the glass from his hand and sets it inside the door himself.

He does not know why he takes it from the servant's hand instead of letting it be set down.

The servant stares after him all the way down.

Inside her door the glass of milk catches the lamp, untouched.

Her eyes touch the glass once — the doorway man bowing with her files, this one carrying milk to her door with his own hand.

Both are the same man, and the sameness unsettles her more than the milk.

She lets the room take the thought.

The corridor's lamps burn steady now, each with its small halo, all the way to the dark stairwell.

## The Moon Night

Over the city the full moon climbs to her open window, and her room by night keeps lamplight and moonlight both.

Ahana sits alone on the bed with the day's jewellery still on, and the girl in her goes back a year — the fire, the shouting, the ground going dark — and comes forward with her jaw set.

*Khatm. Un sab ka ant shuru ho chuka hai.*

The moon fills it whole.

"Yaani kechuli utaarne ki raat aa gayi."

"Nahin, nahin, nahin, nahin. Aaj nahin. Nahin."

Across the corridor his own door stands ajar, and his window beyond it open to the night.

Then murmur, too low for words, and nothing.

Ananta Arora.

In his wife's room, past eleven, and no alarm under the gasp.

"Ye tune kya kiya, Purvi?" Ananta asks quietly.

"Tu pyaar karti hai — aur pyaar ko dafn karke Vihaan se shaadi kar li, use mitaane ke liye?"

"Jab tune use chhua tha, Vikram ke saamne?"

"Jab se mujhe pata chala ye ghar kya hai."

"Jo kiya, theek kiya," she says.

"Jo mere badle ke hisaab se sahi tha."

"Apna badla poora karne ke liye — "

She meets her sister's eyes.

"Main har ek ko mita dungi — yahaan."

A pause.

"Vihaan ko bhi."

Ananta is gone before the lamp gutters twice.

Ahana stands a breath longer — then goes down the stairs in the dark, takes the house car out herself, and drives into the wedding night alone.

## Outside Pinky's Door

Past eleven the upstairs corridor belongs to the lamps alone, their low light pooling outside Pinky's shut door while the house settles into its wedding night.

Vihaan stands in front of the door with his back to it.

He came here from his own window across the corridor, with a gasped name shut in his file and her door cold behind him — past the milk cooling inside hers — and he will stand here till the house sleeps or breaks.

In the shut file behind his eyes the new name sits beside the day's others: Ananta.

And under it, the question with an address.

It holds him here better than the guard-duty does — the door, the sister, the wife across the house who knows what no file holds.

She broke once already today, in the morning, and Aryaman's promise held her; he will not let the night finish what the morning started.

Inside, Pinky sits on the bed with her hands in her lap.

The door between them stands ajar a finger's width, because he promised her the truth in the morning and the truth needs a door it can come through.

"Bhai," she says through the gap.

"Tumne kar li? Sach mein kar li?"

"Kar li," he says.

"Saat phere. Agni ke saamne."

The gap goes silent.

"Vikram chala gaya?" she asks.

"Chillaya, aur chala gaya," he says.

"Tumne suna hoga."

"Suna," she whispers.

"Sab suna. Mera naam bhi."

"Haan," he says.

"Usne kaha, sabke saamne, ki woh tumse shaadi nahin karega. Gusse mein kaha — par kaha."

The gap goes silent for a long time.

He hears her breathe, once, twice, the way a person breathes when the air has turned against them.

"Theek hai," she says at last, very small.

"Ahana didi — " she starts.

"Bhabhi — ruk gayin?"

"Haan," he says.

"Usne kaha — yehi uska ghar hai ab."

A pause.

"Main dekhna chaahti hoon use," she whispers.

"Vikram ko. Ek baar. Bhai, please — "

"Nahin," he says, gently, and does not move from the door.

"Tum use aise nahin dekhogi. Aur woh tumhein aise nahin dekhega. Jo tootna hai, ye darwaza uske beech mein rahega."

"Pinky," he says.

"Darwaza band kar do, bhai," she says.

"Aaj ke liye."

He shuts the door between them with his own hand, softly — one low string under the shut door — and stands outside it with his back to the wood.

Then a car turns into the drive below — at this hour, with the house asleep — and his head comes up off the door.

"Andar se band kar lo," he tells the gap, and is gone down the stairs before she can answer.

## Radhika's Door

The bottles in Aryaman's hands catch the light from the street lamps as he walks the night-empty road outside Radhika's house.

He has been drinking since he walked out of the wedding; these are not the day's first bottles.

He is drunk past steadiness and short of collapse, and he throws the first bottle at her gate.

It shatters.

"Radhika!" he screams at the dark windows.

"Mujhe chhod ke mat jaao."

He sways on his feet.

"Please."

His voice breaks.

"I LOVE YOU."

He steadies on the gate.

"I LOVE YOU SO MUCH."

The windows stay dark.

"Tum jo bologi — taiyaar hoon main karne ko."

He spreads his hands.

"I will do ANYTHING."

His voice cracks.

"Please."

He sags against the bars.

"Please don't leave me."

A whisper now.

"I know you love me."

No window lights.

He throws the second bottle, and the blood starts on his hand where the glass comes back.

"Magar ab tumhaare saath nahin ho sakti,"

he says in her voice, to himself, swaying.

"Meri aadhi zindagi — tumne barbaad kar di thi."

He sways where he stands.

"Par jo bacha tha na — "

He laughs, broken.

"Aaj maine khud hi barbaad kar li."

He stares at the dark windows.

"Ab Aryaman toh — poora ka poora khatm ho chuka."

His voice drops.

"I am — "

He laughs.

"I am sab — khatm."

Headlights swing round the corner, too fast. The brakes scream. And a woman's hand hauls him backwards by the collar out of the road, hard enough to tear the seam, and the car goes past with its horn tearing the night.

He hangs on her shoulder, breathing paint and dust.

"Radhika," he mumbles.

"Tum aa gayi. Mujhe pata tha — tumhein ab bhi fikr hai."

She gets him to her car somehow, pours him into the passenger seat, and fastens his seatbelt with hands that do not shake.

The broken glass glitters across the road in the lamp-light, green and brown, in a wide fan.

Her car starts on the first turn, and the headlights take the road home.

## The Crossing

Black and flat between the two countries the lake takes the boat, the storm sky pressing down, the boat's lamp throwing a small ring on the water as the landing stage's posts go by.

"Bahut kam log jaante hain," Parmeet says, standing in the prow, "ki Hindustan aur Changistaan ke beech mein — "

The boat rocks, the score sounding under the lake (m30:05).

"Bas ek badi jheel ka faasla hai."

He grips the rail.

"Har saal — kisi na kisi toofaani raat ko — "

The wind rises.

"Hamaare baadshahon ka humein bulaawa aata hai — "

He looks at them.

"Aur humein jaana padta hai."

A pause.

"Aur is saal — "

His voice drops.

"Hum ek bahut hi wahiyaat khabar — "

"Ye hai hamaara desh — Changistaan," Parmeet says.

"Baap aur patang ki zameen."

"Yahaan andhera hi andhera hai — "

"Kisi kaid-khaane jaisa."

"Ye desh Hindustan jaisa nahin."

"Koyle sa kaala — "

He looks round.

"Logon ke paas paisa nahin yahaan."

His voice drops.

"Jinke paas betahaasha paisa hai — "

"Unke paas insaaniyat nahin hai."

"Money — Naagmani se."

"Jitni kaali hamaare desh ki niyat hai — "

His voice lowers.

"Hamaari Pashaon ki roohein — usse bhi kaali."

"Jo maaf karna jaanti hi nahin."

He stares ahead.

"Kya karein?"

"Is muflisi mein logon ko khush rakhne ke liye — "

He holds up two fingers.

"Sirf do hi cheezein kaam aati hain — "

"Nafrat aur tabaahi."

His voice firms.

"Maksad saaf hai."

"Agar hum Hindustan se jeet nahin sakte — "

"Toh use barbaad kar dein — kam se kam."

"Mitaa kar rakh dein."

The boat's lamp gutters in the wind off the black water, and steadies.

On the far shore a single dog barks, once, and stops, and the dark takes the shore back, all but one lit door.

## Brought Home

Headlights sweep the gate as the car comes up the drive, and the house at midnight answers with only the porch lamp.

Vihaan comes down the stairs from Pinky's door with one phone in his hand.

The car pulled him off his guard — no house car goes out on a wedding night without his knowing, and this one went out with the new bride driving.

In the porch light the driver and the chowkidar unload Aryaman from the passenger seat: drunk past standing, one hand cut and wrapped in a handkerchief, reeking of the street.

The bride is nowhere — upstairs already, by the stairs' sound; a door latches above as Vihaan reaches the last step.

"Chhote sahab," the driver says, straightening.

"Madam gaadi chala kar laayin. Sahab — sahab sadak par the. Gaadi — bach gaye."

Vihaan looks at his brother's cut hand, at the torn collar, at the seatbelt crease run crooked across the sherwani.

"Doctor ko mat bulao," he says.

"Upar le jaao. Uske kamre mein. Meri biwi ke kamre mein nahin. Paani rakh do sirhane. Aur ye haath — subah dekhte hain."

The protection in the order surprises no one but him, and he files even that shut.

"Ji, chhote sahab," the driver says.

"Madam ne gaadi kahaan se uthaayi thi?" he asks.

"Ghar se, sahab," the driver says.

"Khud nikaali. Chabiwale ko kuch nahin kaha. Wapas bhi khud laayin."

Vihaan files it with the day, shut: the bride drove at midnight, alone, and brought Aryaman home torn, and the house slept through all of it.

"So jaao," he says.

"Subah kisi se kuch mat kehna. Ye ghar ki baat hai."

"Ji," the driver says, and they carry Aryaman up, and the porch goes quiet.

Upstairs, behind her latched door, the bride stands at the window with the moon on her face.

"Vihaan, teri maut mere haath se tay hai," she breathes, the score striking under the vow (m31:54).

"Aur main tumhein itni aasaani se nahin marne dungi."

The porch lamp burns on over the empty drive, haloed with the night's first insects.

The wedding's last marigold string hangs torn across the gate, swaying a finger's width in no wind at all.
