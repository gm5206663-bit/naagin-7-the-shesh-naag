## History Repeats

The temple holds the deep night in rows of burning lamps, and the door stands open on the dark she went out into.

Bharani stands between the pillars with her lamp lowered, and the fear she has carried for years comes up in her at last without a hand to hold it down.

"Jis baat ka dar tha,"

she says to the empty air, "bilkul wahi hone ja raha hai. Hum is anhooni ko rok nahin paaye. Itihaas phir se khud ko dohra raha hai."

Her voice thins.

"Ek naagin ek insaan ke pyaar mein pad rahi hai. Aur isi ko rokne ke liye maine Purvi ko maara tha."

"Haan, Bharani."

The answer comes out of the lamp-smoke, and Uttara's restless soul stands there, grey and grieving.

"Main jaanti hoon."

The soul's voice thins.

"Isi baat ka dukh hai — hum naagrani ko,"

the soul says, "insaani pyaar se rok nahin paaye."

The soul's hands fold and unfold.

"Kitni koshish ki thi humne, Bharani."

"Nahin, nahin."

Bharani turns on her, fierce.

"Ruk jaaiye — aap mat jaaiye. Aapko badla lena hoga."

But the soul does not go, and the temple wind moves the flames, and the past comes up between them the way smoke comes up off a snuffed wick.

Uttara's voice shakes.

"Naagrani apna dil laga rahi hai. Use rokna hoga. Us ladke se — us insaan se — door rakhna hoga use."

She catches Bharani's wrist with a hand that has no weight.

"Mujhe yaad dilaana hoga — badla lena hai use."

The soul's grip tightens without weight.

"Desh ko bachaana hai."

A breath like wind off the lamps.

"Wahi karna — tere se jo ban pade."

Her grey eyes hold Bharani's.

"Kisi insaan ke pyaar mein — naagrani ko nahin padne dena."

"Vaada karti hoon aapse."

Bharani's voice goes iron.

"Iske baad jo bhi hoga — naagin Vihaan se sirf nafrat karengi. Pyaar kabhi nahin."

The lamp between them gutters in a draught off the door, and Bharani cups her hand round the flame till it stands straight again.

The lamps lean all one way, and her account comes, low and dreadful, out of the year it happened in.

"Jab maine naagrani ko upar jaate dekha, to mujhe pata tha ke wo usse madad maangne ja rahi hai."

Bharani stares into the flame.

"Isiliye maine turant Vihaan ka roop le liya. Aur maine naagrani ko aise maara ke unhe lage ke unka ant ho gaya hai."

A breath.

"Jaanboojh kar."

Her face twists in the lamplight.

"Reedh ki haddi se door — vaar kiya tha maine."

A breath.

"Ichchhadhari kabhi itni aasaani se nahin maregi."

Her voice steadies.

"Main jaanti thi — jab tak vaar na ho."

A pause.

"Reedh ki haddi par."

"Lekin jab baaki sab ne vaar kiya — aur unhone dam tod diya — to mujhe — "

She stops.

The temple holds its breath, the score sounding under the question (m03:40).

she whispers, frustrated, to the shut sanctum, to the queen who is gone to the sea.

## The Sea

The sea lies black to the horizon past midnight, and the moon puts one shaking road on it from the shore to the deep.

Ahana stands at the water's edge with her sari tucked to her knees, and the wind takes her hair, and she does not hesitate the way people hesitate who mean to turn back.

"Itna jaanti hoon main — bas itna,"

she says to the water, "ke tumhe marne nahin de sakti main. Aryaman."

The name goes out over the waves and comes back to her off nothing.

She steps in.

The cold takes her legs, then her waist, then her shoulders, and she dives, the score sounding under the dive (m04:36), and the sea closes over her head without a sound the shore could carry.

Down in the green dark she moves the way her blood knows, and the water parts for her.

Amid the cold and the pressure the memories surface in her unbidden — a channel lobby at half past one, priced water, a name given with the surname left off; a bulletin-name read out over her parents' television while both sisters hear it for the first time.

Moments of laughter and closeness, unspoken and unspent, Purvi's own, from before the oath buried her.

"Oh shit."

The breath leaves her in a burst of silver as something vast moves past in the dark beyond her — and is gone, and she is alone again with her heartbeat loud in her ears.

There.

Between the rocks, swaying in the current though no current should reach there — the blue herb, the anokhi neeli jadibooti, with its liquid shining at the heart of it like a lamp under water.

She takes it.

She rises.

She breaks the surface with the night air cold in her throat, and wades out, and stands streaming on the sand, and lifts the herb liquid to her mouth and drinks it down to the last shining drop, the score sounding under the drink (m04:56).

## The Empty Guard

The temple keeps the small hours in lowered lamps, and the unconscious man lies between the pillars where the coils settled round him.

His eyes open.

For one breath he lies still, staring up at the lamp-smoke and the dark of the roof.

Then the memory of the portraits floods him — gold on black, her face newest among them — and he sits up with his hand to his pounding head.

"Mujhe sabko bataana hoga."

His voice cracks on it.

"Sach bataana hoga — ghar jaake, sabko."

He gets to his feet, swaying, and looks once at the thick shadows between the pillars — coils and eyes, watching, not moving — and walks out past them, faster with every step, into the night.

His shoe knocks a brass plate at the pillar's foot, and the plate rings once against the stone and lies still.

The sea-wind comes in at the door an hour later with Ahana on it, salt in her hair and the herb's strength new in her veins — and stops dead at the empty flags.

"Bharani."

Her voice is very quiet.

"Aryaman kahan hai?"

"Bharani — "

She turns.

"Aryaman yahan nahin hai."

"Maine kaha tha aapse."

Ahana's hands close into fists.

"Rokna tha use. Jaane nahin dena tha."

Bharani lifts her chin.

"Aadesh tha aapka — Aryaman ko koi nuksaan na pahunchaaye."

Each word lands separate.

"Ye nahin kaha tha aapne — use jaane se roko. Isiliye jab wo gaya,"

she says, "naag-rakshakon ne use jaane diya — roka nahin."

A beat.

"Varna aadesh ke khilaaf chale jaate wo — aapke aadesh ke."

Ahana stares at her.

The loophole sits between them, neat as a coiled snake, made of her own sworn words.

"Aryaman ko rokna hi hoga mujhe."

Her voice drops to iron, the score striking under the resolve (m06:07).

"Varna meri asli pehchaan — sabko bata dega wo."

She turns toward the door, toward the town, toward the house.

"Aur Bharani galat nahin hai. Jaan to meri Aryaman ne hi li thi na."

The belief leaves her mouth like a stone.

"Isiliye — chaahe jo keemat, ab, chukaani pad jaaye — rok ke rahoongi main Aryaman ko."

Bharani says nothing to that part.

She looks at the lamps, and the lamps lean, and say nothing either.

There — out in the human town, past hearing — a man walks home through the dark muttering that a woman he knows is not human at all.

Naagin hai.

The word goes with him down the empty road like a second pair of footsteps, the score sounding twice over the mutter (m06:36+m06:50 as one).

## The Laugh-Off

The Suri hall holds the last of the night in one burning lamp, and the windows stand grey with the coming dawn.

Vihaan sits in the chair by the cold fireplace with his diary shut on his thumb and his eyes on the door, and the lamp has burned an inch down while he watched it.

He has not slept.

The house sleeps all round him — his wife's room empty, her bed cold, the night gone and no word with it — and his file lies open in his head with all its questions intact, and nothing in it lets him close it.

Somewhere above, a door shuts in the night's last hour, and the house settles again into its held breath.

The door opens.

Aryaman comes in with the dawn on his shoulders and his cut hand in a fresh handkerchief, and stops in the middle of the hall with his eyes too bright.

## Where She Was

His study keeps the dawn behind slatted blinds, and the desk stands deep in the papers of the sale — files, sheets, the investments going out piece by piece — with one old file lying open on top.

He shut the door behind them himself.

That is the first thing she understands, and it lands like a lock turning: the hall has ears — this house has proved it twice in two days — and whatever he means to say, he means to say it where no one listens in.

"Kahan thi tum?" His voice is quiet.

It is the quiet that frightens her, not the words.

"Raaj bhar. Kahan thi?"

She looks at him — hollow-eyed, the lamp-night still on him, the diary's edge white where his thumb held it — and the lies come up ready-made, smooth as poured cream, and stick in her throat anyway.

"Mandir,"

she says.

"Mandir gayi thi."

"Mandir." He repeats it the way he repeats a figure that does not tally.

"Saari raat. Akeli. Bina bataaye."

"Puja thi — "

"Kaunsi puja raat bhar chalti hai, Ahana?"

He takes one step toward her, and stops himself, and his hands close at his sides.

"Tum jaanti ho maine kya socha? Teen baje. Chaar baje. Paanch baje."

Each hour lands.

"Maine socha — Vikram."

The name hangs in the slatted light like dust.

"Vikram se milne gayi hogi."

His jaw moves once, without his leave.

"Uska dil toota hai. Tumhaari shaadi — mujhse — uske muh pe hui hai. Aur tum — "

He breathes.

"Tum raat bhar ghar se ghaayab. Batao. Kya sochta main?"

"Ye — ye tum kya keh rahe ho?"

Her fury rises to meet his hurt, grateful for something to fight.

"Tumhe — tumhe mujh pe bharosa nahin?"

"Bharosa?"

Something flashes in his face, too quick to read.

"Bharosa karta hoon. Tabhi to pooch raha hoon — bataane ke liye keh raha hoon, ilzaam ke liye nahin."

He holds her eyes, and will not let them go.

"Dukhta hai. Ye — ye jo raat bhar hua — dukhta hai. Aur jalan — "

He stops.

His throat moves.

"Jalan bhi hoti hai. Main jhooth nahin boloonga tumse. Hoti hai."

She stares at him.

Against everything — the oath, the face over the knife, the years — something in her chest turns over at the plain admitting of it, the man who reads faces for a living laying his own face down without a cover on it.

"Sun lo." His voice drops.

"Ek baat sun lo, Ahana. Tum — meri patni ho." He says it the way a man sets a stone down.

"Ye — ye koi khel nahin. Ye baat samajh lo. Samajhna hoga tumhe."

The screen rises in her behind his shoulder — unbidden, perfect: the channel's bulletin, his voice saying there is no naagin, the cover told smooth for the house's sake.

*Koi naagin nahin hai.*

The man on the screen said what the house needed.

The man in the room asks what he needs.

And between the two of them, whole and unprompted, the calibration settles in her like a weight finding its pan: he covers what he is given to cover.

Give him the half he can carry — and he will carry it, and ask nothing past it.

"Mandir gayi thi."

Her voice comes steadier now.

"Sach. Raat bhar — prarthna. Vrat."

She meets his eyes, and does not look away, and the lie wears the temple's true half like gold wears gilt.

"Isse zyaada — isse zyaada mat poocho. Abhi nahin."

He looks at her for a long moment — the set face, the salt-stiff sari, the sindoor dark in her maang — and nods once, slowly.

The file opens in his head, and the half-truth goes into it, and the file shuts.

For now.

Everything in his file waits.

"Achha."

He turns to the desk.

"Achha."

Her eyes follow his turning — to the desk, to the papers of the sale — and stop.

The old file lies open on top, and the lamplight lies across its first page, and the date on it catches her the way a hook catches a sleeve.

Ghaziabad.

That night's date.

And under it, his hand — his signature, the young hurried hand of months ago, on the deal's last page with the town's stamp beside it.

Her breath stops.

The paper says what it says, plain as print: that town, that night, his hand on the deal's last page — the real Vihaan, hours from this house.

Not here.

Not on those stairs.

Not with that knife.

Against it, unbidden, the other thing rises — the face over the knife, the laugh, the promise, months old and yesterday-clear.

The face is his.

The paper puts him elsewhere.

Both cannot be true.

Both stand in front of her, and neither moves.

She says nothing.

Her face gives the room nothing.

The paper goes into her own file, shut, with everything else — and the file burns where it lies.

He reaches past the file for his diary, lying where he left it at the desk's edge — and his hand overshoots, and catches the top drawer, and the drawer slides open an inch, two inches, on its old runners.

Her eyes drop with it.

Out of habit.

Out of nothing.

Inside, wrapped in a square of faded blue cloth, folded with a care that stops her breath a second time — a pair of spectacles.

Broken at one corner.

The frame bent where a hand once closed on it too hard.

Her spectacles.

Purvi's.

The ones she held on the back stairs while the bulb hummed and a boy sat below her with a bottle against her wrist.

The room goes very far away.

She hears the blinds tick.

She hears her own heart.

He follows her eyes.

He sees the drawer open, and what lies in it, and something crosses his face — not alarm, not guilt; something older, worn smooth by keeping.

"Kisi ki amanat hai." His voice is quiet.

He does not look at her; he looks at the cloth, the bent frame.

"Seedhiyon pe mili thi. Peechhe waali seedhiyon pe."

A pause.

"Lauta nahin paaya. Waqt hi nahin mila."

*Amanat.*

A trust.

Kept — how long?

Folded in cloth.

In his drawer, where his diary lives, where his own hand goes every day.

And the knowing comes up in her whole, the way the sea came up over her head in the night — not thought, not suspicion, knowing: he kept them.

All these months.

He found them on those stairs and he kept them, carefully, the way people keep what they love and cannot name.

He loved her.

Purvi.

Back then.

While she breathed and walked and never knew.

For one breath, held past manners past fury past everything, he looks at her — the long corridor look — and something looks back out of her eyes, sudden and stripped and true, and is gone before either of them can hold it.

Purvi.

One breath.

Gone.

He files it with the rest.

He does not know what he filed.

"Jaao." His voice is gentle now, and spent — one low string under the release.

"Aaraam kar lo. Raat bhar ki — thaki hogi."

She goes out past him without a word spoken between them that the house could use, and the door shuts soft behind her, and the corridor takes her with her two new knowings burning shut inside her like coals in a covered brazier.
