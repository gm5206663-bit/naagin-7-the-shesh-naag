# Chapter 24 — The Wrong Groom (Part 1)

The logotype comes up over thundercloud, with something red falling through the frame too slowly to be rain.

A man's voice asks two words in the pitch of a been: *"Kaun hai woh."*

A choir of women answers underneath him and leaves the answer unfinished.

The been stops.

One low string is struck and left hanging over the black.

A drum, once.

Mahadev's trishul is not shown, but the thunder is his.

Then the story, and no music under it until the story asks.

## Pinky's Morning

Morning comes to the small room at the top of the house through one window only, and lays a clean bar of light across the desk where the wedding day waits to begin.

Vihaan sits at the desk with the laptop open and one earbud in, and Bombay talks in his ear about a feed that must run clean through fifteen days of dust.

On the desk the diary lies open at today's page, and today's page says the wedding first and the money after, in his own hand.

Pinky sits on the bed with her knees up, still in the suit she slept in.

She has not dressed for the wedding, and nobody has come to dress her.

"Bombay ko bolo," he says into the phone.

"Feed aaj shaam paanch baje tak locked chahiye. Uske baad mela ground ki light mere haath mein nahin."

He listens.

"Haan. Meri zimmedari."

He takes the earbud out.

The room goes quiet except for the house below, which drums like a dhol it cannot put down.

Under the diary's edge Verma's number waits where he left it days ago, and the missing-person file it asked for has still not come.

"Bhai," Pinky says.

"Aaj woh shaadi kar raha hai."

"Haan," he says.

"Us ladki se. Jise woh jaanta tak nahin."

He looks at her.

Her face does its best to be still, and fails the way his own never does.

"Pinky," he says.

"Tum taiyaar ho jaao. Neeche mat aao. Ye tumhara din nahin hai, aur ghar ko aaj tumhari himmat dekhne ki zaroorat nahin."

"Kya karun, bhai?" she asks.

"Kamre mein baith kar dhun sunun?"

"Haan," he says.

"Kaan band karke nahin. Khol kar. Jo sunai de, mujhe bataana. Main neeche rahunga."

Beside the diary, the steel glass of water sweats through the morning untouched, the spoon still in it.

Her wedding-guest suit waits folded on the bed's edge, the paper price tag tucked under the collar where no one sees it.

Downstairs somebody tests the mandap mike, and the whine climbs the stairwell and dies.

Someone knocks once — and through the door comes Aryaman's voice from the stairs, sharp, calling her name.

Pinky lifts her head, and something in her face gives way all at once.

"Ye Tina hai," she says quickly.

"Mujhe nahin — Tina ko bula raha hai."

"Tina nahin," he says.

"Tum. Apna naam mat chhupaao, Pinky — woh tumhein bula raha hai, aur tum jaanti ho kyun."

She is past him and out of the door before he can stand — feet running on the stairs, her own voice raised and breaking, and then Aryaman's voice after it, sharp.

He sits back down.

The diary's wedding page; the number under its edge that no office has answered; the bride's house with no floor under it no matter how he knocks — Vikram's deflections stacked three deep where answers should be.

A stranger marries into his house today, and the file on her is all cover and no boards.

He shuts the diary on it.

Watch, file, hold: that is the day's whole order, and he gives it to himself standing.

## Pinky Breaks

Noise climbs the stairs ahead of her — the house below at full wedding pitch, the guest corridor carrying it, the tube light at its end buzzing over the stairs.

Pinky stands in the corridor with her back to the wall, and her voice carries down to the hall.

"Aap kar kya rahe hain?" she demands.

"Humne kaha tha ye shaadi nahin hogi. Shaadi toh ho rahi hai, aur mera pyaar Ahana ko mil raha hai. Iska matlab meri zindagi toh bematlab hai na."

Aryaman comes up the stairs two at a time.

"Pinky,"

he says.

"Neeche chalo. Abhi."

"Nahin," she says, and laughs the laugh that frightens the servants.

"Pinky — paki Pinky — tu pagal ho gayi hai. Am I mad? Haan, main pagal ho gayi hoon. Humaare khoon mein hai pagalpan."

She runs.

Her door slams, and the bolt goes home, and something glass breaks inside against tile.

Aryaman puts his shoulder to the door once, twice, and the bolt holds, and then the key turns from the inside of the next room — the servant's pass — and he goes through the bathroom and comes out of her door with blood on one knuckle and her wrist in his hand.

"Are you mad?"

he asks her.

"Haan, main pagal ho gayi hoon," she says, trembling.

"Aap bhi toh us Radhika ke pyaar mein abhi tak pagal ho. Aur main Vikram ke — "

Her voice breaks clean open.

"Main nahin jee paungi uske bina. I can't live without him. I love him, bhai. Please listen to me."

Three teeth short, a hairbrush marks the floor, and the mirror above the dresser carries a crack from one corner halfway down.

On the bed the bridal-guest favours sit in a row, unopened, each tied with the same gold thread.

"Tere bhai ne tujhse kaha hai na?" Aryaman asks.

"Vikram ka phera us Ahana ke saath nahin hoga — ye mera vaada raha. Bharosa rakh."

"Trust me," Pinky repeats, and the fight goes out of her all at once, and she sits down on the floor where she stands.

## The Accusation

Inside the side room off the hall the wedding morning reaches only as dim, and a single bar of light from the door cuts across the carpet.

Parmeet shuts the door and turns on Rishabh, the score striking under the accusation (m01:29).

"Tumhari himmat kaise hui humein dhokha dene ki?"

he asks.

"Tumhein kya laga? Humein pata nahin chalega. Itne saal ki dosti bhool gaya tu?"

"Parmeet, lekin maine kiya kya hai?" Rishabh asks.

"Naagmani samet London khisakne ka plan bana raha hai," Parmeet says.

"Puchte ho — kiya kya hai? Ye kaisa plan? Rishabh, jhooth bolna band kar. Humne apne kaanon suna hai Ahana ko tere London ticket ke baare mein charcha karte hue."

"Tum logon ka dimaag kharab ho gaya hai kya?" Rishabh asks.

"Yaar, maine koi aisa plan nahin banaaya tha. Na London ke liye, na Naagmani ke liye."

Outside the door the corridor stands empty, and then it does not: Mahasapera comes down it without hurry, slows at the sound of the Naagmani word, and stops where the door's bar of light does not touch him.

"Naagwani?"

he says under his breath.

"Matlab andar wali naagin koi aam naagin nahin."

Rishabh's voice rises inside.

"Kya kar raha hai tu?"

Mahasapera smiles at the shut door.

"Woh Anant kul ki Naagrani hai,"

he says to no one.

"Ab main inko ek shabd nahin kahunga. Is Naagmani ko main yahaan se uthaa le jaaunga. Naagin bhi haasil karoonga, Naagmani bhi."

A brass key turns slowly in the lock of the side room from the inside, and stops halfway.

On the wall the framed photograph of the house's groundbreaking holds its dust undisturbed, eleven men with shovels and no smiles.

## The Basket

The bride stands half-dressed in the mirror, and Ahana's room behind her is all wedding-morning light and open door, the passage beyond it, the been's note a breath away.

*Ichchadhari.*

The word has landed in him already, at the threshold, in the held breath.

Now Mahasapera comes back along the passage with the been in his hand, and he sets his mouth to it before the door.

The been's note goes through the door like a needle through cloth.

Inside, Ahana stops with one earring in her hand.

The note finds the spine and climbs it.

She dances two steps she does not choose, and the third step is not hers at all, and where the bride stood the naagin pours down shining and coils.

Mahasapera lifts the basket's lid.

"Aa ja,"

he says.

The earring winks from the dresser where her fingers left it, one of a pair.

Across the threshold the white line of powder runs end to end, unbroken, keeping nothing now.

He settles the lid, takes up the basket's rope, the score sounding three times over the taking (m02:59+m03:11+m03:15 as one), and goes down the back stairs with it, and the house below drums on and knows nothing.

## The Empty Room

By noon the search has taken the upstairs passage, the score sounding three times over the search (m04:08+m04:26+m04:31 as one) — heat and noise in equal measure, the stairwell window's light fallen across Ahana's shut door.

Vihaan comes up with the search because Parmeet's voice sent for every son of the house, and he stands at the back of the crowd while Tina goes from room to room saying the bride is nowhere.

Below, at the mandap, Vikram waits with the sehra in his lap, asking the air.

"Kahan ho, baby?" Vikram calls up the stairs.

"Kitna time kyun laga diya? Ahana to room mein hai hi nahin. Kya tumne har jagah theek se dekh liya?"

"Ji," somebody answers.

Bharani pushes through the crowd with her face white.

"Apne kamre mein toh Naagrani hai nahin — phir woh gayi kidhar?" she asks the passage at large.

They open the bride's door.

The room stands wrecked — the dresser swept, the bed stripped to the mattress, a chair on its side.

"Is kamre ki ye gat kisne banaayi?" Parmeet asks.

Dimple wrings her hands.

"Bhai sahab, mujhe Ahana ki bahut fikr ho rahi hai. Kahin use kuch ho na gaya ho — mister Dhawan ko fauran bata dena chahiye humein."

"Haan, chal," Parmeet says.

Vihaan looks at the wreckage the way he looks at a tampered page: once, whole, and without touching.

His eyes stop on the washroom door, shut, with no light under it.

"Papa," he says.

"Main gate par khada hota hoon. Dhawan sahab aayenge toh seedha upar nahin aayenge. Pehle aap se baat hogi."

Parmeet looks at him for one beat, and nods.

"Ja."

He turns to go — and the washroom door opens, the score sounding under the opening (m06:13).

"Itni bheed ek saath mere kamre mein," Ahana says, blinking.

"Dara diya mujhe."

"Beta ji, dar toh hum gaye the," Dimple says.

"Humne aapko sab jagah dekha. Aap kahin nahin mili. Aur ye room ka aisa haal kaise hua?"

"Main na apne earring ka jodaa dhoond rahi thi," the bride says, smooth as poured cream.

"Usi talaash mein ye sab ho gaya."

The score sounds under the lie (m06:23).

Vihaan stands still.

The answer came too fast and too complete, like a total that has been worked backwards from, and he files it where he files everything the house says too smoothly: shut, and dated today.

"Ye sab chhodo," somebody says.

"Humein muhurat ke liye late ho raha hoga na."

"Par maine is room mein already check kiya tha," Tina insists, staring.

"Par tum yahan nahin thi."

The bride smiles at her.

"Room tum dekh chuki thi, Tina. Washroom mein jhaanka thodi tha."

"Ye saari baatein hum baad mein karenge," Dimple says.

"Pandit neeche tumhara intezaar kar rahe hain. Let's go. Chal, chal, sab log chal."

The crowd pours down the stairs with the bride in its middle.

Tina stands where she left her, shaking, half a step behind them all.

Vihaan offers her his arm, the way the house's son does for a guest who shakes.

"Chalo, Tina," he says.

"Neeche pandit intezaar kar rahe hain. Aur tumhaari tabiyat theek nahin lag rahi."

She takes it.

He walks her down behind the rest, and his face gives the stairs nothing.

The crowd's feet missed a single bangle on the landing — red glass in two clean halves.

The stairwell window throws its bars across them both as they go down, light and dark, light and dark.

## Snow and Verdict

Snow-glare presses at the hideout's stone room from every crack, and the cold comes off the walls in sheets the one fire cannot dent.

Ahana lies human-shaped on the stone, shaking, her breath coming short, the score sounding three times over the cold (m07:01+m07:09+m07:14 as one).

The been plays on without pause, and each note takes a little more of the warmth out of her fingers.

*Cold.*

The word in her is her mother's voice.

*Cold, and the ground, and the men with the torches.*

She remembers the night the family died — the fire, the shouting, the ground going dark under her feet — and the memory comes up through the cold like a hand through ice.

"Anant kul ka taaj mere sar hai — main uski Naagrani," she says, sitting up.

"Dushman ke saamne ghutne tek doon — itni kamzor nahin."

The score sounds under the naming (m08:00).

Mahasapera lowers the been a finger's width.

"Agar tu naaginon ki naagin hai,"

he says, "toh main anek siddhiyon ka swami hoon. Sirf taap nahin — aur bhi jaanwar mere bas mein hain. Woh tera sabse puraana bairi. Tu shayad usse takraayi nahin. Barf ki gufaaon mein in ichchadhari naaginon se mera naata saalon puraana hai. Woh kab se tera in gufaaon mein raah dekh raha hai. Maat khaayi thi usne ek naagin se. Is baar humne apni siddhi se usko aur bhi shaktishaali aur khatarnaak bana diya. Wakt aa gaya hai uske badle ka."

The snow at the cave's mouth stands up and walks: the Yeti comes down the slope with its arms wide, roaring.

What follows is fast and shine-bright and terrible.

She meets it head-on, and the snow flies up in walls, and when the walls come down she stands and it does not.

Mahasapera backs against the stone with his hands up.

"Maaf kar do,"

he says.

"Mujhe kshama kar do. Kshama karo mujhe. Daya karo mujh par. Bas daya karo."

"Naagin ki adaalat gunahgaaron ko bakhshti nahin," she says.

"Wahaan sirf maut banti hai. Tera bhi wahi hashr hoga."

"Sharanaagatam," he whispers.

She laughs once, short and bright.

"Chand tandav."

The ice on the wall cracks in one long line from floor to ceiling, and holds.

The been lies where it fell, split at the mouthpiece, silent for the first time that day.

## The Pheras

The wedding crowd sits three deep around the swept ground with the afternoon sun on its shoulders, ringing a mandap dressed in marigold.

Vihaan sits in the family's row with his hands empty — the phones left upstairs, the diary shut, the day given to the house.

Pinky's absence sits beside him like a second person; nobody asks after her, which tells him Parmeet's orders went ahead of him.

The bride sits beside the groom with her eyes down.

The groom's sehra hides his face.

Aryaman's phone rings in the family's row.

"Haan, bolo,"

he says into it.

"Main sun raha hoon."

He half-rises.

Parmeet's hand lands on his shoulder.

"Shaadi beech mein chhod kar kahaan chal diye? Phere poore kar lo, phir nikalna."

He sits.

The bride leans toward him.

"Vikram," she says, low.

"Washroom jaana hai. Abhi."

"Jao,"

he says.

"Pandit ji — paanch minute ke liye mantra rok dijiye. Ahana aa jaayegi, phir shuru karte hain."

The pandit folds his hands.

"Kaisi baat kar rahe hain, jajmaan? Shaadi ke phere beech mein nahin roke ja sakte. Apshagun hota hai."

"Inke yahaan kuch bhi karo, sab mein shagun-apshagun hota hi hai," somebody mutters.

"Arre pandit ji, kya baat kar rahe hain aap?" Rishabh says.

"Jaane dijiye bachchi ko. Jao. Hatiye. Please."

She goes.

The crowd shifts and murmurs, and the sun moves one bar's width across the marigold.

Then the lights go out — every string, every bulb, the mike's red eye — and the courtyard drops into brown dark, the score striking under the blackout (m13:06).

"Ye light ko kya hua?" somebody calls.

Vihaan rises half out of his seat in the dark — and hands close on his arms from both sides.

His father's voice at his ear: "Chup. Aawaaz nahin." He says it into the dark: "Pitaji."

The sehra comes down over his face before he understands; the mandap seat takes his weight; somebody else's sherwani shoulders brush past him going the other way, half-carried.

He understands everything in one breath — the tampered story read whole, the way he reads everything — and sits.

Sits, while the file in his head turns its shut pages: the bride who is not what she shows, the father spending his son as a seal.

Withheld trust.

He will have it out after.

One low string is struck somewhere above the hush and left hanging — no melody, and nobody's to thank.

The lights come back, the score sounding under the swap (m13:39).

The bride sits beside the groom with her eyes down, the same and not the same, and the crowd settles.

"Shri Ganeshay namo namah," the pandit says, folding his hands, the score sounding under the rite (m10:04).

"Ab dulha-dulhan pheron ke liye khade hon," the pandit says.

"Pehle phere mein var aage rahengi, vadhu peeche,"

he says.

"Mangalam kundaliksham, mangal tano hari."

The score sounds under the rounds (m14:07).

Round by round they go, and the mantras rise, and the marigold nods in the heat.

At the fourth round the back of his neck answers her nearness before his eyes do, and there is no reason in it.

Three hundred people watched this hand go out on a dance floor; now it holds at a fire, and the house is watching again.

"Sarv mangal maangalye," the pandit sings over the fire.

"Shive sarvaarth saadhike. Sharanye. Gauri Narayani namostute," the score sounding twice over the mantra (m14:27 x2 as one).

"Isi ke saath ye vivaah sampann ho gayaa," the pandit says at last.

"Aaj se tum dono ek dooje ke hue."

The pandit holds out the sindoor dibbi.

His hand does not shake — it is the steadiest thing in the courtyard — and the red line goes into her maang in one unbroken stroke.

The mangalsutra follows, black beads and gold, knotted where the whole crowd can see the knot.

She does not move.

Nobody breathes.

The house has its seal — one low string under the sealing. Something in his chest sits down with the knot, unasked.

Saat phere, agni ke saamne — the written thing, read aloud.

The mike stands dead through it all, its red eye dark, and nobody troubles to wake it.

Seven rounds of rice scatter where the couple's feet turned, white on the red cloth.

"Vikram," the pandit says.

"Tum bhi apna sehra hata sakte ho."

The score sounds under the invitation (m15:17).

## The Sehra Lifts

Every face in three rows has turned to the groom's hands, and the mandap on the swept ground holds its breath under the afternoon sun.

The groom lifts the sehra, the score striking under the lifting (m15:56).

"Vikram — oh god."

Pammi's voice goes up like a kite.

"Are, ye toh Vikram ke mandap mein aayi thi! Vihaan ke saath phere kab le liye?"

"Vikram kahaan hai?" Her voice comes small under the eruption, asking it first, the score sounding under her ask (m16:56); the hall will hear it twice more.

In the family's row, one chair stands empty — Aryaman's.

The pheras are done, the call's summons kept; the eruption covers his going, and he goes like a man with somewhere to be.

Vihaan stands in the groom's sherwani with the sehra in his hand, his face giving the hall nothing.

"Kya hua?" he asks the hall.

"Jhatka kuch zyaada zor ka laga?"

The hall erupts.

And under the eruption, an aunt two rows back whispers to her neighbour, loud in the way of whispers meant to carry: "Us din dance floor par jodi bani thi — aaj dekho, mandap mein bhi wahi jodi."

Vihaan hears it.

The pair stays public — three hundred witnesses at the dance, his hand held out, Parmeet's reading of her character with the whole floor watching — and now the floor's jodi sits married at the fire, to him.

He files the whisper with the day: the house has begun reading the wedding against the dance, and there is no unread-ing it.

Under his ribs the day's turn answers again, unnamed.

He looks for Vikram and does not find him.

His own shoulders still wear the stolen sherwani; beside him the bride's shock sits on her face like jewellery put on for the occasion.

*Pinky.* Upstairs, alone, with the hall about to tear itself open below her.

Parmeet's hand finds his shoulder and stays there, heavy as a seal, while every eye sits on the mandap — held, witnessed, married.

On the mandap steps a brass plate of rice has gone over, white spilled across the red cloth where no foot will clean it tonight.

Above the emptying chairs the hall clock carries the evening past five, ticking on though nobody is listening.
